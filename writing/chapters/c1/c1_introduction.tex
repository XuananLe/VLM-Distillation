\chapter{Introduction}
\label{chap:introduction}

This chapter introduces the problem, explains the need for compact
vision-language model distillation and states the main research objectives. It
first describes why deployment constraints make sub-billion students important,
then positions the work against prior methods. It ends by defining the
technical gap that leads to the proposed approach.

\section{Background and Motivation}
Vision-Language Models (VLMs) jointly process images and text for tasks such
as image captioning, visual question answering, document understanding and
instruction following
\cite{radford2021learningtransferablevisualmodels,alayrac2022flamingovisuallanguagemodel,li2023blip2bootstrappinglanguageimagepretraining,liu2023visualinstructiontuning,hu2024visualprogramdistillation}.
The core task is to transfer strong multimodal capabilities from
large VLM teachers to a compact VLM student while preserving performance on
multimodal understanding tasks.

Here, \textit{multimodal understanding} refers to practical capabilities such as reading text in images, understanding the input question and performing basic reasoning over visual and textual evidence, not broad general intelligence \cite{singh2019vqamodelsread,mathew2021docvqadatasetvqadocument,masry2022chartqabenchmarkquestionanswering}. The focus is on efficiently transferring these capabilities to compact students.

Many modern VLM families still build on the basic vision-text pretraining
pattern shown in Figure~\ref{fig:intro-vlm-pretraining}.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.82\linewidth]{figures/Contrastive_Language-Image_Pretraining.png}
    \caption{CLIP-style vision-language pretraining with a shared image-text embedding space \cite{wikimedia_clip_pretraining_image,radford2021learningtransferablevisualmodels}.}
    \label{fig:intro-vlm-pretraining}
\end{figure}

Scaling-law results explain why strong teachers tend to be large. Kaplan et
al. show that model quality improves predictably with scale, while Hoffmann et
al. show that compute-optimal training still requires jointly increasing model
size and data tokens
\cite{kaplan2020scalinglaws,hoffmann2022trainingcomputeoptimal}. Larger models
therefore remain attractive for accuracy, but they remain costly to train and
deploy.

\begin{figure}[tp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.84\linewidth]{figures/kaplan_scaling_law_chart.png}
    \caption[Scaling-law trends from Kaplan et al.]{Scaling-law trends from Kaplan et al. \cite{kaplan2020scalinglaws}.}
    \label{fig:intro-scaling-laws}
\end{figure}

High-performing VLMs often incur high memory use, latency and deployment cost
\cite{alayrac2022flamingovisuallanguagemodel,li2023blip2bootstrappinglanguageimagepretraining,chu2023mobilevlm,zhou2024tinyllava}.
This constraint is sharper in VLMs because visual tokens increase the effective
sequence length and Transformer attention scales quadratically with sequence
length \cite{vaswani2023attentionneed,liu2023visualinstructiontuning,marafioti2025smolvlm}.
Therefore, this work focuses on \textbf{\textit{sub-billion-parameter VLMs}},
a stricter deployment regime than the 1B to 3B compact models commonly used as
lightweight alternatives.

Deployment pressure is illustrated by the SmolVLM memory comparison in
Figure~\ref{fig:intro-smolvlm-memory} and by the compact-model scale comparison
in Figure~\ref{fig:intro-compact-vlm-scale}.

The issue is especially important for \textit{text-rich multimodal tasks} where
the model must read image text, understand the question and perform basic
reasoning. The three benchmarks used in this thesis are TextVQA, DocVQA and
ChartQA
\cite{singh2019vqamodelsread,mathew2021docvqadatasetvqadocument,masry2022chartqabenchmarkquestionanswering}.

Lightweight multimodal models can already be competitive
(Figure~\ref{fig:intro-small-vlm-tradeoff}), but closing the gap to
higher-capacity systems still requires more effective knowledge transfer.

Figures~\ref{fig:intro-scaling-laws} through~\ref{fig:intro-closed-open-gap}
summarize the central tension: larger models benefit from scale, competitive
teachers are accessible, but practical deployment often requires much smaller
students. This points to knowledge
distillation (KD) as a natural transfer mechanism for improving compact VLMs
with supervision from high-performing teachers
\cite{hinton2015distillingknowledgeneuralnetwork,xu2024surveyknowledgedistillationlarge}.

Because competitive open-source VLMs are accessible as teachers, \textit{white-box KD}
becomes especially attractive. In \textit{black-box KD}, the student can only
query an external teacher service and read its returned predictions. In
\textit{white-box KD}, the student can also use teacher logits and intermediate
states \cite{wang2021blackboxkd,ma2024blackboxkd}. This additional access
enables the trie-Wasserstein KD loss used here, as well as baseline methods such as ULD and hidden-state matching, which are not
usable in a pure black-box setting
\cite{boizard2024towardscrosstokenizerdistillation,takezawa2021supervisedtreewasserstein,dasgupta2025improving,xu2024surveyknowledgedistillationlarge}.

Another major compression approach is \textit{quantization}. Unlike distillation,
\textit{quantization} compresses an already trained model by reducing numerical
precision. It can cut memory and improve inference speed substantially, so it
is a serious practical alternative to KD
\cite{frantar2022gptq,xiao2023smoothquant,lin2024awq}.

In VLMs, however, naive low-bit quantization can hurt multimodal accuracy
\cite{lin2024awq,li2025mbq}. For this reason, we treat quantization as a
complementary deployment step, not a substitute for better transfer during
training.

Heterogeneous multi-teacher distillation is harder than the base case. The
candidate teachers can come from distinct VLM families with mismatched
tokenizers, output vocabularies and sample-level strengths
\cite{boizard2024towardscrosstokenizerdistillation,yuan2020reinforcedmultiteacherselection,zhang2023adaptivemultiteachermetalearning}.
A naive uniform average can therefore mix teachers that help one sample but
hurt another
\cite{xu2024surveyknowledgedistillationlarge,yuan2020reinforcedmultiteacherselection,zhang2023adaptivemultiteachermetalearning}.
This supports a \textit{selective multi-teacher strategy} rather than
\textit{static teacher averaging}.

We introduce Gradient-Routed Alignment with Capacity-aware Experts (GRACE), a
selective multi-teacher distillation method for compact VLMs.

\section{Related Work}

The most relevant literature can be grouped by transfer mechanism:
matched-vocabulary logits distillation, cross-tokenizer distillation,
representation matching and multi-teacher selection.

\subsection{Response-Based Knowledge Distillation}

Classical KD matches softened teacher and student output distributions,
usually under a shared tokenizer or matched output space
\cite{hinton2015distillingknowledgeneuralnetwork,xu2024surveyknowledgedistillationlarge}.
When teacher and student share the same tokenizer, the common choice is a
Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence between softened logits
\cite{hinton2015distillingknowledgeneuralnetwork}. In multimodal systems,
prior work has also transferred reasoning behavior or representation knowledge
into compact vision-language models
\cite{fang2021compressingvlm,hu2024visualprogramdistillation,yang2024clipcid}.
This line shows that lightweight VLMs benefit from teacher supervision, but it
usually assumes one teacher or a matched output space. It therefore does not
directly address heterogeneous teachers with mismatched tokenizers.

Quantization is a separate compression line that reduces numerical precision
after or during training
\cite{frantar2022gptq,xiao2023smoothquant,lin2024awq,li2025mbq}. It helps
deployment, but it does not solve the transfer problem studied here.

\subsection{Cross-Tokenizer and Transport-Based Distillation}

Cross-tokenizer KD becomes necessary when teacher and student vocabularies do
not match. ULD is a direct baseline in this setting because it compares sorted
probability mass instead of token IDs
\cite{boizard2024towardscrosstokenizerdistillation}. More recent work also
studies tokenizer-agnostic distillation through transport-based formulations
\cite{takezawa2021supervisedtreewasserstein,leyamadafukumizucuturi2019treesliced,dasgupta2025improving}.

The weakness of ranked-mass matching is that it discards relations between
decoded strings. Two pairs with the same probability shape can therefore
receive the same loss even when one pair shares a long prefix and the other
does not. Tree-structured transport keeps that information by comparing mass on
shared branches of a hierarchy
\cite{takezawa2021supervisedtreewasserstein,peyre2019computationaloptimaltransport}.
This leaves clear room for cross-tokenizer objectives that match teacher and
student by mass on shared substructures, not by token ID.

\subsection{Layer Distillation}

Another line of work transfers intermediate representations instead of only
final logits. Similarity measures such as CKA provide a tokenizer-independent
way to compare hidden states across layers
\cite{kornblith2019similarityrepresentations}. This makes representation
matching attractive when teacher and student architectures differ. However,
hidden-state alignment alone does not determine which teacher should receive
the largest weight for a given sample and does not resolve conflicts among
several teachers.

\subsection{Multi-Teacher Distillation}

Multi-teacher KD has been studied with fixed averages, stochastic teacher
selection and meta-learned weighting rules
\cite{yuan2020reinforcedmultiteacherselection,zhang2023adaptivemultiteachermetalearning,yu2024selectdistill}.
These methods motivate the use of more than one teacher, but fixed or
meta-learned weights can still mix useful and harmful teachers when teacher
utility changes across samples.

Sparse routing and load balancing are well established in mixture-of-experts
models
\cite{shazeer2017outrageouslylargeneuralnetworks,fedus2022switchtransformersscalingtrillion,zhou2022mixtureofexpertswithexpertchoice}.
Gradient-based filtering also appears in distillation through
alignment-style objectives
\cite{zhu2021studentcustomized}. These ideas support the routing and
score-refinement mechanisms used later in this work.

\subsection{Research Gap}

Taken together, prior work leaves three gaps:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textit{Tokenizer mismatch} across heterogeneous teachers and students.
    \item \textit{Sample-dependent teacher utility}.
    \item \textit{Post-routing instability}.
\end{itemize}

These gaps define the technical problem studied here.
In particular, even after a router selects candidate teachers, their KD
gradients may still conflict with the supervised objective, making
post-routing weighting unstable.

\section{Problem Statement and Research Objectives}
Given a pool of frozen teacher VLMs and a compact student, the goal is to learn
a heterogeneous multi-teacher multimodal distillation strategy that preserves
task performance while reducing deployment cost. The strategy must satisfy four
requirements:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Remain valid when teacher and student architectures, tokenizers and output vocabularies differ.
    \item Assign teachers adaptively for each sample.
    \item Keep routing sparse through per-sample top-\(k\) teacher selection.
    \item Suppress routed teachers whose KD direction conflicts with the supervised objective.
\end{itemize}

\methodfull{} (\methodname{}) addresses these requirements as a selective
multi-teacher distillation method for compact VLMs.

\section{Research Questions}
The thesis is organized around three research questions.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{RQ1: Performance Comparison.} How does \methodname{} compare with same-family logits KD, cross-tokenizer ULD and other multi-teacher baselines on TextVQA, DocVQA and ChartQA?
\item \textbf{RQ2: Component Analysis.} What is the contribution of trie-Wasserstein KD, sparse routing and GRACE score refinement to the final performance of \methodname{}?
\item \textbf{RQ3: Efficiency Analysis.} What are the execution-time and computational costs of \methodname{}?
\end{enumerate}

\section{Thesis Contributions}
The main contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item We formulate \textit{heterogeneous multi-teacher distillation} for compact VLMs with mismatched teacher and student architectures, tokenizers and output vocabularies.
    \item We propose \methodname{}, which combines \textit{trie-Wasserstein cross-tokenizer KD}, \textit{student-conditioned routing}, \textit{sparse top-\(k\) selection} and \textit{gradient-based score refinement}.
    \item We evaluate \methodname{} on TextVQA, DocVQA and ChartQA against fine-tuning and distillation baselines.
    \item We analyze routing behavior, component ablations, representation similarity and efficiency.
\end{itemize}

\section{Thesis Organization}
The remainder of the thesis is organized as follows. Chapter~\ref{chap:preliminaries}
summarizes the technical preliminaries, including Transformer-based VLMs,
token-level knowledge distillation, cross-tokenizer distribution matching,
Wasserstein and tree-structured transport, sparse routing and gradient conflict.
Chapter~\ref{chap:proposed-method}
presents the proposed method, including the trie-Wasserstein KD loss, the
student-conditioned router and the GRACE refinement mechanism. Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation-methodology}
describes the datasets, baselines, training configuration and evaluation
protocol. Chapter~\ref{chap:experiment-results} addresses the three research
questions through the main empirical results, ablations and efficiency
analysis. The final chapter concludes the thesis and outlines the main
limitations and future directions.
