\chapter{Introduction}
\label{chap:introduction}

This chapter introduces the problem, motivates the need for compact
vision-language model distillation and states the main research objectives. It
first explains why deployment constraints make sub-billion students important,
then positions the work against prior methods. It ends by defining the
technical gap that motivates the proposed approach.

\section{Background and Motivation}
The core task here is to distill strong multimodal ability from large
VLM teachers into \textbf{sub-billion} VLM students. The goal is to do so
without losing performance on text-centric benchmarks. Vision-Language Models
(VLMs) jointly process images and text for tasks such as image captioning,
visual question answering, document understanding and instruction following
\cite{radford2021learningtransferablevisualmodels,alayrac2022flamingovisuallanguagemodel,li2023blip2bootstrappinglanguageimagepretraining,liu2023visualinstructiontuning,hu2024visualprogramdistillation}.

\textit{Multimodal understanding} here refers to practical capabilities such as reading text in images, understanding the input question and performing basic reasoning over visual and textual evidence, not broad general intelligence \cite{singh2019vqamodelsread,mathew2021docvqadatasetvqadocument,masry2022chartqabenchmarkquestionanswering}. Our focus is efficient transfer of this ability to compact students.

    \caption{CLIP-style vision-language pretraining with a shared image-text embedding space \cite{wikimedia_clip_pretraining_image,radford2021learningtransferablevisualmodels}.}
    \label{fig:intro-vlm-pretraining}
\end{figure}

Many modern VLM families still build on the basic vision-text pretraining
pattern shown in Figure~\ref{fig:intro-vlm-pretraining}.

However, high-performing VLMs are often large and expensive in memory, latency and deployment cost \cite{alayrac2022flamingovisuallanguagemodel,li2023blip2bootstrappinglanguageimagepretraining,chu2023mobilevlm,zhou2024tinyllava}. This cost limits their use in resource-constrained settings.

This pressure is consistent with scaling-law results. Kaplan et al. show that
model quality improves predictably with scale, while Hoffmann et al. show that
compute-optimal training still requires jointly increasing model size and data
tokens \cite{kaplan2020scalinglaws,hoffmann2022trainingcomputeoptimal}. Larger
models therefore remain attractive for accuracy, but they do not become cheap
to train or deploy.

\begin{figure}[tp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.84\linewidth]{figures/kaplan_scaling_law_chart.png}
    \caption[Scaling-law trends from Kaplan et al.]{Scaling-law trends from Kaplan et al. \cite{kaplan2020scalinglaws}.}
    \label{fig:intro-scaling-laws}
\end{figure}

The vision side makes this constraint sharper in VLMs. VLMs insert visual embeddings or
visual tokens into the multimodal context together with text, so
the effective sequence length grows with image tokenization density. Since
Transformer attention scales quadratically with sequence length, increasing the
visual token budget quickly raises memory and latency \cite{vaswani2023attentionneed,liu2023visualinstructiontuning,marafioti2025smolvlm}. For compact VLMs, this means that shrinking only the language backbone is not enough. The visual stack and the number of visual tokens also need to be controlled.

The most important regime here is \textbf{small models}, especially
\textbf{sub-billion VLMs}. Models in the 1B to 3B range are already cheaper
than large 7B to 70B systems, but they can still be heavy for edge deployment,
memory-limited serving, or repeated evaluation across many checkpoints.
Sub-billion students are a stricter target. They force the model to use its
parameter budget and visual tokens more carefully, while still aiming to retain
useful multimodal performance \cite{chu2023mobilevlm,zhou2024tinyllava,marafioti2025smolvlm}.

The issue is especially important for text-centric tasks such as scene-text reading, document understanding and chart reasoning \cite{singh2019vqamodelsread,mathew2021docvqadatasetvqadocument,masry2022chartqabenchmarkquestionanswering}.

teachers benefit from scale, but the deployment target is still a much smaller

Knowledge distillation (KD) transfers knowledge from a large teacher to a
compact student, often preserving strong performance at lower cost
\cite{hinton2015distillingknowledgeneuralnetwork,xu2024surveyknowledgedistillationlarge}.

Compact multimodal models can already be competitive
(Figure~\ref{fig:intro-small-vlm-tradeoff}), but closing the gap to stronger
models still requires better transfer of knowledge.

On these three benchmarks, the best open-source models are already close to the
best proprietary models and in two cases they are ahead
(Figure~\ref{fig:intro-closed-open-gap}).

This makes white-box distillation especially attractive. In black-box KD, the
student can only query an external teacher service and read its returned
predictions. In white-box KD, the student can also use teacher logits and
intermediate states \cite{wang2021blackboxkd,ma2024blackboxkd}. That extra
access enables objectives such as the trie-Wasserstein KD loss used here, as well as prior baselines such as ULD and hidden-state matching, which are not
available in a pure black-box setting
\cite{boizard2024towardscrosstokenizerdistillation,takezawa2021supervisedtreewasserstein,dasgupta2025improving,xu2024surveyknowledgedistillationlarge}.

Another major competing compression path is quantization. Unlike distillation,
quantization compresses an already trained model by reducing numerical
precision. It can cut memory and improve inference speed substantially, so it
is a serious practical alternative to KD
\cite{frantar2022gptq,xiao2023smoothquant,lin2024awq}.

In VLMs, however, naive low-bit quantization can hurt multimodal accuracy
\cite{lin2024awq,li2025mbq}. For this reason, we treat quantization as a
complementary deployment step, not a substitute for better transfer during
training.

\begin{figure}[tp]
    \centering

    \caption[Low-bit quantization can sharply reduce VLM accuracy.]{SEED accuracy of LLaVA-OneVision-7B under several low-bit quantization settings, reconstructed from MBQ Table 4 \cite{li2025mbq}.}
    \label{fig:intro-quantization-drop}
\end{figure}

This observation is exactly why we focus on \textbf{sub-billion vision-language
models}. They are small enough to make the efficiency constraint real, yet
strong enough that improvements from better distillation remain meaningful
\cite{marafioti2025smolvlm}.

In the multimodal setting, recent work has shown that distillation can transfer
reasoning or representation knowledge into more efficient VLMs
\cite{fang2021compressingvlm,hu2024visualprogramdistillation,yang2024clipcid}.
This is especially relevant on text-rich benchmarks such as TextVQA, DocVQA,
and ChartQA
\cite{singh2019vqamodelsread,mathew2021docvqadatasetvqadocument,masry2022chartqabenchmarkquestionanswering}.

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.70\linewidth]{figures/knowledge_distillation_example.png}
    \caption[Teacher-student knowledge distillation.]{Generic teacher-student knowledge distillation \cite{wikimedia_knowledge_distillation_example,hinton2015distillingknowledgeneuralnetwork}.}
    \label{fig:intro-knowledge-distillation}
\end{figure}

The pattern in Figure~\ref{fig:intro-knowledge-distillation} is the base case
extended here to the heterogeneous multi-teacher setting.

Since no single teacher excels across all multimodal tasks, \textit{multi-teacher distillation} can provide richer supervision than a single-teacher pipeline \cite{yuan2020reinforcedmultiteacherselection,zhang2023adaptivemultiteachermetalearning,yu2024selectdistill}.

In our setting, this is not just a matter of picking several strong teachers. The available teachers come from heterogeneous VLMs with different tokenizers, different output vocabularies and different sample-level strengths \cite{boizard2024towardscrosstokenizerdistillation,yuan2020reinforcedmultiteacherselection,zhang2023adaptivemultiteachermetalearning}.

A naive uniform average can therefore mix teachers that help one sample but hurt another \cite{xu2024surveyknowledgedistillationlarge,yuan2020reinforcedmultiteacherselection,zhang2023adaptivemultiteachermetalearning}.

This motivates a selective multi-teacher framework over static teacher averaging.

We introduce \methodfull{} (\methodname{}), a selective multi-teacher distillation framework that routes teachers per sample, applies a trie-Wasserstein cross-tokenizer loss and refines teacher weights with gradient-based alignment. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first distillation framework to employ a shared tree-metric OT for heterogeneous multi-teacher VLM distillation. The framework tackles tokenizer mismatch, heterogeneous teacher specialization and post-routing instability in one recipe.

\section{Related Work}

The literature that matters most here is best grouped by transfer mechanism:
matched-vocabulary logits distillation, cross-tokenizer distillation,
representation matching and multi-teacher selection.

\subsection{Compact VLM Distillation with Matched Output Spaces}

Knowledge distillation is a standard way to compress large models into smaller
students while retaining part of the teacher's predictive structure
\cite{hinton2015distillingknowledgeneuralnetwork,xu2024surveyknowledgedistillationlarge}.
When teacher and student share the same tokenizer, the common choice is to
match softened output distributions with a Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence
\cite{hinton2015distillingknowledgeneuralnetwork}. In multimodal systems,
prior work has distilled large VLMs into smaller students, transferred
reasoning behavior or improved compact vision-language models
\cite{fang2021compressingvlm,hu2024visualprogramdistillation,yang2024clipcid}.
This line shows that compact VLMs benefit from teacher supervision, but it
usually assumes one teacher or a matched output space. It therefore does not
directly address heterogeneous teachers with different tokenizers.

Quantization is a separate compression line that reduces numerical precision
after or during training
\cite{frantar2022gptq,xiao2023smoothquant,lin2024awq,li2025mbq}. It helps
deployment, but it does not solve the transfer problem studied here.

\subsection{Cross-Tokenizer and Transport-Based Distillation}

Cross-tokenizer KD becomes necessary when teacher and student vocabularies do
not match. ULD is a direct baseline in this setting because it compares sorted
probability mass instead of token IDs
\cite{boizard2024towardscrosstokenizerdistillation}. More recent work also
studies tokenizer-agnostic distillation through transport-based formulations
\cite{takezawa2021supervisedtreewasserstein,leyamadafukumizucuturi2019treesliced,dasgupta2025improving}.

The weakness of ranked-mass matching is that it discards relations between
decoded strings. Two pairs with the same probability shape can therefore
receive the same loss even when one pair shares a long prefix and the other
does not. Tree-structured transport keeps that information by comparing mass on
shared branches of a hierarchy
\cite{takezawa2021supervisedtreewasserstein,peyre2019computationaloptimaltransport}.
This leaves clear room for cross-tokenizer objectives that match teacher and
student by mass on shared substructures, not by token ID.

\subsection{Representation Matching Beyond Logits}

Another line of work transfers intermediate representations instead of only
final logits. Similarity measures such as CKA provide a tokenizer-independent
way to compare hidden states across layers
\cite{kornblith2019similarityrepresentations}. This makes representation
matching attractive when teacher and student architectures differ. However,
hidden-state alignment alone does not decide which teacher should dominate a
sample and does not resolve conflicts among several teachers. It is therefore a
useful baseline, but still incomplete for heterogeneous multi-teacher
distillation.

\subsection{Multi-Teacher Distillation and Adaptive Teacher Selection}

Multi-teacher KD has been studied with fixed averages, stochastic teacher
selection and meta-learned weighting rules
\cite{yuan2020reinforcedmultiteacherselection,zhang2023adaptivemultiteachermetalearning,yu2024selectdistill}.
These methods motivate the use of more than one teacher, but fixed or
meta-learned weights can still mix useful and harmful teachers when teacher
utility changes across samples.

Sparse routing and load balancing are well established in mixture-of-experts
models
\cite{shazeer2017outrageouslylargeneuralnetworks,fedus2022switchtransformersscalingtrillion,zhou2022mixtureofexpertswithexpertchoice}.
Gradient-based filtering also appears in distillation through
alignment-style objectives
\cite{zhu2021studentcustomized}. These ideas are relevant, but prior work does
not jointly handle heterogeneous VLM teachers, tokenizer mismatch and
post-routing instability in one compact-VLM setting. The remaining gap is a
method that can select teachers per sample while staying stable under
heterogeneous supervision.

\subsection{Research Gap}

Taken together, the literature leaves three gaps open: tokenizer mismatch
across heterogeneous teachers and students, sample-dependent teacher utility
and instability after adaptive routing. Those gaps define the technical problem
studied in the following chapters.

\section{Problem Statement and Research Objectives}
The central problem is how to transfer multimodal
understanding from large VLMs to compact students. The transfer must preserve
task performance while reducing deployment cost at inference time.

We focus on text-rich multimodal tasks where the student must read image text, understand the question and perform basic reasoning \cite{singh2019vqamodelsread,mathew2021docvqadatasetvqadocument,masry2022chartqabenchmarkquestionanswering}.

In this setting, existing distillation pipelines often assume a single teacher, a shared tokenizer, or a fixed averaging rule across teachers. These assumptions are restrictive for heterogeneous multi-teacher VLM distillation \cite{hinton2015distillingknowledgeneuralnetwork,boizard2024towardscrosstokenizerdistillation,yuan2020reinforcedmultiteacherselection,zhang2023adaptivemultiteachermetalearning}.

More specifically, the method should satisfy three requirements.

\begin{enumerate}
\item The KD loss should remain valid even when teacher and student tokenizers differ.
\item Teacher assignment should be \emph{sample-dependent}, not fixed across the whole dataset.
\item The routing mechanism should remain sparse while suppressing routed teachers whose KD direction conflicts with the supervised objective. When needed, the framework should also support regularizers that discourage routing collapse.
\end{enumerate}

Given a pool of \(N\) frozen teacher VLMs and a compact student, the goal is therefore to learn a distillation strategy that:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Remains valid across mismatched teacher and student vocabularies.
\item Routes teachers adaptively from student-side features through a learned student-conditioned gate.
\item Retains sparse top-\(k\) computation under capacity constraints.
\item Filters routed teachers when their KD update direction conflicts with the supervised objective, while keeping a fallback teacher when necessary.
\end{enumerate}

\methodfull{} (\methodname) addresses these requirements.

\methodname{} is a selective multi-teacher distillation framework.

It combines a trie-Wasserstein cross-tokenizer KD loss, a student-conditioned teacher gate, top-\(k\) capacity-aware sparse routing and gradient-based GRACE score refinement after routing. The framework also supports routing fallback after capacity pruning, uniform fallback when score spread is too small and balancing and expert-bias updates \cite{boizard2024towardscrosstokenizerdistillation,peyre2019computationaloptimaltransport,fedus2022switchtransformersscalingtrillion,zhou2022mixtureofexpertswithexpertchoice,zhu2021studentcustomized}.

We evaluate it on text-rich multimodal understanding tasks such as TextVQA, DocVQA and ChartQA \cite{singh2019vqamodelsread,mathew2021docvqadatasetvqadocument,masry2022chartqabenchmarkquestionanswering}.

Accordingly, we focus on cross-tokenizer multimodal distillation for compact students and evaluate \methodname{} on TextVQA, DocVQA and ChartQA, with analysis of routing behavior and representation matching.

\section{Research Questions}
The thesis is organized around three research questions.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{RQ1: Performance Comparison.} How does \methodname{} compare with same-family logits KD, cross-tokenizer ULD and other multi-teacher baselines on TextVQA, DocVQA and ChartQA?
\item \textbf{RQ2: Component Analysis.} What is the contribution of trie-Wasserstein KD, sparse routing and GRACE score refinement to the final performance of \methodname{}?
\item \textbf{RQ3: Efficiency Analysis.} How efficient is \methodname{} in terms of execution time and computational cost?
\end{enumerate}

\section{Thesis Contributions}
The main contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item We define a heterogeneous multi-teacher distillation setting for compact VLMs with mismatched teacher and student vocabularies on TextVQA, DocVQA and ChartQA.
\item We present \methodfull{} (\methodname{}), a selective multi-teacher distillation framework that combines trie-Wasserstein cross-tokenizer KD, a student-conditioned gate, sparse top-\(k\) routing and GRACE score refinement.
\item We show that this framework directly addresses the main practical failures of heterogeneous VLM distillation: tokenizer mismatch, sample-dependent teacher conflict and unstable teacher weighting after routing.
\item We establish consistent gains over fine-tuning and strong distillation baselines, supported by routing, ablation and representation analyses.
\end{enumerate}
