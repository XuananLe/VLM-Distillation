\chapter{Introduction}
\label{chap:introduction}

\textbf{Vision-language models (VLMs)} have emerged as a \textbf{central direction} in modern AI
because they integrate visual perception with language reasoning. By coupling
powerful image encoders with autoregressive language decoders through learned
multimodal connectors, these models can interpret visual content, read text
embedded in images and reason across modalities in ways that neither vision
systems nor language models alone can easily achieve
\cite{alayrac2022flamingovisuallanguagemodel,li2023blip2bootstrappinglanguageimagepretraining,liu2023visualinstructiontuning}.
This architectural synthesis has unlocked a \textbf{broad set of capabilities},
including extracting information from scanned documents, understanding visually
rich scenes and grounding language instructions in physical environments
\cite{singh2019vqamodelsread,mathew2021docvqadatasetvqadocument,masry2022chartqabenchmarkquestionanswering,driess2023palme,brohan2023rt2}.

Driven by neural scaling laws, the most capable VLMs are usually built on large
language backbones trained with large multimodal corpora. This recipe reliably
improves performance but also raises computational cost
\cite{kaplan2020scalinglaws,hoffmann2022trainingcomputeoptimal}. As model size
grows, the gap between what is achievable in research and what is deployable
in practice widens accordingly. The \textbf{highest-scoring models are often the
hardest to deploy} when latency, memory footprint, energy consumption and
hardware constraints matter. In robotics and embodied AI, an agent must process
visual observations, interpret language instructions and produce action
decisions in real time, often on resource-constrained hardware
\cite{driess2023palme,brohan2023rt2}. Similar constraints appear in mobile
applications, document-processing pipelines and enterprise deployments, where
users and operators need models with low latency, controlled cost and clear security boundaries. This
widening \textbf{gap between frontier capability and deployment constraints} creates a
central tension for applied VLM research.

\textbf{Knowledge distillation} offers a principled path through this tension by
compressing the behavior of a large teacher model into a compact student. The
student avoids training from scratch on the same large-scale corpora and learns
from the teacher's output distributions and internal representations,
which carry richer information than hard labels alone
\cite{hinton2015distillingknowledgeneuralnetwork,xu2024surveyknowledgedistillationlarge}.
Distillation has been widely used to produce smaller models that \textbf{retain part
of the capability of their larger counterparts} and recent lightweight VLMs
demonstrate that this direction is viable for latency-sensitive and
cost-sensitive deployment
\cite{chu2023mobilevlm,zhou2024tinyllava,marafioti2025smolvlm}.

However, distillation in the VLM setting cannot be treated as a straightforward
compression problem. Existing approaches each address part of the challenge,
but \textbf{none covers it fully}. Response-based distillation aligns teacher and
student output distributions effectively when both share a tokenizer, but
\textbf{breaks down} when teacher and student use \textbf{different vocabularies}
\cite{hinton2015distillingknowledgeneuralnetwork,xu2024surveyknowledgedistillationlarge}.
Transport-based methods relax this assumption by comparing probability mass
over decoded strings, but they are usually formulated for single-teacher
transfer and do not determine which teacher should supervise a given sample
\cite{boizard2024towardscrosstokenizerdistillation,takezawa2021supervisedtreewasserstein,leyamadafukumizucuturi2019treesliced}.
Representation matching through hidden states or similarity measures such as
CKA provides a tokenizer-independent signal, but does not directly optimize
answer likelihood and \textbf{cannot resolve conflicts between teachers}
\cite{kornblith2019similarityrepresentations}. Multi-teacher distillation
methods show the value of pooling several teachers, but fixed or globally
learned weights can mix useful supervision with low-utility or conflicting signals
\cite{yuan2020reinforcedmultiteacherselection,zhang2023adaptivemultiteachermetalearning,yu2024selectdistill}.

These limitations are most severe in text-rich visual reasoning tasks such as
document question answering, chart interpretation and scene text understanding.
Although recent open-source VLMs have narrowed the gap with proprietary systems
on common benchmarks \cite{wang2024qwen2vl,chen2024internvl15,chen2024internvl25},
smaller models continue to struggle when fine visual details, document
structure and numerical relations must be captured. The central motivation of
this thesis is to combine \textbf{tokenizer-agnostic} distribution matching,
\textbf{sample-dependent} teacher selection and \textbf{gradient-aware conflict
resolution} for heterogeneous VLM distillation.

This thesis evaluates \textbf{\gracefull{} (\gracename{})} using VLM-Eval-Kit
on TextVQA, DocVQA and ChartQA with SmolVLM-500M and SmolVLM-256M as student
models. In the four-teacher setting, \methodname{}
moves SmolVLM-500M closer to the highest-scoring teacher by closing \(55.03\%\),
\(39.53\%\) and \(55.34\%\) of original teacher-student gaps of
22.95, 34.38 and 31.44 points on TextVQA, DocVQA and ChartQA. This reduction
supports deployment of smaller student VLMs in text-rich VQA
scenarios.
The study is guided by three research questions:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{RQ1: Performance.} How does \methodname{} compare
with direct fine-tuning, same-family logits KD, ULD and
heterogeneous multi-teacher baselines on TextVQA, DocVQA and ChartQA
benchmarks?
\item \textbf{RQ2: Component Analysis.} How much do trie-Wasserstein KD,
sparse top-\(k\) routing and gradient-agreement refinement contribute to
the final performance?
\item \textbf{RQ3: Efficiency.} What execution-time and computational overhead
does \methodname{} introduce during training?
\end{itemize}

This thesis addresses this gap through three contributions to knowledge
distillation for a pool of heterogeneous vision-language models.

\noindent \textbf{First, trie-Wasserstein cross-tokenizer distillation loss.}
We formulate a tokenizer-agnostic KD objective that maps teacher and student
token vocabularies onto a shared UTF-8 byte trie and computes a \(W_1\)
optimal-transport distance through subtree-mass imbalances. Compared with
ranked-mass baselines such as ULD, the trie-Wasserstein loss preserves the
lexical structure of decoded token strings: tokens that share a long byte
prefix are cheaper to transport across than unrelated tokens. A sparse
top-\(K_{\mathrm{TW}}\) support with a residual tail edge keeps per-position
cost bounded without discarding low-probability mass.

\noindent \textbf{Second, gradient-agreement refinement of routed teacher weights.}
We introduce a post-routing correction mechanism that scores each routed
teacher by comparing its KD gradient against the student's supervised CE
gradient with cosine similarity. The scores are stabilized with an
exponential moving average, converted to weights through a score-softmax and
geometrically blended with the router's own weights. This refinement detects
and suppresses teachers whose KD direction conflicts with the supervised
objective, a failure mode that sparse routing alone cannot prevent.

\noindent \textbf{Finally, GRACE as an integrated framework for heterogeneous multi-teacher
VLM distillation.}
We combine the trie-Wasserstein loss, a sparse top-\(k\) teacher routing
network and gradient-agreement refinement into a single end-to-end training
objective. Empirical evaluation on TextVQA, DocVQA and ChartQA with
SmolVLM-500M and SmolVLM-256M students under a four-teacher pool shows that
\methodname{} improves over direct finetuning, same-family KD, cross-family
ULD and the compared heterogeneous multi-teacher baselines, closing up to
55\% of the teacher-student gap.

The remainder of this thesis is structured as follows.
Chapter~\ref{chap:preliminaries} provides background on VLMs, knowledge
distillation, tokenizer-mismatched distribution matching, sparse routing and
gradient conflict resolution. Chapter~\ref{chap:proposed-method} presents
\methodname{} in full, covering the trie-Wasserstein objective, sparse teacher
router, gradient-agreement refinement and combined training objective.
Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation-methodology} describes the datasets, baselines,
metrics and implementation details. Chapter~\ref{chap:experiment-results}
reports results across RQ1 to RQ3. The thesis concludes with the main
findings and future directions.
