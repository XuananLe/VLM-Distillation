\chapter{Introduction}
\label{chap:introduction}

\section{Background}

\textbf{Vision-language models (VLMs)} have emerged as a \textbf{central direction} in modern AI
because they integrate visual perception with language reasoning. By coupling
powerful image encoders with autoregressive language decoders through learned
multimodal connectors, these models can interpret visual content, read text
embedded in images and reason across modalities in ways that neither vision
systems nor language models alone can easily achieve
\cite{alayrac2022flamingovisuallanguagemodel,li2023blip2bootstrappinglanguageimagepretraining,liu2023visualinstructiontuning}.
This architectural synthesis has unlocked a \textbf{broad set of capabilities},
including extracting information from scanned documents, understanding visually
rich scenes and grounding language instructions in physical environments
\cite{singh2019vqamodelsread,mathew2021docvqadatasetvqadocument,masry2022chartqabenchmarkquestionanswering,driess2023palme,brohan2023rt2}.

Driven by neural scaling laws, the most capable VLMs are usually built on large
language backbones trained with large multimodal corpora. This recipe reliably
improves performance but also raises computational cost
\cite{kaplan2020scalinglaws,hoffmann2022trainingcomputeoptimal}. As model size
grows, the gap between what is achievable in research and what is deployable
in practice widens accordingly. The \textbf{models that perform best are often the
hardest to deploy} when latency, memory footprint, energy consumption and
hardware constraints matter. In robotics and embodied AI, an agent must process
visual observations, interpret language instructions and produce action
decisions in real time, often on resource-constrained hardware
\cite{driess2023palme,brohan2023rt2}. Similar constraints appear in mobile
applications, document-processing pipelines and enterprise deployments, where
users and operators need models that are \textbf{fast}, \textbf{affordable} and \textbf{secure}. This
widening \textbf{gap between frontier capability and practical deployment} creates a
central tension for applied VLM research.

\textbf{Knowledge distillation} offers a principled path through this tension by
compressing the behavior of a large teacher model into a compact student. The
student avoids training from scratch on the same massive corpora and learns
from the teacher's output distributions and internal representations,
which carry richer information than hard labels alone
\cite{hinton2015distillingknowledgeneuralnetwork,xu2024surveyknowledgedistillationlarge}.
Distillation has been widely used to produce smaller models that \textbf{preserve much
of the capability of their larger counterparts} and recent lightweight VLMs
demonstrate that this direction is practical for latency-sensitive and
cost-sensitive deployment
\cite{chu2023mobilevlm,zhou2024tinyllava,marafioti2025smolvlm}.

However, distillation in the VLM setting cannot be treated as a straightforward
compression problem. Existing approaches each address part of the challenge,
but \textbf{none covers it fully}. Response-based distillation aligns teacher and
student output distributions effectively when both share a tokenizer, but
\textbf{breaks down} when teacher and student use \textbf{different vocabularies}
\cite{hinton2015distillingknowledgeneuralnetwork,xu2024surveyknowledgedistillationlarge}.
Transport-based methods relax this assumption by comparing probability mass
over decoded strings, but they are usually formulated for single-teacher
transfer and do not determine which teacher should supervise a given sample
\cite{boizard2024towardscrosstokenizerdistillation,takezawa2021supervisedtreewasserstein,leyamadafukumizucuturi2019treesliced}.
Representation matching through hidden states or similarity measures such as
CKA provides a tokenizer-independent signal, but does not directly optimize
answer likelihood and \textbf{cannot resolve conflicts between teachers}
\cite{kornblith2019similarityrepresentations}. Multi-teacher distillation
methods show the value of pooling several teachers, but fixed or globally
learned weights can mix useful supervision with weak or conflicting signals
\cite{yuan2020reinforcedmultiteacherselection,zhang2023adaptivemultiteachermetalearning,yu2024selectdistill}.

The gap is most visible in text-rich visual reasoning tasks such as document
question answering, chart interpretation and scene text understanding, which
require optical character recognition, spatial layout understanding and
multi-step inference
\cite{singh2019vqamodelsread,mathew2021docvqadatasetvqadocument,masry2022chartqabenchmarkquestionanswering}.
Although recent open-source VLMs have narrowed the gap with proprietary systems
on common benchmarks \cite{wang2024qwen2vl,chen2024internvl15,chen2024internvl25},
smaller models continue to struggle on these harder tasks, where limited
parameter capacity makes it difficult to capture fine visual details, document
structure and numerical relations. Closing this gap requires a method that
combines \textbf{tokenizer-agnostic} distribution matching, \textbf{sample-dependent} teacher
selection and \textbf{gradient-aware conflict resolution} in a single framework. This is
the central motivation of the thesis.

\section{Problem Statement}
Given a pool of frozen teacher VLMs and a trainable student VLM, the goal is to
learn a distillation strategy that transfers task performance to the student
while reducing deployment cost. The strategy must satisfy four requirements: it
must remain valid when teachers and student differ in architecture, tokenizer
and output vocabulary, assign teacher weights per sample and not globally,
select only a sparse top-\(k\) subset of teachers per input and detect and
suppress distillation gradients that conflict with the supervised objective.

This thesis addresses all four requirements in a unified framework.
This thesis proposes \textbf{\gracefull{} (\gracename{})} as a method that
satisfies these requirements. \methodname{} combines tokenizer-agnostic output
matching, sparse sample-dependent routing and gradient-agreement refinement
into a unified distillation objective for heterogeneous VLMs. Its design is
evaluated through three research questions:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{RQ1: Performance.} How does \methodname{} compare
with direct fine-tuning, same-family logits KD, ULD and
heterogeneous multi-teacher baselines on TextVQA, DocVQA and ChartQA
benchmarks?
\item \textbf{RQ2: Component Analysis.} How much do trie-Wasserstein KD,
sparse top-\(k\) routing and gradient-agreement refinement contribute to
the final performance?
\item \textbf{RQ3: Efficiency.} What execution-time and computational overhead
does \methodname{} introduce during training?
\end{itemize}

\section{Thesis Contributions}
This thesis presents three main technical contributions to the field of knowledge distillation in vision-language models.


\textbf{A geometry-aware cross-tokenizer distillation objective.}
Trie-Wasserstein KD maps decoded teacher and student tokens into a shared UTF-8
byte trie and defines distillation loss as subtree-mass imbalances under the
induced tree metric. Unlike methods that treat all token-string mismatches as
equally costly, the objective penalizes distributional divergence in proportion
to lexical distance. A sparse top-\(K\) support with a residual tail edge keeps
computation tractable without discarding probability mass or constructing a
full vocabulary-to-vocabulary transport matrix.

\textbf{An objective-aware refinement mechanism for routed multi-teacher distillation.}
Gradient-agreement refinement is a post-routing step that measures cosine
similarity between each routed teacher's distillation gradient and the
supervised cross-entropy gradient, then adjusts teacher weights by their degree
of objective alignment. Sparse routing captures sample-level teacher relevance,
while gradient-agreement refinement captures compatibility with the supervised
objective. The two mechanisms are complementary and operate in sequence.

\textbf{Empirical evidence supporting output-level over representation-level matching in heterogeneous VLM distillation.}
Centered kernel alignment is tokenizer-agnostic and a natural baseline for
heterogeneous distillation, but controlled ablations show it does not yield
consistent improvements when used alone, even when teachers and students share
no vocabulary. The combination of Trie-Wasserstein matching, sparse routing and
gradient-agreement refinement outperforms both CKA matching and uniform teacher
averaging across DocVQA, ChartQA and related benchmarks, indicating that
tokenizer-aware output-level distillation is a more reliable basis for
heterogeneous knowledge transfer.

\section{Thesis Organization}

The remainder of this thesis is structured as follows. Chapter~\ref{chap:preliminaries}
provides the background and related work necessary to situate \methodname{},
covering VLMs, knowledge distillation, tokenizer-mismatched distribution
matching, sparse routing mechanisms and gradient conflict resolution.
Chapter~\ref{chap:proposed-method} presents \methodname{} in full. It begins
with the trie-Wasserstein KD objective, which enables tokenizer-agnostic
distribution matching, then introduces the sparse teacher router for
sample-dependent teacher selection and concludes with the gradient-agreement
refinement step and the combined training objective.
Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation-methodology} describes the experimental setup in
detail, including the text-rich VQA datasets, student and teacher model
configurations, baseline methods, evaluation metrics and implementation
details. Chapter~\ref{chap:experiment-results} reports results across the
three research questions: main benchmark comparisons against fine-tuning and
distillation baselines, component ablations that isolate the contribution of
each \methodname{} module and an efficiency analysis of the routing and
gradient-refinement overhead. Finally, the thesis concludes with a summary of the main findings and a discussion of promising directions for future work in
heterogeneous multi-teacher VLM distillation.
