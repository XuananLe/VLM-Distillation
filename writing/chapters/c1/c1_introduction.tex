\chapter{Introduction}
\label{chap:introduction}

\section{Background and Motivation}
Vision-Language Models (VLMs) process images and text together for tasks such
as image captioning, visual question answering, document understanding and
instruction following
\cite{radford2021learningtransferablevisualmodels,alayrac2022flamingovisuallanguagemodel,li2023blip2bootstrappinglanguageimagepretraining,liu2023visualinstructiontuning,hu2024visualprogramdistillation}.
Within this setting, the core task is to transfer strong multimodal
capabilities from stronger VLM teachers to a student VLM that remains
practical under deployment constraints. In this thesis, \textit{multimodal
understanding} refers to reading text in images,
understanding the input question and performing basic reasoning over visual and
textual evidence
\cite{singh2019vqamodelsread,mathew2021docvqadatasetvqadocument,masry2022chartqabenchmarkquestionanswering}.

Many modern VLM families still build on the basic vision-text pretraining
pattern shown in Figure~\ref{fig:intro-vlm-pretraining}. This pattern first
learns a shared space for image and text signals, then later VLM variants add
instruction tuning, cross-attention modules or language-model decoders on top of
that foundation.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.82\linewidth]{figures/Contrastive_Language-Image_Pretraining.png}
    \caption{CLIP-style vision-language pretraining with a shared image-text embedding space \cite{wikimedia_clip_pretraining_image,radford2021learningtransferablevisualmodels}.}
    \label{fig:intro-vlm-pretraining}
\end{figure}

Although alternative architectures exist for vision-language modeling, including
state-space models (SSMs) and Mamba-based designs
\cite{gu2024mambalineartimesequencemodeling}, this thesis focuses on
\textbf{Transformer methods}. All teachers and students considered in this work
use Transformer backbones for both vision and language processing.

Scaling-law results help explain why the strongest teacher models tend to be
large, since performance generally improves as model size, training data and
compute increase. Kaplan et al. quantify this scaling pattern, while Hoffmann et
al. show that compute-optimal training still requires increasing model
size and data tokens
\cite{kaplan2020scalinglaws,hoffmann2022trainingcomputeoptimal}. Larger models
therefore remain attractive for accuracy but costly to train and
deploy because they require more GPU memory, longer inference time and larger
serving infrastructure. This creates a practical gap between the models that
achieve the strongest benchmark scores and the models that can be used under
limited deployment budgets.

\begin{figure}[tp]

High-performing VLMs often incur high memory use, latency and deployment cost
\cite{alayrac2022flamingovisuallanguagemodel,li2023blip2bootstrappinglanguageimagepretraining,chu2023mobilevlm,zhou2024tinyllava}.
This constraint is more pronounced in VLMs because visual tokens increase the effective
sequence length and Transformer attention scales quadratically with sequence
length \cite{vaswani2023attentionneed,liu2023visualinstructiontuning,marafioti2025smolvlm}.
Therefore, deployment pressure motivates teacher-to-student distillation, but
the method itself is not tied to a fixed parameter budget. This setting is
relevant when the student must fit on limited hardware, serve many requests
cheaply or act as the deployable endpoint after training with stronger
teachers.

Deployment pressure is illustrated by the SmolVLM memory comparison in
Figure~\ref{fig:intro-smolvlm-memory} and by the model-scale comparison
in Figure~\ref{fig:intro-vlm-scale}.

This deployment setting is especially important for \textit{multimodal
tasks}, where the model must read image text, understand the question and
perform basic reasoning. Therefore, this thesis evaluates on TextVQA, DocVQA
and ChartQA
\cite{singh2019vqamodelsread,mathew2021docvqadatasetvqadocument,masry2022chartqabenchmarkquestionanswering}.

Although lightweight multimodal models can already be competitive
(Figure~\ref{fig:intro-small-vlm-tradeoff}), closing the remaining gap to
higher-capacity systems still requires more effective knowledge transfer from
stronger teachers.

Figures~\ref{fig:intro-scaling-laws} through~\ref{fig:intro-closed-open-gap}
summarize the central tension: scaling improves performance and competitive
teachers are available, yet practical deployment often requires efficient
student models. Knowledge
distillation (KD) bridges this gap by transferring supervision from
high-performing teachers to student models
\cite{hinton2015distillingknowledgeneuralnetwork,xu2024surveyknowledgedistillationlarge}.

Because competitive open-source VLMs are accessible as teachers, \textit{white-box KD}
becomes especially attractive. In \textit{black-box KD}, the student can only
query an external teacher service and read its returned predictions. In
\textit{white-box KD}, the student can also use teacher logits and intermediate
states \cite{wang2021blackboxkd,ma2024blackboxkd}. This additional access
enables the trie-Wasserstein KD loss used here, as well as baseline methods such as ULD and hidden-state matching, which are not
usable in a pure black-box setting
\cite{boizard2024towardscrosstokenizerdistillation,takezawa2021supervisedtreewasserstein,dasgupta2025improving,xu2024surveyknowledgedistillationlarge}.

Another major compression approach is \textit{quantization}, which
compresses an already trained model by reducing numerical
precision. It can cut memory and improve inference speed substantially, so it
is a serious practical alternative to KD
\cite{frantar2022gptq,xiao2023smoothquant,lin2024awq}.

In VLMs, however, naive low-bit quantization can hurt multimodal accuracy
\cite{lin2024awq,li2025mbq}. For this reason, we treat quantization as a
complementary deployment step, not a substitute for better transfer during
training.

Figure~\ref{fig:intro-quantization-drop} illustrates this limitation: lowering
precision can reduce VLM accuracy sharply, so quantization is treated as a
deployment complement, not the main transfer mechanism.

Heterogeneous multi-teacher distillation is harder than the standard
single-teacher setting because the teachers may differ in architecture,
tokenizer, vocabulary and task-specific strength. The candidate teachers can
come from distinct VLM families with mismatched tokenizers, output vocabularies
and strengths that vary across samples
\cite{boizard2024towardscrosstokenizerdistillation,yuan2020reinforcedmultiteacherselection,zhang2023adaptivemultiteachermetalearning}.
A naive uniform average can therefore combine useful and harmful teacher
signals for the same sample
\cite{xu2024surveyknowledgedistillationlarge,yuan2020reinforcedmultiteacherselection,zhang2023adaptivemultiteachermetalearning}.
This motivates selective multi-teacher distillation, where teacher
assignment depends on the input sample.

We present \textbf{\gracefull{} (\gracename{})}, a heterogeneous multi-teacher
distillation framework that treats frozen VLM teachers as tokenizer-specific
experts, routes a sparse teacher subset for each sample and aligns
teacher-student output distributions through trie-Wasserstein KD with
gradient-agreement refinement.

\section{Related Work}

The most relevant literature can be grouped by transfer mechanism:
matched-vocabulary logits distillation, tokenizer-mismatched distillation,
representation matching and multi-teacher selection.

\subsection{Response-Based Knowledge Distillation}

Classical KD matches softened teacher and student output distributions,
usually under a shared tokenizer or matched output space
\cite{hinton2015distillingknowledgeneuralnetwork,xu2024surveyknowledgedistillationlarge}.
When teacher and student share the same tokenizer, the common choice is a
Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence between softened logits
\cite{hinton2015distillingknowledgeneuralnetwork}. In multimodal systems,
prior work has also transferred reasoning behavior or representation knowledge
into vision-language models
\cite{fang2021compressingvlm,hu2024visualprogramdistillation,yang2024clipcid}.
These studies show that lightweight VLMs can benefit from teacher supervision,
but they usually assume one teacher or a matched output space. They therefore
do not directly address heterogeneous teachers with mismatched tokenizers.

Quantization is a separate compression line that reduces numerical precision
after or during training
\cite{frantar2022gptq,xiao2023smoothquant,lin2024awq,li2025mbq}. It helps
deployment, but it does not solve the transfer problem studied here.

\subsection{Cross-Tokenizer and Transport-Based Distillation}

Tokenizer-mismatched KD becomes necessary when teacher and student vocabularies do
not match. ULD is a direct baseline in this setting because it compares sorted
probability mass instead of token IDs
\cite{boizard2024towardscrosstokenizerdistillation}. More recent work also
studies tokenizer-agnostic distillation through transport-based formulations
\cite{takezawa2021supervisedtreewasserstein,leyamadafukumizucuturi2019treesliced,dasgupta2025improving}.

The weakness of ranked-mass matching is that it discards relations between
decoded strings. Two pairs with the same probability shape can therefore
receive the same loss even when one pair shares a long prefix and the other
does not. Tree-structured transport keeps that information by comparing mass on
shared branches of a hierarchy
\cite{takezawa2021supervisedtreewasserstein,peyre2019computationaloptimaltransport}.
This motivates objectives that match teacher and student distributions by shared
substructure instead of token ID.

\subsection{Layer Distillation}

Another line of work transfers intermediate representations instead of only
final logits. Similarity measures such as CKA provide a tokenizer-independent
way to compare hidden states across layers
\cite{kornblith2019similarityrepresentations}. This makes representation
matching attractive when teacher and student architectures differ. However,
hidden-state alignment alone does not determine which teacher should receive
the largest weight for a given sample and does not resolve conflicts among
several teachers.

\subsection{Multi-Teacher Distillation}

Multi-teacher KD has been studied with fixed averages, stochastic teacher
selection and meta-learned weighting rules
\cite{yuan2020reinforcedmultiteacherselection,zhang2023adaptivemultiteachermetalearning,yu2024selectdistill}.
These methods show the value of multiple teachers, but fixed or meta-learned
weights can still mix useful and harmful supervision when teacher utility varies
across samples.

Sparse routing and load balancing are well established in mixture-of-experts
models
\cite{shazeer2017outrageouslylargeneuralnetworks,fedus2022switchtransformersscalingtrillion,zhou2022mixtureofexpertswithexpertchoice}.
Gradient-based filtering also appears in distillation through
alignment-style objectives
\cite{zhu2021studentcustomized}. Together, these ideas suggest that a
multi-teacher student should first narrow the teacher pool and then check
whether the selected teachers provide gradients that agree with the supervised
objective.

\subsection{Research Gap}

Prior work leaves three gaps that must be handled together. \textbf{First},
heterogeneous teacher and student VLMs often use different tokenizers and
output vocabularies, so direct token-index KL is not valid. \textbf{Second}, teacher
usefulness changes across image-question pairs, which means that a fixed
teacher average can dilute useful supervision with weaker or conflicting
signals. \textbf{Third}, even after a router selects a candidate teacher set, the
routed KD gradients may still disagree with the supervised objective. These
gaps motivate a method that combines tokenizer-agnostic distribution matching,
sample-dependent teacher selection and post-routing gradient refinement.



\section{Problem Statement and Research Objectives}
Given a pool of frozen teacher VLMs and a student VLM, the goal is to learn
a heterogeneous multi-teacher multimodal distillation strategy that preserves
task performance while reducing deployment cost. The strategy must satisfy four
requirements: it must remain valid when teacher and student architectures,
tokenizers and output vocabularies differ, assign teachers adaptively for each
sample, keep routing sparse through per-sample top-\(k\) teacher selection and
suppress routed teachers whose KD direction conflicts with the supervised
objective.

\gracefull{} (\gracename{}) addresses these requirements as a selective
multi-teacher distillation method for VLMs. In this name,
``Experts'' refers to the frozen heterogeneous teacher VLMs, while ``Routed
Alignment'' refers to selecting a sparse teacher subset and aligning
teacher-student output distributions through trie-Wasserstein KD.

\section{Research Questions}
This thesis is organized around three research questions that evaluate
\methodname{} from the perspectives of predictive performance, component
contribution and computational efficiency.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{RQ1: Performance Comparison.} How does \methodname{} compare
with direct fine-tuning, same-family logits KD, ULD and
heterogeneous multi-teacher baselines on TextVQA, DocVQA and ChartQA?
\item \textbf{RQ2: Component Analysis.} How much do trie-Wasserstein KD,
sparse top-\(k\) routing and gradient-agreement refinement each contribute to
the final performance?
\item \textbf{RQ3: Efficiency Analysis.} What execution-time and
computational costs are introduced by \methodname{}?
\end{itemize}

These questions structure the experimental chapter: the main benchmark
comparison answers RQ1, the ablation study answers RQ2 and the runtime and
cost analysis answers RQ3.

\section{Thesis Contributions}
The thesis makes four main contributions that together define, implement and
evaluate \textbf{\gracefull{} (\gracename{})} for heterogeneous
teacher-to-student VLM distillation.

\paragraph{Heterogeneous multi-teacher VLM distillation.}
The thesis formulates
the problem of distilling a student VLM from teachers with mismatched
architectures, tokenizers and output vocabularies. This setting captures the
practical case where strong teachers come from different model families and
cannot be compared with the student through standard same-vocabulary logits KD.

\paragraph{Gradient-routed teacher alignment.}
The method addresses cases where
KD gradients conflict with the supervised task objective by refining routed
teacher weights according to their agreement with the CE gradient. This makes
teacher weighting both sample-dependent and objective-aware after sparse
routing has selected the candidate teacher set.

\paragraph{Tree-metric optimal transport for KD.}
The thesis introduces
trie-Wasserstein distillation, which maps decoded token strings into a shared
UTF-8 byte trie and computes KD through tree-metric
subtree-mass imbalance. This preserves token-string structure while avoiding
dense vocabulary-level optimal transport.

\paragraph{Evidence against representation-only matching.}
The experiments
evaluate hidden-state matching with CKA as a heterogeneous teacher baseline and
show that representation similarity alone is not sufficient for effective
distillation in this setting. This motivates the combination of output-level
matching, routing and gradient-aware refinement.

\section{Scope and Assumptions}

This thesis studies heterogeneous multi-teacher distillation for generative
VLM students. The proposed method is not tied to a specific student parameter
count. The experiments instantiate the setting with SmolVLM-500M and
SmolVLM-256M because they provide practical student models for evaluating
teacher-to-student transfer. The target setting is supervised visual
question answering, where each example consists of an image, a natural-language
question and a short answer sequence. The student is trained from labeled
answer tokens and teacher output distributions, not from open-ended
dialogue data or large-scale vision-language pretraining.

The method assumes white-box access to the teacher models during data
preparation. In particular, teacher logits over supervised answer positions are
available for distillation. The teachers are frozen throughout training and
only the student model and the teacher routing network are updated. This
assumption is important because the proposed trie-Wasserstein loss, the
tokenizer-mismatched baselines and the gradient-agreement scores all depend on
teacher-side probability distributions, not only final text responses.

The work does not assume that teachers and students share the same tokenizer,
vocabulary, architecture or model family. This is the main reason for using
tokenizer-agnostic distribution matching instead of direct token-index KL
divergence. The teacher pool may contain models with different strengths and
the method treats teacher usefulness as sample-dependent. GRACE therefore
combines a tokenizer-agnostic KD loss with sparse routing and post-routing
gradient refinement.

The empirical scope is limited to SmolVLM-family students and three
VQA benchmarks: TextVQA, DocVQA and ChartQA. These datasets cover
scene-text reading, document understanding and chart reasoning, but they do not
exhaust all multimodal tasks. The method is also answer-token-centric: it
distills teacher output distributions on supervised answer positions and does
not explicitly align visual tokens, cross-attention maps or region-level
evidence. These choices keep the training objective practical, but they also
define the limits of the claims made in this thesis.

\section{Thesis Organization}

The remainder of the thesis is organized as follows. Chapter~\ref{chap:preliminaries}
introduces the technical background needed to understand \methodname{}. It
begins with Transformer-based vision-language models and token-level knowledge
distillation, then narrows to the specific difficulties considered in this
thesis: tokenizer mismatch, Wasserstein and tree-structured
transport, sparse teacher routing and gradient conflict.

Chapter~\ref{chap:proposed-method} presents the proposed method. It first
formulates heterogeneous teacher-to-student VLM distillation, then
defines the main components of \methodname{}: the trie-Wasserstein
KD loss, the teacher routing network, sparse top-\(k\)
teacher selection and gradient-agreement refinement. The chapter ends by
combining these components into the final optimization objective.

Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation-methodology} describes the evaluation
methodology. It introduces the three benchmark tasks, the student and teacher
models, the training configuration, the evaluation metrics and the compared
baselines. It also records implementation details needed for reproducibility,
including offline teacher-logit preparation, remote logit storage and lazy
streaming during training.

Chapter~\ref{chap:experiment-results} reports the experimental results. It
compares \methodname{} with direct fine-tuning, same-family logits KD,
ULD, uniform multi-teacher averaging, hidden-state matching and
reinforced teacher selection. It then analyzes representation similarity,
component ablations, routing behavior and efficiency.

The final chapter summarizes the findings, discusses the main limitations and
outlines future work, including repeated-seed evaluation, lower-cost
teacher-logit storage and explicit visual-token or region-level distillation.
