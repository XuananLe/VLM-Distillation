\chapter{Introduction}
\label{chap:introduction}

The ability to reason jointly over images and text has become central to a
wide range of real-world tasks. Automated document processing pipelines
extract tables, figures and key fields from millions of scanned pages daily.
Financial and scientific workflows depend on systems that can read charts and
answer quantitative questions about them. Scene-text understanding is
increasingly embedded in consumer devices, enabling features from translation
overlays to accessibility tools. Getting a model to read text in an image or
answer a question about a chart was a research challenge just a few years ago,
but it is now a core component of production systems. Across all
of these settings, the underlying requirement is the same: a model that can
interpret visual content and produce accurate, grounded text responses.
\textbf{Vision-language models (VLMs)} have emerged as the leading approach,
combining a vision encoder, a multimodal connector and an autoregressive
language decoder into a unified architecture capable of handling this full
range of tasks
\cite{alayrac2022flamingovisuallanguagemodel,li2023blip2bootstrappinglanguageimagepretraining,liu2023visualinstructiontuning,singh2019vqamodelsread,mathew2021docvqadatasetvqadocument,masry2022chartqabenchmarkquestionanswering}.
Over the past few years these models have advanced rapidly: modern systems
can parse receipts, describe medical images and answer free-form questions
about charts with accuracy that matches or surpasses specialist tools on
several benchmarks \cite{bai2025qwen25vl,wang2024qwen2vl,chen2024internvl25}.

In this thesis, \textit{multimodal understanding} refers to practical
capabilities such as reading text in images, understanding the input question
and performing basic reasoning over visual and textual evidence, rather than
broad general intelligence
\cite{singh2019vqamodelsread,mathew2021docvqadatasetvqadocument,masry2022chartqabenchmarkquestionanswering}.

This progress, however, has come with a steep increase in model size. Neural
scaling laws favour larger language backbones and the most capable
open-weight VLMs, such as Qwen2.5-VL-72B and Llama 3-V 405B, require tens
to hundreds of gigabytes just to store their weights, well beyond the memory
budget of a single GPU
\cite{kaplan2020scalinglaws,hoffmann2022trainingcomputeoptimal,bai2025qwen25vl,grattafiori2024llama3}.
Serving them demands multi-GPU clusters that are incompatible with the
latency, cost and privacy constraints of deployment in edge devices, mobile
applications, robotics and on-premise enterprise systems
\cite{driess2023palme,brohan2023rt2}. The result is a growing gap between
what the best models can do and what can realistically be deployed: a model
that can read a receipt or interpret a chart is only useful if it can
actually run where those tasks arise and for most practical settings the
frontier models cannot.

\textbf{Knowledge distillation} offers a principled route to closing this
gap. Rather than training a compact model from scratch on \textit{hard labels}, the
student is trained to match the teacher's \textit{soft output distributions}, which
carry probability signals over alternative answers that provide far richer
supervision than one-hot targets
\cite{hinton2015distillingknowledgeneuralnetwork,xu2024surveyknowledgedistillationlarge}.
Recent lightweight VLMs produced by distillation reach competitive benchmark
scores at a fraction of the teacher's compute cost
\cite{chu2023mobilevlm,zhou2024tinyllava,marafioti2025smolvlm}. A natural
and attractive extension is to distill from a \textit{pool} of teachers drawn
from different model families, each specialising in different visual domains.
In practice, no single model is the strongest teacher across all task types:
one family may have been pretrained on richer document corpora, making it a
better supervisor for layout-heavy samples, while another may have stronger
chart or scene-text representations. Pooling their supervision could expose
a single compact student to a breadth of complementary capabilities that no
individual teacher can provide on its own.

In practice, however, heterogeneous multi-teacher distillation is harder
than it first appears and the challenges compound in ways that existing
methods do not anticipate. Teachers from different families use incompatible
tokenizers, making the standard approach of comparing output distributions
at shared vocabulary indices invalid. Teacher usefulness also varies per
sample: the strongest supervisor for a chart question is rarely the best
choice for a scanned receipt or a scene-text image, so fixed-weight
averaging dilutes or wastes the most relevant signals. Most critically, even
a well-matched teacher can produce KD gradients that directly oppose the
supervised learning objective, degrading the student instead of improving it.
These three constraints, \textbf{tokenizer mismatch}, \textbf{per-sample teacher relevance}
and \textbf{gradient conflict}, must be resolved simultaneously, yet no existing
method addresses all three at once
\cite{boizard2024towardscrosstokenizerdistillation,yuan2020reinforcedmultiteacherselection,yu2024selectdistill}.

\textbf{\gracefull{} (\gracename{})} treats each of the three constraints as
a first-class design requirement, not an edge case. To handle
tokenizer mismatch, it encodes all decoded tokens as UTF-8 byte sequences
and compares teacher and student output distributions through a shared byte
trie. Tokens that spell similar strings incur low transport cost regardless
of which vocabulary produced them, giving a meaningful signal
even when teacher and student tokenizers are completely different. To handle
per-sample teacher relevance, a lightweight router conditioned on the
student's own hidden states selects a small subset of teachers for each
input, so the supervision mix adapts to the content of the image and
question instead of remaining fixed across all training samples. To handle
gradient conflict, a refinement step measures how well each selected
teacher's KD gradient aligns with the supervised CE gradient and
down-weights any teacher whose signal would pull the student away from the
correct answer even when that teacher appears relevant.

Together, these three components form a distillation framework in which
teacher diversity is genuinely put to use: each sample receives supervision
from the teachers most suited to it, filtered by both content relevance and
optimization compatibility. With a 500M-parameter student and a four-teacher
pool drawn from three model families, \methodname{} closes more
than half the original teacher-student gap on two of three benchmarks. This
result suggests that capable VLMs can be built on a single GPU and that
the primary obstacle to doing so is not model size but the quality and
consistency of the distillation signal.

Figure~\ref{fig:good-example} shows what this looks like in practice.
The image is a low-quality, angled photo of a phone screen and the
question is simply: what month is shown? The text is barely readable,
the screen is tilted and the whole thing looks like a casual snapshot
and not a clean document scan. The original student model, without any
distillation, does not even attempt the question, it just describes
the phone in vague terms and misses the point entirely. The teacher
reads \textit{May} straight off the screen. After \methodname{}, the
small student does the same. That shift, from ignoring the question to
reading the answer correctly, is what the distillation signal is
actually buying.

This thesis investigates three
questions to characterise where these gains come from:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{RQ1: Performance.} How does \methodname{} compare with direct
fine-tuning, same-family logit-based KD, cross-tokenizer single-teacher
distillation and heterogeneous multi-teacher baselines on the target VQA
benchmarks?

\newpage{}

\item \textbf{RQ2: Component Analysis.} How much do the cross-tokenizer
distillation loss, the routing mechanism and the gradient-conflict resolution
step each contribute to the final performance? Specifically, does adding
teacher diversity alone account for the gains over single-teacher baselines,
or does the adaptive routing and gradient filtering provide independent
and measurable improvements on top of diversity?
\item \textbf{RQ3: Efficiency.} What runtime overhead does \methodname{}
introduce during training?
\end{itemize}

This thesis makes three main contributions:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Byte-Trie Wasserstein distillation.}
    We propose a KD loss that maps teacher and student tokens onto a shared
    UTF-8 byte trie and compares output distributions through byte-path
    proximity. Without this, direct output comparison across heterogeneous
    teachers is invalid. The proposed loss removes this assumption entirely, enabling
    distillation from any teacher regardless of its tokenizer or vocabulary
    size.

    \item \textbf{Objective-aware multi-teacher weighting.}
    Top-\(k\) routing selects candidate teachers per sample, gradient
    agreement then filters their KD signals against the supervised CE
    objective, suppressing teachers whose gradients conflict with learning.

    \item \textbf{Empirical validation on VQA benchmarks.}
    We evaluate \methodname{} on \textbf{TextVQA}, \textbf{DocVQA} and
    \textbf{ChartQA} with two SmolVLM students and a four-teacher
    heterogeneous pool. \methodname{} outperforms all compared baselines,
    with SmolVLM-500M it closes 55\%, 40\% and 55\% of the original
    teacher-student gap on the three benchmarks. 
\end{itemize}

The thesis is organized as follows. Chapter~\ref{chap:preliminaries} reviews
Vision-Language Models (VLMs), Knowledge Distillation (KD), cross-tokenizer
matching, sparse routing and gradient conflict resolution.
Chapter~\ref{chap:proposed-method} presents \methodname{} in detail.
Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation-methodology} describes the datasets, evaluation
metrics, baselines and ablation settings. Chapter~\ref{chap:experiment-results}
reports the main comparative results, ablation studies, efficiency analysis
and discussion. Finally, Chapter~5 summarizes the key findings and discusses
limitations and directions for future work.
