\chapter{Proposed Method}
\label{chap:proposed-method}

This chapter presents \textbf{\gracefull{} (\gracename{})} by first defining
the heterogeneous multi-teacher distillation setting, then introducing the
\textbf{trie-Wasserstein KD loss}, \textbf{teacher routing network},
\textbf{gradient-agreement refinement mechanism} and \textbf{final training
objective}. \methodname{} addresses three core difficulties in heterogeneous
teacher-to-student VLM distillation: vocabulary mismatch that makes teacher
and student output spaces incomparable, variable teacher usefulness across
samples, and KD gradients that conflict with the supervised objective. Each
component of the method corrects one failure that the previous component
cannot fix, so the pieces form a dependency chain, not an independent
collection of techniques.

\section{Method Overview and Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:method-overview-formulation}

We consider multimodal understanding tasks that require reading image
text, understanding the question and generating an answer sequence
\cite{singh2019vqamodelsread,mathew2021docvqadatasetvqadocument,masry2022chartqabenchmarkquestionanswering}.
At its core, GRACE separates two questions that most KD methods conflate: what
does the teacher believe, and should the student trust this teacher here.
Trie-Wasserstein KD makes teacher beliefs comparable across tokenizers, sparse
routing asks which teachers are relevant for this sample, and
gradient-agreement refinement asks which of those teachers is currently
compatible with the supervised objective.
The training set is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{D}=\{(x_n,y_n)\}_{n=1}^{N_{\mathrm{data}}},
\end{equation}
where \(x_n\) is a multimodal input and \(y_n\) is the target answer sequence.
Let \(S_{\theta}\) denote the student VLM with trainable parameters
\(\theta\), let \(G_{\phi}\) denote the trainable teacher router with
parameters \(\phi\) and let \(\mathcal{T}=\{T_i\}_{i=1}^{N}\) be the frozen
teacher pool. For each sample \(b\), the student produces logits
\(\mathbf{z}_s^{(b)}\) and teacher \(T_i\) provides teacher logits
\(\mathbf{z}_t^{(b,i)}\).

Throughout this chapter, the frozen teachers are treated as tokenizer-specific
experts: each teacher may use a different tokenizer, vocabulary and model
family and the router selects which experts contribute to each training
sample.
The full training pipeline is summarized in
Figure~\ref{fig:grace-architecture}. Four components define the method.
\textbf{Trie-Wasserstein KD} computes per-teacher distillation losses over a
shared byte trie, enabling tokenizer-agnostic output matching. The
\textbf{teacher routing network} selects a sparse subset of candidate teachers
from student-side hidden states at each training step.
\textbf{Gradient-agreement refinement} adjusts the routed teacher weights
based on how well each teacher's distillation gradient aligns with the
supervised objective. Finally, the \textbf{training objective} combines
supervised cross-entropy, routed KD and router auxiliary losses into a single
end-to-end update. These components are ordered by dependency:
trie-Wasserstein makes cross-tokenizer comparison possible but does not choose
which teacher to use. Routing chooses teachers but does not check whether
their gradients help. Gradient-agreement refinement corrects routed weights
but still requires trie-Wasserstein to produce meaningful per-teacher losses in
the first place. Together, they extend sparse expert routing and token-level
logit supervision to the heterogeneous multi-teacher VLM setting,
where teachers and student may use different tokenizers and vocabularies
\cite{shazeer2017outrageouslylargeneuralnetworks,fedus2022switchtransformersscalingtrillion,zhu2021studentcustomized}.
The main notation used throughout the method is collected in
Table~\ref{tab:notation}. The next subsection walks through one complete update
before the remaining sections define each component formally.

\subsection{Training Flow}
\label{subsec:training-flow}

A GRACE update starts from a mini-batch
\(\mathcal{B}=\{(x_b,y_b)\}_{b=1}^{B}\) and a frozen teacher pool
\(\mathcal{T}=\{T_i\}_{i=1}^{N}\). The student first produces answer-token
logits, hidden states and the supervised cross-entropy loss. The hidden states
serve a dual role: they participate in the CE computation and act as a compact
description of the sample that drives teacher routing. Supervised answer
positions define both the CE objective and the hidden-state region pooled by
the router. Sparse routing selects a teacher subset
\(\mathcal{R}_b\), trie-Wasserstein KD computes per-teacher losses
\(\mathcal{L}_{KD}^{(b,i)}\), and gradient-agreement refinement converts
routing scores into final KD weights. The router's answer to which teachers
are relevant and the agreement check's answer to which teachers are compatible
together ensure that only useful teacher signals reach the optimization. The
update ends by combining supervised CE, routed KD and router regularization
terms into one optimization objective.

\section{Trie-Wasserstein Cross-Tokenizer Distillation}
\label{sec:trie-wasserstein-distillation}

\subsection{Supervised Answer Tokens}

Distillation is applied only on \textbf{supervised answer tokens}, not on prompt
tokens. For sample \(b\), let \(\Omega_b^{CE}\) denote the student positions
whose labels are not ignored. For teacher \(T_i\), let \(\Omega_{b,i}^{KD}\)
denote the matched answer positions after masking ignored labels on both
student and teacher sides and truncating both spans to a common length. This is
a position-wise approximation across tokenizers: after answer-span
preprocessing, the \(j\)-th student and teacher answer positions are treated as
corresponding positions. Trie-Wasserstein matching handles the token-distribution
mismatch at each aligned position, while sequence-level byte alignment remains
outside the objective. Restricting distillation to answer positions avoids
wasting capacity on prompt formatting, padding and instruction wording, where
teacher and student distributions carry no task-relevant signal.

\subsection{Shared Byte Trie}

For each sample \(b\), token position \(j \in \Omega_{b,i}^{KD}\) and teacher \(T_i\), let
\(\mathbf{z}_{s,j}^{(b)} \in \mathbb{R}^{V_s}\) be the student logits and
\(\mathbf{z}_{t,j}^{(b,i)} \in \mathbb{R}^{V_i}\) be the teacher logits.
The student and teacher probability distributions are
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{p}_{s,j}^{(b)}
=
\mathrm{softmax}\!\left(\frac{\mathbf{z}_{s,j}^{(b)}}{\tau_s}\right),
\qquad
\mathbf{p}_{t,j}^{(b,i)}
=
\mathrm{softmax}\!\left(\frac{\mathbf{z}_{t,j}^{(b,i)}}{\tau_t}\right),
\end{equation}
where \(\tau_s\) and \(\tau_t\) are the student and teacher temperatures.

Direct token-index matching assumes that bin 17 in the teacher vocabulary
means the same thing as bin 17 in the student vocabulary. That assumption fails
entirely when tokenizers differ. Instead, GRACE maps decoded teacher and
student token strings into a \textbf{shared UTF-8 byte trie}, so tokens live in
a shared prefix tree, not arbitrary vocabulary-index space. Tokens
with common byte prefixes share the same initial trie path even if their
tokenizer IDs are unrelated. UTF-8 bytes
provide a fixed alphabet and expose tokenizer artifacts such as leading spaces,
wordpiece prefixes and multimodal special tokens. Character-level matching is
less stable because the same visible text may have different Unicode code-point
sequences. Concretely, a token with a leading space maps to bytes
\([32,99,97,116]\), while \texttt{cat} maps to \([99,97,116]\): the same
visible string can have a different byte sequence, so byte-level comparison is
necessary for consistent matching.

Each token string is converted to a byte sequence and terminated with an EOS
sentinel. The sentinel separates complete tokens from strict prefixes, so
\texttt{doc} and \texttt{document} remain distinct leaves even though they share
an initial path. The practical effect is a shift from matching identical token
IDs to keeping probability mass on similar decoded strings close, giving all
tokenizers a common comparison space. The trie is therefore more than a
tokenizer-compatibility device: it defines a geometry over decoded token
strings. Tokens such as \texttt{doc}, \texttt{document} and
\texttt{documentation} are close because they share deep trie paths, while
tokens such as \texttt{chart}, \texttt{invoice} and \texttt{red} diverge
immediately. That geometry is well suited to OCR-heavy VQA, where correct and
near-correct answers often differ by a prefix, a suffix, a space or a number
format instead of being lexically unrelated. For a normal byte-trie or EOS edge
\(e\), let \(d(e)\) be its depth. GRACE assigns depth-based edge weights as
follows:
\begin{equation}
w(e)=\rho_{\mathrm{trie}}^{d(e)-1},
\label{eq:tw_edge_weight}
\end{equation}
where \(0<\rho_{\mathrm{trie}}<1\). The first edge from the root has weight
\(1\) and deeper edges decay geometrically. This makes shared prefixes cheaper
to transport across than unrelated byte paths: two tokens that agree on the
first three bytes pay much less than two tokens with no overlap at all. The
loss therefore penalizes how far the student's probability mass must move
through the shared lexical tree to resemble the teacher's mass, not whether the
student assigns probability to the same token indices. The decay still
preserves a valid tree metric
\cite{takezawa2021supervisedtreewasserstein,peyre2019computationaloptimaltransport}.
Different tokenizers may stop at different depths, but they still share the
same byte-prefix edges when their decoded token strings overlap in byte-prefix
structure. The example in Figure~\ref{fig:grace-trie-shared} uses the pair
\texttt{document}/\texttt{receipt} and \texttt{doc}/\texttt{rece}.

\subsection{Trie-Wasserstein Objective}

The shared byte trie induces a tree metric over decoded token strings. Let
\(\mathcal{V}_s\) and \(\mathcal{V}_t\) denote the student and teacher token
vocabularies being compared at a given answer position and let \(\pi(v)\)
denote the trie path of decoded token \(v\). For an edge \(e\), the student
and teacher subtree masses are
\[
m_s(e)
=
\sum_{\substack{v\in\mathcal{V}_s\\ e\in\pi(v)}} p_s(v),
\qquad
m_t(e)
=
\sum_{\substack{u\in\mathcal{V}_t\\ e\in\pi(u)}} p_t(u).
\]
\(m_s(e)\) and \(m_t(e)\) measure how much probability mass each distribution
assigns to token paths passing through edge \(e\). The \(W_1\)
optimal transport distance between teacher and student token distributions on
the trie can then be written as a weighted sum of subtree-mass imbalances. If
teacher and student both concentrate mass on strings beginning with
\texttt{doc}, they are already aligned along that branch. If one puts mass on
\texttt{doc} and the other on \texttt{abc}, the mass diverges near the root and
the transport cost is high. The loss rewards lexical closeness instead of exact
token-ID equality:
\begin{equation}
W_T(p_s,p_t)
=
\sum_{e\in E}
w(e)\left|m_s(e)-m_t(e)\right|.
\label{eq:trie-wasserstein-objective}
\end{equation}
This form follows from the standard \(W_1\) Kantorovich optimal transport
problem under the trie-induced tree metric
\cite{peyre2019computationaloptimaltransport,takezawa2021supervisedtreewasserstein}.
The sparse construction below
applies the same formula to a bounded top-\(K_{\mathrm{TW}}\) support with a
residual tail edge.

\subsection{Sparse Support and Residual Tail Edge}

Equation~\eqref{eq:trie-wasserstein-objective} is exact on the complete shared
trie, but evaluating every vocabulary token at every answer position is
expensive. GRACE builds a \textbf{sparse augmented trie} instead, so most
useful distributional information, concentrated in high-probability tokens, is
captured exactly. The tail edge handles the remainder: without it, omitted mass
would silently disappear, making the teacher appear more confident than it
really is and distorting the transport distance. At each aligned answer
position, it masks ignored vocabulary entries, retains the
\textbf{top-\(K_{\mathrm{TW}}\) student and teacher tokens}, and inserts those
tokens with their exact byte paths.
Probability mass outside the retained sets is not discarded. It is collapsed
into a single \textbf{residual tail edge}.
This gives an exact distance on the collapsed sparse trie while approximating
the complete trie distance with one tail atom for omitted vocabulary mass.

Let \(K_{\mathrm{TW}}\) denote the sparse trie support size. Let
\(\mathcal{V}_s^\star\) and \(\mathcal{V}_t^\star\) denote the non-ignored
student and teacher vocabularies. Before constructing the sparse trie, the
student and teacher probabilities are renormalized over the non-ignored
vocabularies so that the retained and residual masses form valid
distributions. Let
\(\mathcal{K}_{s,j}^{(b)}\subset\mathcal{V}_s^\star\) and
\(\mathcal{K}_{t,j}^{(b,i)}\subset\mathcal{V}_t^\star\) be the retained token
sets. The residual mass assigned to the tail edge is
\begin{equation}
r_{s,j}^{(b)}
=
1-\sum_{v\in\mathcal{K}_{s,j}^{(b)}}p_{s,j}^{(b)}(v),
\qquad
r_{t,j}^{(b,i)}
=
1-\sum_{v\in\mathcal{K}_{t,j}^{(b,i)}}p_{t,j}^{(b,i)}(v).
\end{equation}

Let \(\pi(v)\) be the trie path for token \(v\) and let
\(e_{\mathrm{tail}}\) be the global residual tail edge with
\(w(e_{\mathrm{tail}})=1\). For any active trie edge \(e\), the sparse subtree
masses are
\begin{equation}
m_{s,j}^{(b)}(e)
=
\sum_{\substack{v\in\mathcal{K}_{s,j}^{(b)}\\e\in\pi(v)}}
p_{s,j}^{(b)}(v)
\;+\;
\mathbf{1}[e=e_{\mathrm{tail}}]\,r_{s,j}^{(b)},
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
m_{t,j}^{(b,i)}(e)
=
\sum_{\substack{v\in\mathcal{K}_{t,j}^{(b,i)}\\e\in\pi(v)}}
p_{t,j}^{(b,i)}(v)
\;+\;
\mathbf{1}[e=e_{\mathrm{tail}}]\,r_{t,j}^{(b,i)}.
\end{equation}
The token-level trie-Wasserstein loss is
\begin{equation}
\ell_{TW}\!\left(\mathbf{z}_{s,j}^{(b)},\mathbf{z}_{t,j}^{(b,i)}\right)
=
\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}_{act,j}^{(b,i)}}
w(e)\,
\left|
m_{s,j}^{(b)}(e)-m_{t,j}^{(b,i)}(e)
\right|,
\label{eq:token_tw}
\end{equation}
where \(\mathcal{E}_{act,j}^{(b,i)}\) contains the active student edges, active
teacher edges and the residual tail edge. Before the absolute value is applied,
\(m_{s,j}^{(b)}(e)-m_{t,j}^{(b,i)}(e)\) is positive when edge \(e\) carries more
student mass and negative when it carries more teacher mass.

The per-sample, per-teacher KD loss is the mean over supervised answer
tokens:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{KD}^{(b,i)}
=
\frac{1}{|\Omega_{b,i}^{KD}|}
\sum_{j\in\Omega_{b,i}^{KD}}
\ell_{TW}\!\left(\mathbf{z}_{s,j}^{(b)},\mathbf{z}_{t,j}^{(b,i)}\right).
\label{eq:per_teacher_tw}
\end{equation}

Because the tail edge accounts for all omitted vocabulary mass, the sparse
trie loss is exact on the collapsed structure instead of simply dropping the
tail.

\textbf{Complexity analysis.}
The shared byte trie is built once with time and memory cost
\(O(V_s \bar{L}_s + V_t \bar{L}_t)\), where \(V_s\) and \(V_t\) are the
student and teacher vocabulary sizes and \(\bar{L}_s\) and \(\bar{L}_t\) are
the average byte-path lengths. For one aligned answer position, extracting the
top-\(K_{\mathrm{TW}}\) student and teacher tokens costs
\(O(V_s\log K_{\mathrm{TW}} + V_t\log K_{\mathrm{TW}})\) with heap-based
selection. Given the retained token sets, sparse subtree-mass accumulation
walks only the retained token paths and the residual tail edge, costing
\(O(K_{\mathrm{TW}}\bar{L}_s + K_{\mathrm{TW}}\bar{L}_t + A)\), where \(A\)
is the number of active trie edges. If the implementation explicitly sorts or
deduplicates active edge indices, this adds an \(O(A\log A)\) term. Otherwise,
indexed accumulation gives an \(O(A)\) edge-aggregation cost. Runtime memory is
\(O(E + P_s + P_t + A)\), where \(E\) is the number of trie
edges and \(P_s\) and \(P_t\) are the stored student and teacher path-index
arrays. Unlike dense optimal transport, the sparse computation does not materialize a
\(|V_s| \times |V_t|\) transport matrix.
This gives a tokenizer-agnostic loss that compares probability mass on shared
trie edges instead of matching token indices directly. The
top-\(K_{\mathrm{TW}}\)+tail approximation keeps this structured transport view
bounded by the retained support.

\section{Teacher Routing Network}
\label{sec:teacher-routing-network}

\subsection{Router Architecture}

With multiple teachers, uniform averaging ignores sample-specific teacher
strengths: a document question, a chart question and a scene-text question may
each benefit from a different expert, and the student's own hidden states are
the most direct signal of which kind of example is being processed. This
distinguishes GRACE routing from sparse mixture-of-experts routing in standard
transformers. MoE routing chooses which expert computes the forward pass, while
GRACE routing chooses which teacher shapes the student's learning signal. The
experts here are frozen external models, not trainable FFN blocks, so routing
is a supervision decision, not a computation decision. GRACE therefore
uses a \textbf{teacher routing network} for projector-based VLM students whose
language-model head exposes the text hidden width. The routing pipeline, from
pooled student features to sparse top-\(k\) teacher selection, is shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:deep-router}. Let
\(\mathbf{H}_s^{(b)}\) denote the student hidden states captured immediately
before the language-model head for sample \(b\). Pooling these hidden states
over supervised answer positions gives the \textbf{routing feature}:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{h}^{(b)} = \mathrm{Pool}\!\left(\mathbf{H}_s^{(b)}\right).
\end{equation}
Pooling over answer positions captures what the student currently represents
about the output, which is a more informative routing signal than pooling over
the full sequence. Crucially, the routing feature is student-state-dependent,
not just dataset-dependent. The same image-question pair can produce a
different routing decision as the student's representations evolve during
training, allowing teacher selection to adapt to the student's changing needs.
The operation uses the mean over supervised answer positions and is defined for
samples with at least one supervised answer token. A learned MLP router
\(G_{\phi}(\cdot)\) then produces gate logits
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{r}^{(b)} = G_{\phi}\!\left(\mathbf{h}^{(b)}\right).
\label{eq:router-logits}
\end{equation}
Applying softmax to the router logits gives soft routing weights:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{p}^{(b)} = \mathrm{softmax}\!\left(\mathbf{r}^{(b)}\right),
\label{eq:router-softmax}
\end{equation}
where \(p_i^{(b)}\) is the initial gate weight for teacher \(T_i\).
The router masks to the top-\(k_{\mathrm{route}}\) teachers, renormalizes the
selected softmax weights, and uses the LayerNorm, up-projection, gate-value
split, SiLU gating and down-projection stack shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:deep-router}.

\subsection{Top-k Routing}

Soft routing weights are dense, but distillation should not use every teacher
for every sample. GRACE converts them into a \textbf{sparse routed set} by
selecting the highest-scoring teachers for sample \(b\):
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{C}_b=\mathrm{TopK}(\mathbf{r}^{(b)},\min(k_{\mathrm{route}},N)).
\end{equation}
The candidate set \(\mathcal{C}_b\) serves as the routed teacher set
\(\mathcal{R}_b\). With
\(m_i^{(b)}=\mathbf{1}[i\in\mathcal{C}_b]\) denoting the top-\(k\) assignment
mask, non-selected weights are zeroed and the remaining softmax weights are
renormalized:
\begin{equation}
\hat{p}_i^{(b)}
=
\frac{m_i^{(b)} p_i^{(b)}}{\sum_{j=1}^{N} m_j^{(b)} p_j^{(b)}}.
\label{eq:constrained_gate_weights}
\end{equation}

Dense averaging can dilute a strong teacher with weak or irrelevant ones.
Top-\(k\) routing makes supervision sparse and sample-specialized instead.
Routing alone is not sufficient, however. A teacher can look relevant because
its domain matches the input while its gradient still conflicts with the
supervised target. For example, a document-specialized teacher may assign high
probability to a plausible but incorrect field value. Gradient-agreement
refinement, introduced in Section~\ref{sec:gradient-agreement-refinement},
addresses this residual failure. This sparse routed teacher set keeps the
per-sample distillation cost bounded by \(k_{\mathrm{route}}\) teachers and
provides the candidate pool that refinement operates on.

    \caption[Top-\(k\) routing dynamics.]{Top-\(k\) routing dynamics.}
    \label{fig:grace-routing-dynamics}
\end{figure}
Figure~\ref{fig:grace-routing-dynamics} contrasts dense gate probabilities
with the sparse routed assignment. The logged assignment rate records how often
each teacher appears in the routed set.

\subsection{Router Entropy and Z-Loss}

Two auxiliary terms regularize teacher selection. \textbf{Entropy
regularization} is implemented as a negative-entropy term that discourages
premature routing collapse:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{ent}
=
-\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B} H(\mathbf{p}^{(b)}).
\end{equation}
The \textbf{router \(z\)-loss} keeps router logits numerically well behaved:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{z}
=
\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}
\left(\log \sum_{i=1}^{N} e^{r_i^{(b)}}\right)^2.
\end{equation}
Both terms are pure stabilizers: entropy discourages the router from collapsing
onto a single teacher too early, while z-loss keeps routing logits numerically
controlled. Neither competes with the CE or KD objectives.

\section{Gradient-Agreement Refinement}
\label{sec:gradient-agreement-refinement}

Routed teachers may still conflict with the supervised objective after teacher
selection. \textbf{Gradient-agreement refinement} checks whether each selected
teacher's KD direction agrees with the supervised CE direction before its loss
is trusted. Routing answers which teacher seems relevant to this sample.
Gradient agreement answers which of those teachers is currently compatible with
learning the correct answer, acting as the method's truth check on the router's
nominations. The two checks are complementary because relevance does not
guarantee gradient compatibility.

\subsection{Gradient Agreement Scoring}

Let \(\Theta\subseteq\theta\) denote the trainable student parameters used by the
optimizer. To score each teacher, GRACE compares its
\textbf{teacher-specific KD gradient} against the \textbf{supervised CE
gradient} in student-parameter space. The CE gradient is
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{g}_{CE}
=
\nabla_{\Theta}\mathcal{L}_{CE}.
\end{equation}
For teacher \(T_i\), the KD gradient comes from that teacher's mean
distillation loss over the full mini-batch:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{g}_{i}
=
\nabla_{\Theta}
\left(
\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\mathcal{L}_{KD}^{(b,i)}
\right).
\end{equation}
Here, \(\mathcal{L}_{KD}^{(b,i)}\) is the trie-Wasserstein loss from
Equation~\ref{eq:per_teacher_tw}. These gradients only score teacher
compatibility. The final parameter update is still computed from the full
training objective. A teacher whose KD gradient points opposite to the CE
gradient is pulling the student away from the correct answer, even if the
router selected it confidently. To see why, consider the first-order parameter
update
\(\Delta\theta \propto -(\mathbf{g}_{CE} + \alpha\sum_i w_i \mathbf{g}_i)\).
When \(\langle\mathbf{g}_{CE},\mathbf{g}_i\rangle > 0\), teacher \(T_i\)
reinforces the supervised direction. When
\(\langle\mathbf{g}_{CE},\mathbf{g}_i\rangle < 0\), its contribution partially
opposes the supervised update and increases the first-order CE loss instead of
reducing it. Cosine similarity therefore approximates whether this teacher's
KD update will help or hurt the supervised objective in the next step.

\subsection{Agreement Scores and Active Set}

Directional agreement between supervised learning and KD is measured with
cosine similarity:
\begin{equation}
s_i
=
\frac{
\langle \mathbf{g}_{CE},\mathbf{g}_i\rangle
}{
\max\!\left(\|\mathbf{g}_{CE}\|_2\|\mathbf{g}_i\|_2,\epsilon_{\mathrm{num}}\right)
}.
\end{equation}

The agreement score \(s_i\) is \textbf{teacher-level} within the current
mini-batch and is broadcast across samples. Sample dependence enters through
the routed set \(\mathcal{R}_b\), routed weights \(\hat{\mathbf{p}}^{(b)}\) and
active set \(\mathcal{A}_b\). To combine teacher-level agreement with
sample-specific routing, GRACE first converts the agreement score into a binary
teacher flag:
\begin{equation}
\tilde{a}_i = \mathbf{1}[s_i > \delta],
\end{equation}
where \(\delta\) is the GRACE threshold. This flag is then intersected with
the routed teacher set for each sample:
\begin{equation}
a_i^{(b)}=\mathbf{1}[i\in\mathcal{R}_b]\tilde{a}_i,
\qquad
\mathcal{A}_b=\{i\in\mathcal{R}_b:a_i^{(b)}=1\}.
\end{equation}

The intersection with \(\mathcal{R}_b\) keeps the search inside the routed
candidates: routing narrows the pool first, and gradient agreement filters
within it. Computing agreement at the teacher level over the full mini-batch,
instead of at the sample level, is a deliberate trade-off. A sample-level
agreement score would be more precise but prohibitively noisy and expensive.
The batch-level score is cheaper and more stable, while sample-level adaptation
is preserved through the routed set \(\mathcal{R}_b\). The final active set
\(\mathcal{A}_b\) intersects both signals: batch-level objective compatibility
from gradient agreement and sample-level relevance from routing.
The threshold \(\delta\) is applied to the raw score \(s_i\), while the
EMA-smoothed score \(\hat{s}_i\) controls score-softmax weighting and fallback
spread. Agreement scores act as non-differentiable routing statistics, so the
final optimization step does not backpropagate through the gradient-agreement
computation itself.

\subsection{Warmup, EMA Smoothing and Score Weights}

Teacher weights can change abruptly when agreement scores are estimated from a
single mini-batch. GRACE stabilizes this step with \textbf{warmup},
\textbf{EMA smoothing} and \textbf{score-softmax weighting}. Let
\(\rho_g \in [0,1]\) denote the warmup ratio relative to the total number of
training steps. During the first \(\rho_g T_{\mathrm{train}}\) steps, routed
gate weights are left unchanged:
\begin{equation}
w_i^{(b)}=\hat{p}_i^{(b)}.
\label{eq:grace_warmup_router_weights}
\end{equation}

Early in training, the student's representations and the router's assignments
are both unstable, so gradient-based filtering applied too soon risks rejecting
useful teachers because the agreement scores themselves are meaningless.
Warmup prevents the agreement mechanism from overfitting to early gradient
noise instead of genuine teacher usefulness.
After warmup, routed teachers are refined with a smoothed score distribution.
Let \(\bar{s}_i^{\mathrm{prev}}\) be the running exponential moving average
(EMA) of past batch means for teacher \(T_i\) and let
\(\beta_{\mathrm{EMA}}\) be its decay. The smoothed score is
\begin{equation}
\hat{s}_i
=
\beta_{\mathrm{EMA}} \bar{s}_i^{\mathrm{prev}}
+
(1-\beta_{\mathrm{EMA}}) s_i.
\end{equation}

EMA smoothing turns gradient agreement from a momentary vote into a reputation
score. A teacher's raw score on one batch answers whether it was helpful this
step, while the smoothed score answers whether it has been consistently helpful
recently. This reduces oscillation in teacher weights and makes the scoring
more robust to difficult batches on which even a useful teacher may temporarily
misalign. For nonempty active sets, smoothed scores are converted into a
score-softmax weight vector over \(\mathcal{A}_b\):
\begin{equation}
\omega_i^{(b)}
=
\frac{
\exp(\beta_{\mathrm{score}}\hat{s}_i) \mathbf{1}[i \in \mathcal{A}_b]
}{
\sum_{j=1}^{N}
\exp(\beta_{\mathrm{score}}\hat{s}_j) \mathbf{1}[j \in \mathcal{A}_b]
},
\label{eq:grace_softmax}
\end{equation}
\(\beta_{\mathrm{score}}\) controls how sharply the highest-scoring active
teacher is preferred, setting up the fallback and blending cases below.

\subsection{Weight Blending and Fallback}

Final KD weights are assigned through three mutually exclusive cases for each
sample. The three cases implement a calibrated decision rule. When evidence is
missing, GRACE uses the router fallback. When evidence is weak, it applies
uniform active weights instead of treating a small score difference as
meaningful. When evidence is confident, agreement drives the weighting.
Overcommitting to weak gradient signals is as harmful as ignoring strong ones.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{No active teacher.} If \(\mathcal{A}_b=\emptyset\), the routed
weights are retained:
\begin{equation}
w_i^{(b)}=\hat{p}_i^{(b)}.
\end{equation}

\item \textbf{Low-confidence scores.} If active teachers exist but their
smoothed scores are too close, uniform weights are used over the active set.
The score range is
\begin{equation}
\Delta^{(b)}
=
\max_{i \in \mathcal{A}_b}\hat{s}_i
-
\min_{i \in \mathcal{A}_b}\hat{s}_i.
\end{equation}
If \(\mathcal{A}_b\neq\emptyset\) and
\(\Delta^{(b)} < \epsilon_{\mathrm{fb}}\), then
\begin{equation}
w_i^{(b)}
=
\frac{\mathbf{1}[i \in \mathcal{A}_b]}
{|\mathcal{A}_b|}.
\label{eq:alignment_fallback}
\end{equation}

\item \textbf{Confident scores.} Otherwise, router weights are blended with
score weights. Let
\(\lambda_{\mathrm{blend}} \in [0,1]\) be the router-blend exponent. The
unnormalized blended weights are
\begin{equation}
\tilde{w}_i^{(b)}
\propto
\left(\hat{p}_i^{(b)}\right)^{\lambda_{\mathrm{blend}}}
\left(\omega_i^{(b)}\right)^{1-\lambda_{\mathrm{blend}}},
\end{equation}
followed by normalization over the active routed teachers:
\begin{equation}
w_i^{(b)}
=
\frac{\tilde{w}_i^{(b)} \mathbf{1}[i \in \mathcal{A}_b]}
{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\tilde{w}_j^{(b)} \mathbf{1}[j \in \mathcal{A}_b]}.
\label{eq:effective_teacher_weights}
\end{equation}
\end{itemize}

Equations~\ref{eq:grace_softmax}, \ref{eq:alignment_fallback} and
\ref{eq:effective_teacher_weights} define the gradient-refinement operator:
the router chooses \(\mathcal{R}_b\), agreement filtering narrows it to
\(\mathcal{A}_b\), and final weights are retained from the router, assigned
uniformly or geometrically blended depending on confidence. Neither signal is
trusted alone because they measure different things: router weights capture
sample-specific relevance, while agreement weights capture objective
compatibility. A teacher could be globally gradient-aligned but irrelevant to a
specific sample type, while another could be sample-relevant but currently
conflicting with the supervised objective. The geometric blend respects both,
with \(\lambda_{\mathrm{blend}}\) controlling how much the router's domain
judgment is retained against the agreement signal. These regimes are shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:grace-alignment-cases}.

\vspace{-0.4em}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/grace_alignment_cases.png}
    \caption[Representative KD weighting regimes.]{Representative KD weighting regimes.}
    \label{fig:grace-alignment-cases}
\end{figure}

This design keeps teacher selection sparse throughout: gradient-agreement
refinement can narrow the routed set but never expands it, so KD cost remains
bounded by \(k_{\mathrm{route}}\) teachers per sample.

\section{Routed KD Aggregation and Final Objective}
\label{sec:routed-kd-final-objective}
\label{sec:final-objective}

The final teacher weights \(w_i^{(b)}\) combine the routed teacher set
\(\mathcal{R}_b\), router weights \(\hat{\mathbf{p}}^{(b)}\) and
gradient-agreement scores. The objective aggregates routed per-teacher KD
losses into a sample-level term, averages those terms across the mini-batch and
then adds supervised and router-regularization losses.

\textbf{Sample-level aggregation.} For sample \(b\), only teachers in the
routed set \(\mathcal{R}_b\) contribute to the KD loss:
\begin{equation}
\bar{\mathcal{L}}_{KD}^{(b)}
=
\sum_{i\in\mathcal{R}_b} w_i^{(b)}\mathcal{L}_{KD}^{(b,i)}.
\label{eq:sample-kd-aggregation}
\end{equation}
Non-routed teachers have zero contribution, while routed teachers are weighted
by the fallback, uniform-active or blended rule. All post-routing refinement
decisions enter the optimization through \(w_i^{(b)}\).

\textbf{Batch-level aggregation.}
The mini-batch KD term averages the sample-level KD losses:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{KD}
=
\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\bar{\mathcal{L}}_{KD}^{(b)}.
\label{eq:batch-kd}
\end{equation}
This scalar term supplies teacher supervision to the final objective. It stays
compatible with heterogeneous teachers because each per-teacher loss uses
trie-Wasserstein matching, which is tokenizer-agnostic, instead of direct
token-index KL divergence. The role of each term in
Equation~\eqref{eq:final-objective} follows from the dependency chain
introduced in Section~\ref{sec:method-overview-formulation}:
\(\mathcal{L}_{CE}\) is the compass that fixes the learning direction,
\(\mathcal{L}_{KD}\) provides the teacher map, routing chooses which map to
consult, and gradient agreement checks whether the map currently points in the
compass direction.

\textbf{Final objective.}
The complete \methodname{} objective is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}
=
\mathcal{L}_{CE}
+
\alpha_{\mathrm{KD}}\mathcal{L}_{KD}
+
\lambda_{\mathrm{ent}}\mathcal{L}_{ent}
+
\lambda_{z}\mathcal{L}_{z},
\label{eq:final-objective}
\end{equation}
In this objective, \(\mathcal{L}_{CE}\) anchors the student to supervised answer tokens,
\(\mathcal{L}_{KD}\) transfers routed teacher supervision and
\(\mathcal{L}_{ent}\) together with \(\mathcal{L}_{z}\) regularize the teacher
routing network. The coefficient \(\alpha_{\mathrm{KD}}\) controls the strength
of teacher supervision, while \(\lambda_{\mathrm{ent}}\) and \(\lambda_z\)
control the auxiliary router losses. Only the student parameters \(\theta\) and
router parameters \(\phi\) are optimized, while all teacher parameters remain
frozen.
Together, the method can be read as a three-layer defense:
trie-Wasserstein defends against tokenizer mismatch, sparse routing defends
against irrelevant teachers, and gradient-agreement refinement defends against
selected teachers whose gradients conflict with supervised learning. The result
is a training update that is tokenizer-aware, sample-specialized and
objective-compatible.
