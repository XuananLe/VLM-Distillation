\chapter{Background and Related Work}
\label{chap:preliminaries}

This chapter introduces the background concepts and related work used
throughout the thesis. It first covers the building blocks of VLMs and
knowledge distillation, then turns to the cross-tokenizer, routing and
multi-teacher challenges. The final section connects these threads into the
research gap addressed by this thesis.

\section{Vision-Language Models}

\subsection{Architecture Families}

Modern vision-language models often combine a vision encoder with a language
model. On the visual side, most systems use a Vision Transformer pretrained
with a contrastive objective, commonly CLIP
\cite{radford2021learningtransferablevisualmodels} or SigLIP
\cite{zhai2023sigmoidlosslanguageimage}, which converts an image into a
sequence of patch embeddings that capture both local and global visual
features. A connector module then fuses these visual tokens with text, using
cross-attention, a Q-Former or a learned projector
\cite{alayrac2022flamingovisuallanguagemodel,li2023blip2bootstrappinglanguageimagepretraining,liu2023visualinstructiontuning}. This design separates visual perception from language generation: the vision
encoder produces a compact sequence of image features, while the language model
provides the autoregressive prior used to answer questions. Different VLM families mainly differ in where and how fusion happens, as shown
in Figure~\ref{fig:prelim-vlm-families}. Dual-encoder models align image and
text embeddings contrastively, cross-attention models let language tokens
attend to visual features directly and projector-based models map visual
embeddings into the language model hidden space and then decode with a standard
causal language model. This thesis focuses on the projector-based generative
design, because distillation is applied to output token distributions rather
than retrieval embeddings.

\subsection{Autoregressive Answer Generation}

The distillation setting in this thesis assumes a generative VLM student that
produces answer tokens autoregressively. For language modeling, the
Transformer maps each hidden state \(\mathbf{h}_j\) to next-token logits and
probabilities \cite{vaswani2017attentionneed}:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{z}_j=\mathbf{W}_{o}\mathbf{h}_j+\mathbf{b}_{o},
\qquad
p_j(v)=\mathrm{softmax}(\mathbf{z}_j)_v
=
\frac{\exp(z_{j,v})}{\sum_{u\in\mathcal{V}}\exp(z_{j,u})}.
\end{equation}
The full VLM can be abstracted as a mapping from multimodal input to output
logits:
\[
\mathbf{z} = f_{\theta}(x),
\]
where \(x\) is a multimodal input and \(\mathbf{z}\) denotes the output logits
used for autoregressive answer generation.
For an answer sequence \(y=(y_1,\ldots,y_L)\), the model factorizes the
conditional probability as
\begin{equation}
p_{\theta}(y\mid x)
=
\prod_{j=1}^{L}p_{\theta}(y_j\mid x,y_{<j}).
\end{equation}
Token-level distillation can then compare teacher and student distributions,
such as \(p_{t,j}\) and \(p_{s,j}\), on the answer positions used for
supervision
\cite{vaswani2017attentionneed,hinton2015distillingknowledgeneuralnetwork}.
The loss is therefore naturally token-wise. At each answer position, the
student distribution can be compared with either a hard label or a teacher
distribution. This is important for VQA because short answers such as numbers,
names and document fields may differ by only one or two tokens, so supervision
must preserve position-level information, not only sequence-level
accuracy after decoding the full answer.

\section{Knowledge Distillation}

\subsection{Teacher-Student Objective}

Knowledge distillation transfers information from a stronger teacher model to a
student model \cite{hinton2015distillingknowledgeneuralnetwork,xu2024surveyknowledgedistillationlarge}.
Let \(\mathcal{L}_{CE}\) denote the supervised cross-entropy loss of the student.
A generic distillation objective takes the form
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}
=
\lambda_{CE}\mathcal{L}_{CE}
+
\lambda_{KD}\mathcal{L}_{KD},
\end{equation}
where \(\mathcal{L}_{KD}\) is a teacher-guided loss. In token-level distillation,
the teacher and student are compared on their output distributions, not only on
hard labels. The student therefore learns richer distributional structure, the
relative probabilities over incorrect classes often called \textit{dark
knowledge}, that hard labels discard
\cite{hinton2015distillingknowledgeneuralnetwork}.
For a single answer position, a common same-vocabulary form is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{KD}
=
\tau^2
\operatorname{KL}\!\left(
\mathbf{p}_t^{(\tau)}
\middle\|
\mathbf{p}_s^{(\tau)}
\right),
\end{equation}
where \(\tau\) is the distillation temperature. The cross-entropy term anchors
the student to the ground-truth answer, while the KD term transfers the
teacher's relative preference over alternative tokens. In the heterogeneous
setting of this thesis, this template remains useful, but the comparison term
must be changed because teachers and students may not share the same tokenizer.

\subsection{Temperature Scaling and Soft Targets}

Let \(\mathbf{z}_s\) and \(\mathbf{z}_t\) denote student and teacher logits.
With temperatures \(\tau_s\) and \(\tau_t\), the corresponding distributions are
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{p}_s = \mathrm{softmax}\!\left(\frac{\mathbf{z}_s}{\tau_s}\right),
\qquad
\mathbf{p}_t = \mathrm{softmax}\!\left(\frac{\mathbf{z}_t}{\tau_t}\right).
\end{equation}

Many classical KD methods compare \(\mathbf{p}_s\) and \(\mathbf{p}_t\) using
divergences such as forward KL or Jensen-Shannon divergence. However, such
approaches usually assume that teacher and student share the same vocabulary
\cite{hinton2015distillingknowledgeneuralnetwork,lin1991jensen,boizard2024towardscrosstokenizerdistillation}.
The temperature controls how much probability mass is exposed beyond the top
answer token. When \(\tau>1\), the softmax distribution becomes flatter and
low-rank alternatives receive non-negligible probability. When \(\tau<1\), the
distribution becomes sharper and approaches hard-label supervision. This matters
because the teacher may encode useful semantic alternatives in the tail of the
distribution, for example related words, number formats or subword variants.
The same softening idea is used later before applying tokenizer-independent
distribution matching.
In practice, the student and teacher temperatures may be chosen separately.
Using \(\tau_t\) for the teacher controls how much dark knowledge is exposed,
whereas \(\tau_s\) controls the smoothness of the student distribution being
matched. For this reason, the temperatures are treated as part of the
distillation interface throughout this thesis, not merely numerical constants
inside the softmax.

\subsection{Answer-Token Supervision}

For multimodal question answering, distillation should focus on
\textit{answer tokens}, not \textit{prompt tokens}~\cite{liu2023visualinstructiontuning}.
The KD loss is therefore applied only on supervised answer positions after
masking ignored tokens and truncating student and teacher answer spans to a
common length. The result is a token-aligned distillation signal that remains
compatible with different teacher and student tokenizers.
Let \(\Omega_b^{CE}\) denote the non-ignored student answer positions for
sample \(b\), so the supervised loss is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{CE}^{(b)}
=
\frac{1}{|\Omega_b^{CE}|}
\sum_{j\in\Omega_b^{CE}}
-\log p_s(y_j\mid x_b,y_{<j}).
\end{equation}
The same masking principle is used for KD positions. This prevents the
distillation objective from spending capacity on prompt reconstruction, padding
tokens or formatting artifacts and concentrates the teacher signal on the
answer span that is scored by the evaluation benchmarks.
For multiple teachers, the KD position set can be teacher-specific because
teacher tokenizers and generated answer spans may differ. The training code
therefore constructs \(\Omega_{b,i}^{KD}\) separately for each teacher
\(T_i\), then averages only over valid aligned answer positions for that
teacher.

\subsection{Response-Based vs. Feature-Based Distillation}

Knowledge distillation methods can be grouped by the type of teacher signal
they transfer. Common response-level divergences and feature-level similarity
losses used in knowledge distillation are collected in
Table~\ref{tab:prelim-kd-objectives}
\cite{hinton2015distillingknowledgeneuralnetwork,lin1991jensen}. In the
table, \(p\) and \(q\) denote teacher and student output distributions over
token events \(t\). \(m=\frac{1}{2}(p+q)\) is the JS midpoint distribution.
\(\mathbf{u}=\Phi_T(f_T(x,y))\) and
\(\mathbf{v}=\Phi_S(f_S(x,y))\) are teacher and student feature vectors. The
index \(r\) marks feature dimensions or normalized feature bins.
\textit{Response-based} distillation uses the teacher's output
distribution or generated response as supervision. In its standard form, the
loss compares next-token distributions:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{resp}
=
\frac{1}{|\Omega|}
\sum_{j\in\Omega}
\operatorname{KL}\!\left(
\mathbf{p}_{t,j}^{(\tau)}
\middle\|
\mathbf{p}_{s,j}^{(\tau)}
\right),
\end{equation}
where \(\Omega\) is the set of supervised answer positions. This objective is
closely tied to the student's final prediction layer and therefore provides a
task-facing signal. It is the classical form of KD introduced by Hinton et
al.~\cite{hinton2015distillingknowledgeneuralnetwork} and remains common in
language-model and VLM compression
\cite{xu2024surveyknowledgedistillationlarge,fang2021compressingvlm}.

\textit{Feature-based} distillation instead aligns intermediate
representations. Given student and teacher hidden states
\(\mathbf{H}_s^{(\ell)}\) and \(\mathbf{H}_t^{(m)}\) at selected layers, a
direct matching objective is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{feat}
=
\left\|
\phi_s\!\left(\mathbf{H}_s^{(\ell)}\right)
-
\phi_t\!\left(\mathbf{H}_t^{(m)}\right)
\right\|_2^2,
\end{equation}
where \(\phi_s\) and \(\phi_t\) are projection functions that make the hidden
states comparable. Feature-based methods are useful when output vocabularies
are incompatible, because the comparison can be defined in representation
space instead of vocabulary space
\cite{dasgupta2025improving,kornblith2019similarityrepresentations}. However,
they are less directly connected to answer likelihood and may transfer
teacher-specific internal structure that is not useful for the downstream task.
This distinction motivates treating response-level and representation-level
signals as complementary instead of interchangeable.

\subsection{Limitations of Same-Vocabulary Assumptions}

Same-vocabulary KD assumes that teacher and student probabilities are indexed
by the same token set \(\mathcal{V}\). Under this assumption, the KL term
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{KL}(\mathbf{p}_t\|\mathbf{p}_s)
=
\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}
p_t(v)\log\frac{p_t(v)}{p_s(v)}
\end{equation}
is well defined because token \(v\) has the same meaning in both
distributions. Heterogeneous VLM distillation violates this assumption. A
concrete example illustrates the problem. A teacher may tokenize the string
``invoice'' as one token while the student may split it into several subword or
character-level tokens. The indices of
\(\mathbf{p}_t\) and \(\mathbf{p}_s\) therefore refer to different discrete
events, so a token-index KL divergence compares unrelated bins. This is the
central obstacle addressed by cross-tokenizer and tokenizer-agnostic
distillation methods
\cite{boizard2024towardscrosstokenizerdistillation}.

\section{Tokenizer-Agnostic Distribution Matching}

\subsection{The Vocabulary Mismatch Problem}

Let \(\mathcal{V}_t\) and \(\mathcal{V}_s\) be the teacher and student
vocabularies. In heterogeneous KD, the probability distributions
\(\mu_t\in\Delta^{|\mathcal{V}_t|-1}\) and
\(\mu_s\in\Delta^{|\mathcal{V}_s|-1}\) live on different supports. A direct
same-index comparison is therefore invalid unless a shared alignment map is
available. One possible abstraction is to decode each token into a string and
compare probability mass over decoded strings:
\begin{equation}
\psi_t:\mathcal{V}_t\rightarrow\mathcal{S},
\qquad
\psi_s:\mathcal{V}_s\rightarrow\mathcal{S},
\end{equation}
where \(\mathcal{S}\) denotes a shared space of strings.
After decoding, distribution matching can be defined by how similar two decoded
token strings are, not by whether their vocabulary indices are equal.
This motivates transport-based and string-structured objectives, which replace
same-vocabulary KL with a distance over probability mass on a shared semantic
or lexical support.

\subsection{Wasserstein Distance}

When teacher and student vocabularies differ, standard divergences defined on a
shared output space are not directly applicable. A distribution-matching
framework that encodes the geometry of the output space is optimal transport. Optimal transport compares two probability measures by the minimum cost required
to move mass from one distribution to the other
\cite{peyre2019computationaloptimaltransport}. For probability measures
\(\mu_s\) and \(\mu_t\), the \(p\)-Wasserstein cost can be written as
\begin{equation}
W_p^p(\mu_s,\mu_t)
=
\min_{\gamma \in \Pi(\mu_s,\mu_t)}
\int_{\Omega_s \times \Omega_t}
\|x-y\|^p \, d\gamma(x,y),
\end{equation}
where \(\Pi(\mu_s,\mu_t)\) is the set of couplings whose marginals are
\(\mu_s\) and \(\mu_t\). The coupling \(\gamma\) specifies how probability mass
from the source distribution is transported to the target distribution. For discrete source weights \(\mathbf{a}\), target weights \(\mathbf{b}\) and
transport cost matrix \(\mathbf{C}\), the corresponding optimization problem can
be written as
\begin{equation}
W(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})
=
\min_{\mathbf{P}\in\mathcal{U}(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})}
\langle \mathbf{P}, \mathbf{C} \rangle,
\qquad
\mathcal{U}(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})
=
\left\{
\mathbf{P}\ge 0 \mid
\mathbf{P}\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{a},\
\mathbf{P}^{\top}\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{b}
\right\}.
\end{equation}
Here, \(\mathbf{P}\) specifies the amount of mass moved from each source bin
to each target bin in the transport plan. A key advantage of Wasserstein
distances over pointwise divergences is geometric sensitivity. If two target
distributions move farther away from a source distribution, a pointwise
divergence may saturate once the supports no longer overlap. In contrast,
Wasserstein distances continue to reflect the displacement cost.
The setup in Figure~\ref{fig:wasserstein-geometry} compares a source
distribution with target distributions that shift
progressively farther away. Pointwise divergences can saturate when supports
stop overlapping, while Wasserstein distances continue to reflect displacement
through the transport cost.

\subsection{Tree-Structured Transport}

Dense OT requires a pairwise ground cost between all source and target
bins. In tokenizer-mismatched KD, however, token identity matching is not feasible because
teacher and student vocabularies differ. A \textit{tree metric} provides a
structured alternative: probability mass is placed on a shared hierarchy and
the distance is computed through \textit{subtree-mass imbalance}
\cite{boizard2024towardscrosstokenizerdistillation,peyre2019computationaloptimaltransport}. For \(W_1\) on a tree metric, \textit{tree-Wasserstein} distances make that
idea explicit by placing probability mass on the tree and summing weighted
subtree imbalances
\cite{takezawa2021supervisedtreewasserstein,peyre2019computationaloptimaltransport}.
For a rooted tree \(T=(V,E)\) with edge weights \(w(e)\), one convenient form is
\begin{equation}
W_T(\mu,\nu)
=
\sum_{e\in E}
w(e)\,
\left|
\mu\!\left(\Gamma(e)\right)-\nu\!\left(\Gamma(e)\right)
\right|,
\label{eq:prelim-tree-wasserstein}
\end{equation}
where \(\Gamma(e)\) denotes the subtree below edge \(e\). The key point is that,
on a tree metric, transport decomposes into edgewise subtree-mass imbalance
instead of a dense source-target coupling
\cite{takezawa2021supervisedtreewasserstein,leyamadafukumizucuturi2019treesliced}.
The computation is efficient because the comparison follows the tree hierarchy
directly and avoids materializing a full pairwise transport matrix.
Figures~\ref{fig:prelim-tree-ot} and
\ref{fig:prelim-optimal-transport} show the two commonly used OT views: a
tree-metric view for fast subtree-mass comparisons and a matrix view for
discrete transport plans. Tree-sliced OT extends this idea by averaging Wasserstein distances over
tree metrics and shows that tree metrics preserve the geometric view of OT while
admitting fast closed-form computations
\cite{leyamadafukumizucuturi2019treesliced}.

\section{Representation Matching}

\subsection{Hidden-State Alignment}

Representation matching transfers knowledge by comparing intermediate hidden
states instead of final token distributions. Let
\(\mathbf{H}_s\in\mathbb{R}^{n_s\times d_s}\) and
\(\mathbf{H}_t\in\mathbb{R}^{n_t\times d_t}\) denote student and teacher
hidden-state matrices. Because the sequence lengths and dimensions may differ,
alignment usually requires pooling, projection, interpolation or layer
selection:
\begin{equation}
\tilde{\mathbf{H}}_s = A_s(\mathbf{H}_s),
\qquad
\tilde{\mathbf{H}}_t = A_t(\mathbf{H}_t),
\qquad
\mathcal{L}_{hid}
=
\left\|\tilde{\mathbf{H}}_s-\tilde{\mathbf{H}}_t\right\|_F^2 .
\end{equation}
Here, \(A_s\) and \(A_t\) denote alignment operations that place student and
teacher activations in a comparable space. This family of methods is naturally
appealing for heterogeneous models because it avoids direct vocabulary
comparison and can use information from image tokens, text tokens or fused
multimodal states \cite{dasgupta2025improving}.

\subsection{Centered Kernel Alignment (CKA)}

Centered Kernel Alignment (CKA) compares representations through their
similarity structure instead of through elementwise hidden-state equality
\cite{kornblith2019similarityrepresentations}. Given two activation matrices
\(\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d_x}\) and
\(\mathbf{Y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d_y}\) for the same \(n\) examples, the
linear CKA score can be written as
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{CKA}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})
=
\frac{\|\mathbf{Y}^{\top}\mathbf{X}\|_F^2}
{\|\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X}\|_F\,
\|\mathbf{Y}^{\top}\mathbf{Y}\|_F}.
\end{equation}
Higher CKA values indicate that the two representations induce similar
geometry over the examples. Because CKA is invariant to isotropic scaling and
orthogonal transformations, it is often more stable than direct hidden-state
matching when the teacher and student have different hidden dimensions. In this
thesis, CKA is therefore relevant both as a possible representation-level
distillation signal and as an analysis tool for measuring how closely student
features follow teacher features.

\subsection{Limitations of Representation-Only Matching}

Representation matching does not require a shared tokenizer, but it also does
not directly optimize answer probabilities. A hidden-state objective can make
the student resemble the teacher internally while still leaving the final
answer distribution poorly calibrated. In a multi-teacher setting, the problem
is sharper: two teachers may have useful but incompatible internal
representations and a representation-only objective does not specify which
teacher should dominate for a given input. It also does not detect whether a
teacher's representation loss produces a gradient that agrees with the
supervised answer loss. For these reasons, representation matching is best
viewed as a complementary signal or diagnostic baseline, not a complete
solution to heterogeneous multi-teacher VLM distillation.

\section{Sparse Routing and Expert Selection}
\label{sec:prelim-sparse-routing}

\subsection{Mixture-of-Experts and Top-\texorpdfstring{$k$}{k} Selection}

Recent sparse expert models route each sample or token through only a subset
of available experts, improving efficiency and allowing specialized
computation
\cite{shazeer2017outrageouslylargeneuralnetworks,fedus2022switchtransformersscalingtrillion,deepseekai2024deepseekv2strongeconomicalefficient}. Let \(\mathbf{h}^{(b)}\) denote a pooled representation for sample \(b\).
A router network \(G_{\phi}(\cdot)\) produces gate logits
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{r}^{(b)} = G_{\phi}(\mathbf{h}^{(b)}),
\qquad
\tilde{\mathbf{r}}^{(b)} = \text{AdjustedScores}\!\left(\mathbf{r}^{(b)}\right),
\qquad
\mathbf{p}^{(b)} = \mathrm{softmax}\!\left(\tilde{\mathbf{r}}^{(b)}\right),
\end{equation}
where \(p_i^{(b)}\) is the initial routing weight for expert \(i\). The
adjusted scores \(\tilde{\mathbf{r}}^{(b)}\) may include expert bias, training
noise or temperature scaling before the routing softmax.
\textit{Top-\(k\) routing} retains only the highest-scoring experts and zeros
out all others. \textit{Capacity constraints} prevent one expert from
receiving too many assignments.
If \(\mathcal{S}_b=\operatorname{TopK}(\mathbf{p}^{(b)},k)\) is the selected
expert set, the sparse expert output can be written as
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{y}^{(b)}
=
\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}_b}
\alpha_i^{(b)}E_i(\mathbf{h}^{(b)}),
\qquad
\alpha_i^{(b)}
=
\frac{p_i^{(b)}}{\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S}_b}p_j^{(b)}}.
\end{equation}
Only the selected experts are evaluated, so the activated computation can remain
small even when the total number of expert parameters is large. The same
abstraction is useful for teacher routing: each teacher can be treated as an
expert that contributes a weighted distillation signal for a subset of samples.

\subsection{Load Balancing Objectives}

\textit{Load balancing} prevents routing collapse onto a small subset of
experts. In a sparse MoE layer, collapse is not only a modeling problem but
also a systems problem: the overloaded experts determine the step latency,
while under-used experts receive few gradients and remain poorly specialized
\cite{shazeer2017outrageouslylargeneuralnetworks,lepikhin2020gshard,zhou2022mixtureofexpertswithexpertchoice}.
Let a minibatch contain \(T\) routed items and let \(N\) denote the number of
experts. For item \(b\), the router produces probabilities
\(\mathbf{p}^{(b)}\in\Delta^{N-1}\). A top-\(k\) router induces the binary
assignment mask
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{a}^{(b)}_i
=
\mathbf{1}\!\left\{
i\in \operatorname{TopK}\!\left(\mathbf{p}^{(b)},k\right)
\right\},
\qquad
n_i
=
\sum_{b=1}^{T}\mathbf{a}^{(b)}_i ,
\end{equation}
where \(n_i\) is the number of routed items assigned to expert \(i\). Balanced
routing corresponds to \(n_i\approx kT/N\). When \(n_i\) is much larger than
this value for a few experts, the router has effectively reduced the model to a
small ensemble of busy experts while wasting the capacity of the remaining
ones. The routing statistics used by many auxiliary losses can be written as a
\textit{soft} load and a \textit{hard} load:
\begin{equation}
\bar{p}_i
=
\frac{1}{T}\sum_{b=1}^{T}p_i^{(b)},
\qquad
\bar{q}_i
=
\frac{1}{kT}\sum_{b=1}^{T}\mathbf{a}^{(b)}_i .
\label{eq:prelim-soft-hard-load}
\end{equation}
Here, \(\bar{p}_i\) measures how much probability mass the router gives to
expert \(i\) before hard selection, whereas \(\bar{q}_i\) measures how often the
expert is actually selected after sparse routing. The Switch Transformer
balancing loss uses their scaled dot product
\cite{fedus2022switchtransformersscalingtrillion}:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{bal}
=
\alpha_{bal}N\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bar{p}_i\bar{q}_i .
\label{eq:prelim-switch-load-balance}
\end{equation}
The factor \(N\) keeps the magnitude approximately independent of the number of
experts because the uniform solution gives
\(\sum_i (1/N)(1/N)=1/N\). The hard load \(\bar{q}_i\) is not differentiable
through the top-\(k\) decision, but \(\bar{p}_i\) is differentiable. The loss
therefore gives the router a gradient signal that discourages assigning high
average probability to already busy experts.

Earlier sparsely-gated MoE work used separate penalties for expert
\textit{importance} and expert \textit{load}
\cite{shazeer2017outrageouslylargeneuralnetworks}. If
\(\mathbf{I}\) is the vector of batchwise gate sums and \(\mathbf{L}\) is a
smooth estimate of the number of examples assigned to each expert, the penalty
can be expressed through the squared coefficient of variation:
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{CV}(\mathbf{z})
=
\frac{\sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(z_i-\bar{z})^2}}{\bar{z}},
\qquad
\mathcal{L}_{imp}
=
\lambda_{imp}\operatorname{CV}(\mathbf{I})^2,
\qquad
\mathcal{L}_{load}
=
\lambda_{load}\operatorname{CV}(\mathbf{L})^2 .
\end{equation}
This view makes the objective explicit: a balanced router should have low
dispersion across experts. Later top-\(1\) and top-\(2\) routers often prefer
the simpler dot-product form in Equation~\ref{eq:prelim-switch-load-balance},
combined with a capacity constraint, because it is easy to compute at scale
\cite{lepikhin2020gshard,fedus2022switchtransformersscalingtrillion}.

\subsection{Capacity and Expert-Choice Routing}

Expert capacity is the hard counterpart to the soft balancing objective. With a
capacity factor \(\gamma\), each expert is allowed to process at most
\begin{equation}
C
=
\left\lceil
\gamma\frac{kT}{N}
\right\rceil
\end{equation}
routed items. A larger \(\gamma\) reduces overflow, but it also reserves more
empty expert slots when the actual load is already close to uniform
\cite{fedus2022switchtransformersscalingtrillion}. A smaller \(\gamma\) is more
efficient but makes imbalance costly because over-capacity items must be
dropped, rerouted or passed through a residual path. Thus, load balancing and
capacity are coupled: good balancing allows a small capacity factor without
losing many routed items.

Load balancing can also be enforced by changing the routing direction.
Expert-choice routing lets each expert select a fixed number of items rather
than letting each item independently select experts. If \(c\) is a capacity
factor, each expert receives
\begin{equation}
C_{EC}
=
\left\lfloor
\frac{cT}{N}
\right\rfloor
\end{equation}
items by construction, so the expert load is balanced without an auxiliary
loss. The tradeoff is that the number of experts assigned to each item becomes
variable, which can be useful for heterogeneous inputs but less convenient for
some autoregressive serving regimes
\cite{zhou2022mixtureofexpertswithexpertchoice}. In distributed expert-parallel systems, balancing individual experts may still
be insufficient if experts are unevenly placed across devices. If
\(\mathcal{E}_d\) is the set of experts on device \(d\), device-level loads can
be summarized as
\begin{equation}
\bar{p}'_d=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{E}_d}\bar{p}_i,
\qquad
\bar{q}'_d=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{E}_d}\bar{q}_i,
\qquad
\mathcal{L}_{dev}
=
\alpha_{dev}D\sum_{d=1}^{D}\bar{p}'_d\bar{q}'_d .
\end{equation}
DeepSeek-V2 uses this type of idea beyond expert-level balancing, adding
device-level and communication-level balance terms so that computation and
all-to-all traffic remain better distributed during expert-parallel training
\cite{deepseekai2024deepseekv2strongeconomicalefficient}. For teacher-routing distillation, the same mathematics applies with teachers
playing the role of experts. After top-\(k\) selection, routed teacher weights
can be renormalized as
\begin{equation}
\omega_i^{(b)}
=
\frac{\mathbf{a}^{(b)}_i p_i^{(b)}}
{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathbf{a}^{(b)}_j p_j^{(b)}+\epsilon}.
\end{equation}
Using these routed weights, the objective adds load balancing to the primary
student loss:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}
=
\mathcal{L}_{main}
+\lambda_{bal}\mathcal{L}_{bal}.
\end{equation}
The coefficient \(\lambda_{bal}\) must be chosen carefully. If it is too small,
the router may collapse onto one dominant teacher. If it is too large, it can
force nearly uniform routing and weaken useful specialization. In this thesis,
load balancing is therefore best understood as a stabilizer for sparse teacher
selection, not a replacement for the task or distillation loss.

\subsection{Sample-Adaptive Routing}

Sparse routing is useful because the best expert can depend on the input. In a
generic sample-adaptive router, the selected expert set is a function of a
sample representation:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{S}_b
=
\operatorname{TopK}
\left(
\mathrm{softmax}(G_{\phi}(\mathbf{h}^{(b)})), k
\right).
\end{equation}
The output or loss is then formed only from experts in \(\mathcal{S}_b\). This
differs from fixed ensembling, where every expert contributes to every example
with the same global weight. Sample-adaptive routing is especially relevant
when inputs have different structure. In multimodal QA, one sample may require
OCR and document layout reasoning, another may require chart interpretation
and another may depend mainly on natural image recognition. A router can learn
to give different experts or teachers different responsibility across these
cases.

The mathematical distinction is simple. A global weighting scheme uses one
vector \(\boldsymbol{\pi}\in\Delta^{N-1}\) for the full training set:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}^{(b)}
=
\sum_{i=1}^{N}\pi_i \mathcal{L}_i^{(b)}.
\end{equation}
A sample-adaptive scheme instead uses \(w_i^{(b)}\):
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}^{(b)}
=
\sum_{i=1}^{N}w_i^{(b)} \mathcal{L}_i^{(b)},
\qquad
w_i^{(b)}=0 \ \text{for} \ i\notin\mathcal{S}_b .
\end{equation}
The sparsity constraint limits the number of active experts and makes the
teacher-selection decision interpretable. It also reduces the chance that weak
or irrelevant teachers dominate the update for a sample.

\subsection{Routing in Distillation Settings}

In distillation, experts can be interpreted as frozen teachers instead of
trainable feed-forward modules. The router does not combine hidden activations.
Instead, it combines teacher-guided losses. If \(T_i\) is teacher \(i\), then
teacher \(T_i\) contributes a loss \(\mathcal{L}_{KD}^{(b,i)}\) for sample
\(b\). A routed distillation loss takes the form
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{route}^{(b)}
=
\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}_b}
\omega_i^{(b)}
\mathcal{L}_{KD}^{(b,i)}.
\end{equation}
This view connects MoE selection to multi-teacher KD: teacher routing is a
conditional computation mechanism over supervision signals. Unlike standard
MoE, the experts here are frozen, so only the student and router parameters
receive gradient updates.

Routing in a distillation setting has two additional complications. First,
teacher quality is not a fixed property of the teacher alone. It depends on the
input, the current student state and the task objective. Second, a high router
score does not guarantee that the selected teacher produces a gradient aligned
with supervised learning. This means routing is useful but not a complete
solution to teacher selection by itself.

\section{Multi-Teacher Distillation}

\subsection{Weighted Teacher Aggregation}

Let \(\mathcal{T}=\{T_1,\ldots,T_N\}\) be a pool of frozen teachers. For sample
\(b\), teacher \(T_i\) produces a per-teacher distillation loss
\(\mathcal{L}_{KD}^{(b,i)}\). In principle, one could average these losses uniformly,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{KD}^{(b)}
=
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathcal{L}_{KD}^{(b,i)},
\end{equation}
but this assumes every teacher is equally useful for every sample. In practice, different teachers may provide conflicting guidance because their
predictions, specialized strengths and KD gradients need not agree on the same
training sample. Some teachers may help the supervised objective on a given
sample, while others may push the student in a different direction
\cite{zhu2021studentcustomized}. The sparse-routing framework introduced in
Section~\ref{sec:prelim-sparse-routing} applies directly here by treating
teachers as experts whose usefulness varies across samples
\cite{yuan2020reinforcedmultiteacherselection,zhang2023adaptivemultiteachermetalearning,yu2024selectdistill}.
More generally, an adaptive multi-teacher objective can be written as
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{KD}^{(b)}
=
\sum_{i=1}^{N}w_i^{(b)}\mathcal{L}_{KD}^{(b,i)},
\qquad
w_i^{(b)}\ge0,
\qquad
\sum_{i=1}^{N}w_i^{(b)}=1.
\end{equation}
Uniform averaging is the special case \(w_i^{(b)}=1/N\). A routed method instead
lets the weights depend on the sample, the student state and the current
teacher-selection policy. This is useful when teachers specialize differently:
one teacher may be stronger on documents, another on charts and another on
natural scene text. The main risk is that adaptive weighting can collapse to one
teacher.

\subsection{Fixed vs. Sample-Dependent Weighting}

Existing multi-teacher KD methods differ mainly in how the teacher weights are
chosen. A fixed weighting method uses a global vector
\(\boldsymbol{\pi}\), usually uniform or learned once:
\begin{equation}
w_i^{(b)}=\pi_i,
\qquad
\sum_{i=1}^{N}\pi_i=1 .
\end{equation}
This is simple and stable, but it cannot express that teacher \(T_i\) may be
helpful for chart questions and less helpful for document questions. A
sample-dependent method instead predicts
\(\mathbf{w}^{(b)}=R_{\phi}(x_b)\), where \(R_{\phi}\) is a router or
teacher-selection policy. Reinforced teacher selection and meta-learned
teacher weighting are examples of adaptive multi-teacher KD
\cite{yuan2020reinforcedmultiteacherselection,zhang2023adaptivemultiteachermetalearning,yu2024selectdistill}.

The distinction matters for heterogeneous VLMs because the teachers may come
from different model families. Their strengths can vary by image type, answer
format and reasoning demand. A global average can dilute a strong teacher with
weaker ones on a sample where specialization matters. A sparse
sample-dependent rule keeps only a small teacher set:
\begin{equation}
w_i^{(b)}
=
\frac{\mathbf{1}\{i\in\mathcal{S}_b\}\exp(r_i^{(b)})}
{\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S}_b}\exp(r_j^{(b)})}.
\end{equation}
This keeps aggregation adaptive without dense all-teacher supervision.

\subsection{Gradient Conflict in Multi-Objective Optimization}
\label{subsec:gradient-conflict}

Even after sparse routing selects a teacher subset, the remaining teachers may
still induce KD gradients that disagree with the supervised objective. To see
why, consider the standard combined distillation loss:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{CE} + \alpha\,\mathcal{L}_{KD}.
\label{eq:prelim-combined-loss}
\end{equation}
This objective minimizes a supervised term and a teacher-guided term together.
Because both terms share the same student parameters, the combined update
direction depends on whether the gradients \(\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{CE}\)
and \(\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{KD}\) agree or conflict. When these two
gradient directions are aligned, the combined update reinforces both objectives.
When they oppose each other, the resulting update is a compromise that may
degrade one or both objectives. This is an instance of \textit{multi-objective optimization}: a single
parameter update must serve competing objectives simultaneously
\cite{zhu2021studentcustomized,yu2020gradient}. In multi-teacher distillation,
the problem is compounded because each teacher contributes its own KD gradient.
A routing mechanism can select a small teacher subset, but selection alone
cannot guarantee that the remaining KD gradients support the supervised task
update.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related-work-gap}

A rich body of prior work has studied teacher-student transfer, but the
existing lines address different parts of heterogeneous VLM distillation.
Response-based KD transfers output distributions and has been extended to
multimodal compression
\cite{hinton2015distillingknowledgeneuralnetwork,xu2024surveyknowledgedistillationlarge,fang2021compressingvlm,hu2024visualprogramdistillation,yang2024clipcid},
but it usually assumes a shared tokenizer, a matched output space or a single
teacher. Tokenizer-agnostic and optimal-transport methods relax the
same-vocabulary assumption by comparing probability mass over different
supports
\cite{boizard2024towardscrosstokenizerdistillation,takezawa2021supervisedtreewasserstein,leyamadafukumizucuturi2019treesliced,dasgupta2025improving},
but they are mainly formulated for single-teacher transfer and do not decide
how multiple heterogeneous teachers should be selected or aggregated.
Representation matching provides another tokenizer-independent signal through
hidden states or similarity measures such as CKA
\cite{kornblith2019similarityrepresentations}, but it is indirect with respect
to answer likelihood and does not resolve teacher conflicts. Multi-teacher KD
methods show that several teachers can improve student learning
\cite{yuan2020reinforcedmultiteacherselection,zhang2023adaptivemultiteachermetalearning,yu2024selectdistill},
while sparse expert routing and gradient filtering suggest mechanisms for
sample-dependent selection and conflict suppression
\cite{shazeer2017outrageouslylargeneuralnetworks,fedus2022switchtransformersscalingtrillion,zhou2022mixtureofexpertswithexpertchoice,zhu2021studentcustomized}.
However, existing work does not address tokenizer mismatch, sample-specific
teacher usefulness and gradient conflict as a single problem for heterogeneous
VLM distillation.

Prior work leaves three interrelated gaps. Standard logit-matching objectives
break down when teacher and student models use different tokenizers and output
vocabularies, while transport-based alternatives that preserve token-string
structure remain underexplored for VLMs. Teacher usefulness varies across
samples, so global weighting strategies can dilute useful supervision with
weaker or conflicting signals. Even a sparse routing mechanism cannot guarantee
that selected teachers provide gradients consistent with the supervised task
objective. Addressing these gaps together requires treating tokenizer mismatch,
sample-adaptive teacher selection and gradient conflict as a single
heterogeneous VLM distillation problem.
