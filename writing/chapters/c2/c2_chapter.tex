\chapter{Preliminaries}
\label{chap:preliminaries}

This chapter keeps only the technical preliminaries that Chapter~\ref{chap:proposed-method}
uses directly. It fixes the model notation, the generic distillation template,
cross-tokenizer distribution matching and the sparse-routing concepts behind
GRACE.
The broader motivation, related work and research gap already appear in
Chapter~\ref{chap:introduction}, so they are not repeated here.

\section{Transformer-Based Vision-Language Models}

\subsection{Transformer Basics}

Modern vision-language models are built on the Transformer architecture
\cite{vaswani2023attentionneed}. For an input sequence, self-attention maps query,
key and value matrices \(\mathbf{Q}\), \(\mathbf{K}\) and \(\mathbf{V}\) to
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Attention}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{K},\mathbf{V})
=
\mathrm{softmax}\!\left(\frac{\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^{\top}}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)\mathbf{V},
\end{equation}
where \(d_k\) is the key dimension. Multi-head attention runs this operation
in parallel across several learned subspaces, then applies a feed-forward
network and residual normalization.

For language modeling, the Transformer produces a sequence of hidden states and
next-token logits. Here the student and teacher interact through
their output logits because the distillation method operates at the
token-distribution level \cite{vaswani2023attentionneed,hinton2015distillingknowledgeneuralnetwork}.

\subsection{Vision Encoder and Multimodal Fusion}

On the visual side, many multimodal systems use a Vision Transformer (ViT)
\cite{dosovitskiy2021imageworth16x16words}, which converts an image into a sequence
of patch embeddings and processes them with Transformer blocks. A connector
module then fuses these visual tokens with text, using cross-attention, a Q-Former,
or a learned projector
\cite{alayrac2022flamingovisuallanguagemodel,li2023blip2bootstrappinglanguageimagepretraining,liu2023visualinstructiontuning}.

We focus on compact generative VLMs in the same general family as
SmolVLM and related projector-based models. Conceptually, we can write such a model as
\[
\mathbf{z} = f_{\theta}(x),
\]
where \(x\) is a multimodal input and \(\mathbf{z}\) denotes the output logits
used for autoregressive answer generation.

\section{Knowledge Distillation for Vision-Language Models}

\subsection{Teacher-Student Learning}

Knowledge distillation transfers information from a stronger teacher model to a
smaller student model \cite{hinton2015distillingknowledgeneuralnetwork,xu2024surveyknowledgedistillationlarge}.
Let \(\mathcal{L}_{CE}\) denote the supervised cross-entropy loss of the student.
A generic distillation objective takes the form
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}
=
\lambda_{CE}\mathcal{L}_{CE}
+
\lambda_{KD}\mathcal{L}_{KD},
\end{equation}
where \(\mathcal{L}_{KD}\) is a teacher-guided loss. In later chapters, GRACE
instantiates this template with a routed trie-Wasserstein term plus small router
regularizers.

In token-level distillation, the teacher and student are compared on their output
logits, not only on hard labels. This allows the student to learn richer
distributional structure, often called \textit{dark knowledge}
\cite{hinton2015distillingknowledgeneuralnetwork}.

\subsection{Temperature-Scaled Output Distributions}

Let \(\mathbf{z}_s\) and \(\mathbf{z}_t\) denote student and teacher logits.
With temperatures \(\tau_s\) and \(\tau_t\), the corresponding distributions are
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{p}_s = \mathrm{softmax}\!\left(\frac{\mathbf{z}_s}{\tau_s}\right),
\qquad
\mathbf{p}_t = \mathrm{softmax}\!\left(\frac{\mathbf{z}_t}{\tau_t}\right).
\end{equation}

Many classical KD methods compare \(\mathbf{p}_s\) and \(\mathbf{p}_t\) using
divergences such as forward KL or Jensen-Shannon divergence. However, such
approaches usually assume that teacher and student share the same vocabulary
\cite{hinton2015distillingknowledgeneuralnetwork,lin1991jensen,boizard2024towardscrosstokenizerdistillation}.

\subsection{Answer-Token Distillation}

For multimodal question answering, distillation should focus on
\textit{answer tokens}, not \textit{prompt tokens}~\cite{liu2023visualinstructiontuning}.
The KD loss is therefore applied only on supervised answer positions after
masking ignored tokens, optionally removing EOS and truncating student and
teacher answer spans to a common length.

In Chapter~\ref{chap:proposed-method}, these sets are denoted by
\(\Omega_b^{\mathrm{CE}}\) for supervised CE positions and
\(\Omega_{b,i}^{\mathrm{KD}}\) for teacher-specific KD positions. This gives a
token-aligned distillation signal while remaining compatible with different
teacher and student tokenizers.

\section{Cross-Tokenizer Distribution Matching}

\subsection{ULD as a Prior Baseline}

When the teacher and student vocabularies differ, KD losses defined on a shared
output space are not directly applicable. Universal Logit Distillation (ULD)
is one prior remedy because it avoids direct token-ID matching
\cite{boizard2024towardscrosstokenizerdistillation}.

Given student logits \(\mathbf{z}_s \in \mathbb{R}^{V_s}\) and teacher logits
\(\mathbf{z}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{V_t}\), we first compute their temperature-scaled
probability distributions \(\mathbf{p}_s\) and \(\mathbf{p}_t\). We then sort both
vectors in descending order, pad the smaller vocabulary with zeros and compare
the sorted masses with an \(L_1\) distance:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{ULD}(\mathbf{z}_s,\mathbf{z}_t)
=
\left\|
\pi(\mathbf{p}_s)-\pi(\mathbf{p}_t)
\right\|_1,
\end{equation}
where \(\pi(\cdot)\) denotes sorting in descending order after zero-padding.

The key advantage of ULD is that it compares \textit{ranked probability vectors}
instead of token identities. This makes it directly applicable
to cross-tokenizer teacher-student distillation
\cite{boizard2024towardscrosstokenizerdistillation}. In this thesis, ULD serves
as a cross-tokenizer baseline. However, it also discards relations between
decoded token strings. Two pairs with the same ranked probability shape receive
the same loss even if one pair shares a long prefix and the other does not. This
motivates alignment methods that use a ground geometry over tokens.

\subsection{Wasserstein Distance and Discrete Optimal Transport}

\textit{Rank-based comparison} is limited because it does not encode the
geometry of the output space. If two target distributions move farther away from
a source distribution, a \textit{pointwise divergence} may saturate once the
supports no longer overlap. In contrast, Wasserstein distances continue to
reflect the displacement cost between distributions.

Figure~\ref{fig:wasserstein-geometry} illustrates this behavior. The source
distribution is fixed, while the target distribution shifts progressively
farther away. The Wasserstein curves grow with the displacement, whereas
pointwise divergences such as total variation and squared Euclidean distance can
saturate. This property is important for cross-tokenizer distillation because
teacher and student probabilities may lie on mismatched token supports.

Pointwise divergences can saturate when supports stop overlapping, while Wasserstein distances continue to reflect displacement through the transport cost. Optimal transport compares two probability measures by the minimum cost required
to move mass from one distribution to the other
\cite{peyre2019computationaloptimaltransport}. For probability measures
\(\mu_s\) and \(\mu_t\), the \(p\)-Wasserstein cost can be written as
\begin{equation}
W_p^p(\mu_s,\mu_t)
=
\min_{\gamma \in \Pi(\mu_s,\mu_t)}
\int_{\Omega_s \times \Omega_t}
\|x-y\|^p \, d\gamma(x,y),
\end{equation}
where \(\Pi(\mu_s,\mu_t)\) is the set of couplings whose marginals are
\(\mu_s\) and \(\mu_t\). The coupling \(\gamma\) specifies how probability mass
from the source distribution is transported to the target distribution.

For discrete source weights \(\mathbf{a}\), target weights \(\mathbf{b}\) and
transport cost matrix \(\mathbf{C}\), the corresponding optimization problem can
be written as
\begin{equation}
W(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})
=
\min_{\mathbf{P}\in\mathcal{U}(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})}
\langle \mathbf{P}, \mathbf{C} \rangle,
\qquad
\mathcal{U}(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})
=
\left\{
\mathbf{P}\ge 0 \mid
\mathbf{P}\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{a},\
\mathbf{P}^{\top}\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{b}
\right\}.
\end{equation}
Here, \(\mathbf{P}\) is a transport plan that specifies how much mass is moved
from each source bin to each target bin.

\subsection{Tree-Structured Mass Matching}

Dense OT requires a pairwise ground cost between all source and target
bins. In cross-tokenizer KD, however, token identity is not meaningful because
teacher and student vocabularies differ. A \textit{tree metric} provides a
structured alternative: probability mass is placed on a shared hierarchy and
the distance is computed through \textit{subtree-mass imbalance}
\cite{boizard2024towardscrosstokenizerdistillation,peyre2019computationaloptimaltransport}.

For \(W_1\) on a tree metric, \textit{tree-Wasserstein} distances make that
idea explicit by placing probability mass on the tree and summing weighted
subtree imbalances
\cite{takezawa2021supervisedtreewasserstein,peyre2019computationaloptimaltransport}.
For a rooted tree \(T=(V,E)\) with edge weights \(w(e)\), one convenient form is
\begin{equation}
W_T(\mu,\nu)
=
\sum_{e\in E}
w(e)\,
\left|
\mu\!\left(\Gamma(e)\right)-\nu\!\left(\Gamma(e)\right)
\right|,
\label{eq:prelim-tree-wasserstein}
\end{equation}
where \(\Gamma(e)\) denotes the subtree below edge \(e\). The key point is that,
on a tree metric, transport decomposes into edgewise subtree-mass imbalance
instead of a dense source-target coupling
\cite{takezawa2021supervisedtreewasserstein,leyamadafukumizucuturi2019treesliced}.
This is computationally efficient because the comparison follows the tree
hierarchy directly and is natural when tokens share prefixes or
other hierarchical relations.

Tree-sliced OT extends this idea by averaging Wasserstein distances over
tree metrics and shows that tree metrics preserve the geometric view of OT while
admitting fast closed-form computations
\cite{leyamadafukumizucuturi2019treesliced}. Here the relevant tree
is not an abstract hierarchy but the shared UTF-8 byte trie induced by teacher
and student decoded single-token strings.

That perspective is closer to the trie-Wasserstein loss used later in
Chapter~\ref{chap:proposed-method}: the method maps tokens to paths on a shared byte
trie and compares mass on edges instead of token index.

\section{Multi-Teacher Distillation and Routing}

\subsection{Per-Teacher KD Loss}

Let \(\mathcal{T}=\{T_1,\ldots,T_N\}\) be a pool of frozen teachers. For sample
\(b\), teacher \(T_i\) produces a per-teacher distillation loss
\(\mathcal{L}_{KD}^{(b,i)}\). In principle, one could average these losses uniformly,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{KD}^{(b)}
=
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathcal{L}_{KD}^{(b,i)},
\end{equation}
but this assumes every teacher is equally useful for every sample.

In practice, different teachers may provide conflicting guidance. Some teachers
may help the supervised objective on a given sample, while others may push the
student in a different direction \cite{zhu2021studentcustomized}. This motivates
a selective routing mechanism that decides, for each sample, which teachers
should be active.

\subsection{Learned Routing and Load Balancing}

Recent sparse expert models route each sample or token through only a subset of available experts. This improves efficiency and allows specialized computation \cite{shazeer2017outrageouslylargeneuralnetworks,fedus2022switchtransformersscalingtrillion,deepseekai2024deepseekv2strongeconomicalefficient}.

The same assignment principle applies to multi-teacher distillation. Instead of averaging all teachers equally, a learned router can propose teacher weights from student-side features \cite{yuan2020reinforcedmultiteacherselection,zhang2023adaptivemultiteachermetalearning,yu2024selectdistill}.

Let \(\mathbf{h}^{(b)}\) denote a pooled student representation for sample \(b\).
A router network \(G(\cdot)\) produces gate logits
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{r}^{(b)} = G(\mathbf{h}^{(b)}),
\qquad
\tilde{\mathbf{r}}^{(b)} = \text{AdjustedScores}\!\left(\mathbf{r}^{(b)}\right),
\qquad
\mathbf{p}^{(b)} = \mathrm{softmax}\!\left(\tilde{\mathbf{r}}^{(b)}\right),
\end{equation}
where \(p_i^{(b)}\) is the initial routing weight for teacher \(T_i\). The
adjusted scores \(\tilde{\mathbf{r}}^{(b)}\) may include expert bias, training
noise, or temperature scaling before the routing softmax.

In sparse routing, these weights are typically refined by additional constraints:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textit{Top-\(k\) routing}, which keeps only the highest-scoring teachers.
\item \textit{Capacity constraints}, which prevent one teacher from receiving
too many assignments.
\item \textit{Load balancing}, which discourages routing collapse onto a small
subset of teachers.
\end{enumerate}

Following the general MoE literature, one can summarize the balancing signal by a
soft load \(\bar{p}_i\) and a hard assignment load \(\bar{q}_i\) after sparse
routing, capacity control and fallback and define an
auxiliary balancing objective of the form
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{bal}
=
N\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bar{p}_i \bar{q}_i.
\end{equation}
This auxiliary term does not replace the KD objective; instead, it regularizes
the router so that different teachers remain usable during training.

Chapter~\ref{chap:proposed-method} instantiates this routing principle with a
small student-conditioned MLP gate on top of pooled student hidden states. It
then applies softmax weights, top-\(k\) sparsification and routed-weight
renormalization. The active implementation uses entropy and router \(z\)-loss
terms to regularize the gate, but it does not use capacity filtering or
expert-bias updates.

This makes the routing stage closer to sparse-expert training than to a static teacher average \cite{shazeer2017outrageouslylargeneuralnetworks,fedus2022switchtransformersscalingtrillion,zhou2022mixtureofexpertswithexpertchoice}.

The second view adds specialization. Sparse routing does not only save compute,
it also lets different experts focus on different inputs.

\subsection{Gradient Conflict and Teacher Agreement}
\label{subsec:gradient-conflict-teacher-agreement}

Routing alone does not fully solve multi-teacher conflict. Even after sparse
routing selects a small teacher subset, the remaining teachers may still induce
KD gradients that disagree with the supervised objective.

In multi-objective optimization, different losses may induce different update
directions for the same model parameters. When these directions disagree, the
resulting update may become a compromise that is not equally beneficial for all
objectives. This phenomenon is commonly referred to as \textit{gradient
conflict}.

In multi-teacher distillation, let \(g_{\mathrm{CE}}\) denote the supervised
gradient of the student and let \(g_i^{\mathrm{KD}}\) denote the KD gradient
induced by teacher \(T_i\). A teacher provides useful supervision for a sample
when its KD gradient is broadly aligned with the supervised objective and potentially
harmful when it points in an opposing direction. Prior work also uses gradient
similarity in distillation as a signal for whether teacher supervision helps or
conflicts with the student objective \cite{zhu2021studentcustomized}. A simple
measure of agreement is \textit{cosine similarity}:
\begin{equation}
s_i =
\frac{\langle g_{\mathrm{CE}}, g_i^{\mathrm{KD}} \rangle}
{\|g_{\mathrm{CE}}\|_2 \, \|g_i^{\mathrm{KD}}\|_2 + \epsilon}.
\end{equation}
Positive values indicate agreement, negative values indicate conflict and
values near zero indicate weak or nearly orthogonal alignment.

Figure~\ref{fig:gradient-conflict} illustrates this intuition. Even when two
objectives are individually meaningful, their local update directions can
disagree, so a naive combined update may be suboptimal. This motivates
post-routing refinement: after teacher selection, the final KD weights can be
adjusted according to gradient agreement. Chapter~\ref{chap:proposed-method}
develops this idea into the \methodname{} score-refinement mechanism.
