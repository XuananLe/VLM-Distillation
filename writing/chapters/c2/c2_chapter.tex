\section{Transformer-Based Vision-Language Models}

\subsection{Transformer Basics}

Modern vision-language models are built on the Transformer architecture
\cite{vaswani2023attentionneed}. For an input sequence, self-attention maps query,
key and value matrices \(\mathbf{Q}\), \(\mathbf{K}\) and \(\mathbf{V}\) to
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Attention}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{K},\mathbf{V})
=
\mathrm{softmax}\!\left(\frac{\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^{\top}}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)\mathbf{V},
\end{equation}
where \(d_k\) is the key dimension. Multi-head attention applies this operation
in parallel across several learned subspaces and is followed by a feed-forward
network and residual normalization.

For language modeling, the Transformer produces a sequence of hidden states and
next-token logits. In this thesis, the student and teacher models are trained or
evaluated through their output logits because the distillation method operates at
the token-distribution level.

\subsection{Vision Encoder and Multimodal Fusion}

On the visual side, many multimodal systems use a Vision Transformer (ViT)
\cite{dosovitskiy2021imageworth16x16words}, which converts an image into a sequence
of patch embeddings and processes them with Transformer blocks. These visual tokens
are then fused with text by a connector module such as cross-attention, a Q-Former,
or a learned projector
\cite{alayrac2022flamingovisuallanguagemodel,li2023blip2bootstrappinglanguageimagepretraining,liu2023visualinstructiontuning}.

This thesis focuses on compact generative VLMs in the same general family as
SmolVLM and related projector-based models. Conceptually, such a model can be
written as
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{z} = f_{\theta}(x),
\end{equation}
where \(x\) is a multimodal input and \(\mathbf{z}\) denotes the output logits
used for autoregressive answer generation.

\section{Knowledge Distillation}

\subsection{Teacher-Student Learning}

Knowledge distillation transfers information from a stronger teacher model to a
smaller student model \cite{hinton2015distillingknowledgeneuralnetwork,xu2024surveyknowledgedistillationlarge}.
Let \(\mathcal{L}_{CE}\) denote the supervised cross-entropy loss of the student.
A generic distillation objective is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}
=
\mathcal{L}_{CE}
+
\alpha \mathcal{L}_{KD},
\end{equation}
where \(\mathcal{L}_{KD}\) is a teacher-guided loss and \(\alpha \ge 0\) controls
its weight.

In token-level distillation, the teacher and student are compared on their output
logits rather than only on hard labels. This allows the student to learn richer
distributional structure, often called \emph{dark knowledge}.

    \centering

    \label{fig:prelim-kd-overview}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Temperature-Scaled Output Distributions}

Let \(\mathbf{z}_s\) and \(\mathbf{z}_t\) denote student and teacher logits.
With temperatures \(\tau_s\) and \(\tau_t\), the corresponding distributions are
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{p}_s = \mathrm{softmax}\!\left(\frac{\mathbf{z}_s}{\tau_s}\right),
\qquad
\mathbf{p}_t = \mathrm{softmax}\!\left(\frac{\mathbf{z}_t}{\tau_t}\right).
\end{equation}

Many classical KD methods compare \(\mathbf{p}_s\) and \(\mathbf{p}_t\) using
divergences such as forward KL or Jensen-Shannon divergence. However, such
approaches usually assume that teacher and student share the same vocabulary.

\section{Universal Logit Distillation}

\subsection{Motivation}

In this thesis, the teacher pool may contain different VLM families with different
tokenizers and vocabulary sizes. Therefore, a KD loss that assumes a shared output
space is too restrictive. To address this, we use Universal Logit Distillation
(ULD) \cite{boizard2024towardscrosstokenizerdistillation}.

\subsection{ULD Formulation}

Given student logits \(\mathbf{z}_s \in \mathbb{R}^{V_s}\) and teacher logits
\(\mathbf{z}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{V_t}\), we first compute their temperature-scaled
probability distributions \(\mathbf{p}_s\) and \(\mathbf{p}_t\). We then sort both
vectors in descending order, pad the smaller vocabulary with zeros, and compare
the sorted masses with an \(L_1\) distance:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{ULD}(\mathbf{z}_s,\mathbf{z}_t)
=
\left\|
\pi(\mathbf{p}_s)-\pi(\mathbf{p}_t)
\right\|_1,
\end{equation}
where \(\pi(\cdot)\) denotes sorting in descending order after zero-padding.

The key advantage of ULD is that it compares distributions by their ranked
probability mass rather than by token identity. This makes it directly applicable
to cross-tokenizer teacher-student distillation.

\subsection{Answer-Token Distillation}

For multimodal question answering, distillation should focus on answer tokens rather
than prompt tokens. In our implementation, KD is computed only on supervised answer
positions after masking ignored tokens, optionally removing EOS, and truncating
student and teacher answer spans to a common length. This gives a token-aligned
distillation signal while remaining compatible with different tokenizers.

\section{Multi-Teacher Distillation}

\subsection{Per-Teacher KD Loss}

Let \(\mathcal{T}=\{T_1,\ldots,T_N\}\) be a pool of frozen teachers. For sample
\(b\), teacher \(T_i\) produces a per-teacher distillation loss
\(\mathcal{L}_{KD}^{(b,i)}\). In principle, one could average these losses uniformly,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{KD}^{(b)}
=
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathcal{L}_{KD}^{(b,i)},
\end{equation}
but this assumes every teacher is equally useful for every sample.

\subsection{Need for Selective Routing}

In practice, different teachers may provide conflicting guidance. Some teachers
may help the supervised objective on a given sample, while others may push the
student in a different direction. This motivates a selective routing mechanism
that decides, for each sample, which teachers should be active.

\subsection{Learned Routing and Load Balancing}

Recent sparse expert models route each sample or token through only a subset of
available experts, which improves efficiency and allows specialized computation
\cite{shazeer2017outrageouslylargeneuralnetworks,fedus2022switchtransformersscalingtrillion,deepseekai2024deepseekv2strongeconomicalefficient}.
The same high-level idea is useful in multi-teacher distillation: instead of
averaging all teachers equally, a learned router can propose teacher weights from
student-side features.

Let \(\mathbf{h}^{(b)}\) denote a pooled student representation for sample \(b\).
A router network \(G(\cdot)\) produces gate logits
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{r}^{(b)} = G(\mathbf{h}^{(b)}),
\qquad
\mathbf{p}^{(b)} = \mathrm{softmax}\!\left(\mathbf{r}^{(b)}\right),
\end{equation}
where \(p_i^{(b)}\) is the initial routing weight for teacher \(T_i\).

In sparse routing, these weights are typically refined by additional constraints:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{top-\(k\) routing}, which keeps only the highest-scoring teachers,
\item \textbf{capacity constraints}, which prevent one teacher from receiving too many assignments,
\item \textbf{load balancing}, which discourages routing collapse onto a small subset of teachers.
\end{enumerate}

Following the general MoE literature, one can summarize the balancing signal by a
soft load \(\bar{p}_i\) and a hard assignment load \(\bar{q}_i\), and define an
auxiliary balancing objective of the form
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{bal}
=
N\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bar{p}_i \bar{q}_i.
\end{equation}
This auxiliary term does not replace the KD objective; instead, it regularizes
the router so that different teachers remain usable during training.

\section{Gradient Alignment}

\subsection{Cross-Entropy Gradient Proxy}

For sample \(b\), let \(\mathcal{L}_{CE}^{(b)}\) be the student cross-entropy loss
and let \(\mathbf{z}_s^{(b)}\) be the corresponding student logits. We define the
cross-entropy logit-gradient proxy as
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{g}_{CE}^{(b)}
=
\nabla_{\mathbf{z}_s^{(b)}} \mathcal{L}_{CE}^{(b)}.
\end{equation}

This gradient describes the local update direction preferred by supervised learning.

\subsection{Per-Teacher KD Gradient Proxy}

Similarly, for each teacher \(T_i\), we define a KD logit-gradient proxy
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{g}_{i}^{(b)}
=
\nabla_{\mathbf{z}_s^{(b)}} \mathcal{L}_{KD}^{(b,i)}.
\end{equation}

This gives a teacher-specific candidate update direction in the same logit space
as \(\mathbf{g}_{CE}^{(b)}\).

\subsection{Cosine Alignment}

To measure whether teacher \(T_i\) is locally consistent with the supervised
objective, we compare pooled CE and KD gradient proxies in the same logit space.
Let
\begin{equation}
\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{CE}^{(b)} = \mathrm{Pool}\!\left(\mathbf{g}_{CE}^{(b)}\right),
\qquad
\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{i}^{(b)} = \mathrm{Pool}\!\left(\mathbf{g}_{i}^{(b)}\right),
\end{equation}
where \(\mathrm{Pool}(\cdot)\) averages over supervised answer positions. The
sample-level cosine alignment score is then
\begin{equation}
s_i^{(b)}
=
\cos\!\left(\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{CE}^{(b)}, \tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{i}^{(b)}\right).
\end{equation}

Thus, \(s_i^{(b)}\) is a sample-level agreement score after pooling the
supervised-token gradients. A larger value indicates that teacher \(T_i\)
provides a KD direction more consistent with the supervised objective, while a
negative value indicates directional conflict.

\subsection{Alignment Filtering on Top of Gated Routing}

In the current method, alignment does not replace the learned gate. Instead, the
gate first proposes constrained routing weights \(\hat{p}_i^{(b)}\) after top-\(k\)
selection and capacity control, and alignment is then used as a binary activation
filter:
\begin{equation}
\hat{a}_i^{(b)}
=
\mathbf{1}[s_i^{(b)}>\delta],
\end{equation}
where \(\delta\) is an alignment threshold.

If no teacher remains active after thresholding, the method falls back to the
least-negative aligned teacher among the currently routed teachers,
\begin{equation}
i^\star = \arg\max_i s_i^{(b)},
\qquad
\hat{a}_i^{(b)} = \mathbf{1}[i=i^\star].
\end{equation}
The final effective teacher weights are then
\begin{equation}
\omega_i^{(b)}
=
\frac{\hat{p}_i^{(b)}\hat{a}_i^{(b)}}{\sum_{k=1}^{N}\hat{p}_k^{(b)}\hat{a}_k^{(b)}}.
\end{equation}
This keeps the learned gate responsible for coarse routing and balancing, while
alignment acts as a second-stage filter that suppresses routed teachers whose KD
gradient conflicts with the supervised objective.

\section{Representation Similarity and CKA}

\subsection{Why Compare Hidden Layers?}

When analyzing whether layer-wise distillation is suitable, it is useful to ask
whether a teacher hidden layer and a student hidden layer actually encode similar
representations. A common way to study this question is to compare activation
matrices collected from the same set of inputs and measure their similarity
\cite{kornblith2019similarityrepresentations}.

\subsection{Centered Kernel Alignment}

Let \(\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}\) and
\(\mathbf{Y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times q}\) denote centered activation matrices from
two layers over the same \(n\) examples. In this thesis, we use linear centered
kernel alignment (CKA) as the layer-similarity measure:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{CKA}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})
=
\frac{\left\|\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{Y}\right\|_{F}^{2}}
{\left\|\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X}\right\|_{F}
\left\|\mathbf{Y}^{\top}\mathbf{Y}\right\|_{F}}.
\end{equation}

CKA produces a scalar similarity score between two representations. Larger values
indicate that the two layers encode more similar geometry across the input set.
Compared with earlier similarity measures, CKA is widely used because it is
stable in practical settings and is well suited for comparing learned hidden
representations across layers and across models
\cite{kornblith2019similarityrepresentations}.

\subsection{Connection to Layer-Wise Distillation}

Later in the thesis, we use CKA as an analysis tool rather than as a training
loss. The goal is to examine whether teacher and student layers exhibit clear
one-to-one correspondence. If the cross-model layer similarity is weak or
diffuse, then directly enforcing layer-wise feature matching is unlikely to be a
good inductive bias. This motivates our later conclusion that explicit layer-wise
distillation is not effective for the current multimodal distillation setting.

\section{Summary}

The preliminaries in this chapter establish the concepts needed for the proposed
method:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Transformer-based VLMs produce token logits for multimodal answer generation.
\item Knowledge distillation adds a teacher-guided loss to the supervised CE loss.
\item ULD enables output-level distillation even when student and teacher vocabularies differ.
\item Multi-teacher distillation benefits from learned routing, sparse activation, and load balancing when teachers are used unevenly.
\item Gradient alignment provides a principled way to filter routed teachers according to whether their KD update agrees with the supervised objective.
\end{enumerate}

These concepts are combined in Chapter~\ref{chap:proposed-method} to define the
full selective multi-teacher distillation method used in this thesis.
