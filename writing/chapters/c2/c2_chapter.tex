\chapter{Background and Related Work}
\label{chap:preliminaries}

Three bodies of prior work bear directly on this thesis: knowledge distillation,
tokenizer-agnostic distribution matching and sparse expert routing. The final
section surveys where these lines of work converge and the gap that motivates GRACE.

\section{Vision-Language Models}

Modern vision-language models combine a vision encoder with a language model. On the visual side, many systems use a \textbf{Vision Transformer} pretrained
with an image-text objective, commonly \textbf{CLIP}
\cite{radford2021learningtransferablevisualmodels} or \textbf{SigLIP}
\cite{zhai2023sigmoidlosslanguageimage}, which converts an image into a
sequence of patch embeddings. A connector module then fuses these visual tokens with text, using
cross-attention, a \textbf{Q-Former} or a learned projector
\cite{alayrac2022flamingovisuallanguagemodel,li2023blip2bootstrappinglanguageimagepretraining,liu2023visualinstructiontuning}. This design separates visual encoding from text generation: the vision
encoder produces a compact sequence of image features, while the language model
generates answers token by token. VLM families differ
in where and how vision-language fusion happens, as shown
in Figure~\ref{fig:prelim-vlm-families}. Dual-encoder models align image and
text embeddings contrastively, cross-attention models let language tokens
attend to visual features directly and projector-based models project visual embeddings into the language model's
input space and decode normally. This thesis focuses on projector-based generative VLMs because their
autoregressive output distributions are what GRACE distills. Projected image
embeddings are inserted as visual tokens, so answer generation is standard
next-token prediction over combined visual and text context.

Most released projector-based VLMs are instruction-tuned on visual
question-answering data after pretraining, making them capable of producing
short, grounded answers before any distillation is applied
\cite{liu2023visualinstructiontuning,marafioti2025smolvlm}. All student and
teacher checkpoints used in this thesis are instruction-tuned variants of
their respective base models.

The student VLM produces answer tokens autoregressively. The Transformer maps
each hidden state \(\mathbf{h}_j\) to next-token logits and probabilities
\cite{vaswani2017attentionneed}:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{z}_j=\mathbf{W}_{o}\mathbf{h}_j+\mathbf{b}_{o},
\qquad
p_j(v)=\mathrm{softmax}(\mathbf{z}_j)_v
=
\frac{\exp(z_{j,v})}{\sum_{u\in\mathcal{V}}\exp(z_{j,u})}.
\end{equation}
For an answer sequence \(y=(y_1,\ldots,y_L)\) with multimodal input \(x\), the model factorizes the
conditional probability as
\begin{equation}
p_{\theta}(y\mid x)
=
\prod_{j=1}^{L}p_{\theta}(y_j\mid x,y_{<j}).
\end{equation}
Token-level distillation compares teacher and student answer-token
distributions, \(p_{t,j}\) and \(p_{s,j}\), at each supervised position
\cite{vaswani2017attentionneed,hinton2015distillingknowledgeneuralnetwork}. In VQA, short answers such as numbers, names and document fields can differ by a single
token, so each position carries real weight.

\section{Knowledge Distillation}

Knowledge distillation trains a compact student to match the outputs of a
larger teacher
\cite{hinton2015distillingknowledgeneuralnetwork,xu2024surveyknowledgedistillationlarge}.
Let \(\mathcal{L}_{CE}\) denote the supervised cross-entropy loss of the student.
The combined objective is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}
=
\lambda_{CE}\mathcal{L}_{CE}
+
\lambda_{KD}\mathcal{L}_{KD},
\end{equation}
where \(\mathcal{L}_{KD}\) is a teacher-guided loss. The student thereby learns
the teacher's relative probabilities over alternative answers, often called
\textbf{dark knowledge}, which hard labels discard
\cite{hinton2015distillingknowledgeneuralnetwork}.
A common same-vocabulary KD loss is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{KD}
=
\tau^2
\operatorname{KL}\!\left(
\mathbf{p}_t^{(\tau)}
\middle\|
\mathbf{p}_s^{(\tau)}
\right),
\end{equation}
where \(\tau\) is the \textit{distillation temperature}. The cross-entropy term anchors
the student to the ground-truth answer, while the KD term transfers the
teacher's relative preference over alternative tokens. In the heterogeneous setting, the KD term must be redefined to handle
mismatched tokenizers.

Let \(\mathbf{z}_s\) and \(\mathbf{z}_t\) denote student and teacher logits.
With temperatures \(\tau_s\) and \(\tau_t\), the softened distributions are
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{p}_s = \mathrm{softmax}\!\left(\frac{\mathbf{z}_s}{\tau_s}\right),
\qquad
\mathbf{p}_t = \mathrm{softmax}\!\left(\frac{\mathbf{z}_t}{\tau_t}\right).
\end{equation}

Classical KD divergences, including forward KL and Jensen-Shannon, assume that
teacher and student share the same vocabulary
\cite{hinton2015distillingknowledgeneuralnetwork,lin1991jensen,boizard2024towardscrosstokenizerdistillation}.
The temperature controls probability mass beyond the top answer token: higher \(\tau\) flattens the distribution and spreads more mass to
lower-ranked alternatives, while lower \(\tau\) sharpens it toward hard-label
supervision. The teacher's tail often encodes semantic alternatives such as
related words, number formats or subword variants.

For multimodal question answering, distillation is restricted to the
supervised answer positions, the span evaluated at test
time~\cite{liu2023visualinstructiontuning}.
The KD loss therefore masks ignored tokens and truncates student and teacher
answer spans to a common length.
Let \(\Omega_b^{CE}\) denote the non-ignored student answer positions for
sample \(b\), so the supervised loss is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{CE}^{(b)}
=
\frac{1}{|\Omega_b^{CE}|}
\sum_{j\in\Omega_b^{CE}}
-\log p_s(y_j\mid x_b,y_{<j}).
\end{equation}
The same masking applies to KD positions, keeping the teacher signal on the
evaluated answer span and not on prompt reconstruction, padding or formatting.
For multiple teachers, the KD position set can be teacher-specific because
teacher tokenizers and generated answer spans may differ. Per-teacher KD positions \(\Omega_{b,i}^{KD}\) cover only the valid aligned
span for each teacher.

Table~\ref{tab:prelim-kd-objectives} lists common response-level divergences
and feature-level losses
\cite{hinton2015distillingknowledgeneuralnetwork,lin1991jensen}. In the
table, \(p\) and \(q\) denote teacher and student output distributions over
token events \(t\) and \(m=\frac{1}{2}(p+q)\) is the JS midpoint distribution.
The teacher and student feature vectors are
\[
\mathbf{u}=\Phi_T(f_T(x,y)),
\qquad
\mathbf{v}=\Phi_S(f_S(x,y)).
\]
The index \(r\) marks feature dimensions or normalized feature bins.
\textit{Response-based} distillation trains on the teacher's output distribution
at supervised positions, the classical form of
\cite{hinton2015distillingknowledgeneuralnetwork}, still standard in
language-model and VLM compression
\cite{xu2024surveyknowledgedistillationlarge,fang2021compressingvlm}.

\textit{Feature-based} distillation matches intermediate hidden states.
Given student and teacher hidden states \(\mathbf{H}_s^{(\ell)}\) and
\(\mathbf{H}_t^{(m)}\) at selected layers, a matching objective is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{feat}
=
\left\|
\phi_s\!\left(\mathbf{H}_s^{(\ell)}\right)
-
\phi_t\!\left(\mathbf{H}_t^{(m)}\right)
\right\|_2^2,
\end{equation}
where \(\phi_s\) and \(\phi_t\) are projection functions that make the hidden
states comparable. Feature-based methods sidestep the vocabulary problem: the comparison
operates on hidden states, not token indices
\cite{dasgupta2025improving,kornblith2019similarityrepresentations}. But they do not supervise answer tokens directly and can transfer
architecture-specific representations, so neither signal is uniformly better
\cite{klabunde2023similarity,dasgupta2025improving}.

\section{Tokenizer-Agnostic Distribution Matching}

Same-vocabulary KD assumes that teacher and student probabilities are indexed
by the same token set. Heterogeneous VLM distillation breaks this assumption because a teacher
may tokenize the string ``invoice'' as one token while the student splits it
into several subword pieces, so directly applying KL over their respective
vocabularies then compares unrelated indices and is invalid \cite{boizard2024towardscrosstokenizerdistillation}. Comparing probability mass over decoded strings, rather than vocabulary
indices, avoids the index-alignment problem. Let
\(\mathcal{V}_t\) and \(\mathcal{V}_s\) be the teacher and student
vocabularies with decoding maps into a shared string space \(\mathcal{S}\):
\[
\psi_t:\mathcal{V}_t\rightarrow\mathcal{S},
\qquad
\psi_s:\mathcal{V}_s\rightarrow\mathcal{S}.
\]
\textit{Transport-based} objectives replace same-vocabulary KL with a distance over
probability mass on a shared string support.

\textbf{Optimal transport} compares two probability measures by the minimum
cost required to move mass from one to the other
\cite{peyre2019computationaloptimaltransport}. For probability measures
\(\mu_s\) and \(\mu_t\), the \(p\)-Wasserstein cost is
\begin{equation}
W_p^p(\mu_s,\mu_t)
=
\min_{\gamma \in \Pi(\mu_s,\mu_t)}
\int_{\Omega_s \times \Omega_t}
\|x-y\|^p \, d\gamma(x,y),
\end{equation}
where \(\Pi(\mu_s,\mu_t)\) is the set of joint couplings with marginals
\(\mu_s\) and \(\mu_t\). For discrete weights \(\mathbf{a}\), \(\mathbf{b}\)
and cost matrix \(\mathbf{C}\), this becomes
\begin{equation}
W(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})
=
\min_{\mathbf{P}\in\mathcal{U}(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})}
\langle \mathbf{P}, \mathbf{C} \rangle,
\qquad
\mathcal{U}(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})
=
\left\{
\mathbf{P}\ge 0 \mid
\mathbf{P}\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{a},\
\mathbf{P}^{\top}\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{b}
\right\}.
\end{equation}
Unlike pointwise divergences, Wasserstein distances reflect displacement even
when supports are disjoint, as Figure~\ref{fig:wasserstein-geometry}
illustrates.

Figure~\ref{fig:wasserstein-geometry} makes this geometric property concrete.
The left panels show a fixed source distribution (red bell curve) and a
sequence of target distributions (blue bell curves) that slide progressively
further away. The right panel plots four distance measures as the shift
increases. The \(\ell_1\) (total variation) and \(\ell_2\) (squared
Euclidean) distances both \textbf{saturate} once the two distributions no longer
overlap, providing no gradient signal about how far apart they are. The
Wasserstein distances \(W_1\) and \(W_2^2\), by contrast, grow monotonically
with the displacement, reflecting the true cost of moving mass from one
location to the other. This is precisely the property needed for
cross-tokenizer distillation: teacher and student distributions rarely share
support, so a loss that saturates when supports are disjoint carries no
useful training signal.

For tokenizer-agnostic matching, dense OT is costly because it requires a pairwise
ground cost between every source and target bin. A \textit{tree metric} places tokens on a shared hierarchy instead and the
distance reduces to weighted \textit{subtree-mass imbalances}
\cite{takezawa2021supervisedtreewasserstein,peyre2019computationaloptimaltransport}.
For a rooted tree \(T=(V,E)\) with edge weights \(w(e)\), the distance is
\begin{equation}
W_T(\mu,\nu)
=
\sum_{e\in E}
w(e)\,
\left|
\mu\!\left(\Gamma(e)\right)-\nu\!\left(\Gamma(e)\right)
\right|,
\label{eq:prelim-tree-wasserstein}
\end{equation}
where \(\Gamma(e)\) denotes the subtree below edge \(e\). On a tree metric, transport reduces to per-edge subtree-mass imbalances,
avoiding the dense source-target coupling of standard OT
\cite{takezawa2021supervisedtreewasserstein,leyamadafukumizucuturi2019treesliced}.
Figures~\ref{fig:prelim-tree-ot} and
\ref{fig:prelim-optimal-transport} compare the tree-metric and matrix-coupling
views of OT. Averaging over random tree metrics preserves OT geometry while
admitting fast closed-form computation \cite{leyamadafukumizucuturi2019treesliced}.

Representation-level alignment avoids the vocabulary problem by matching
intermediate hidden states. Because sequence lengths and hidden dimensions
differ across architectures, alignment requires pooling or projection before
comparison. The resulting Frobenius-norm loss operates without any
vocabulary assumption \cite{dasgupta2025improving}.

\textbf{Centered Kernel Alignment} (CKA) compares representations through
their similarity structure and avoids direct elementwise hidden-state matching
\cite{kornblith2019similarityrepresentations}. Given activation matrices
\(\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d_x}\) and
\(\mathbf{Y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d_y}\) over the same \(n\) examples, the
linear CKA score is
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{CKA}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})
=
\frac{\|\mathbf{Y}^{\top}\mathbf{X}\|_F^2}
{\|\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X}\|_F\,
\|\mathbf{Y}^{\top}\mathbf{Y}\|_F}.
\end{equation}
Higher CKA values mean the two representations share the same pairwise
similarity structure across the evaluated samples. Because CKA is invariant to
isotropic scaling and orthogonal transformations, it remains valid when teacher
and student have different hidden dimensions, unlike direct hidden-state matching. CKA serves here both as a distillation signal and as a diagnostic for
feature alignment across heterogeneous architectures
\cite{kornblith2019similarityrepresentations,klabunde2023similarity}.


\section{Sparse Routing and Expert Selection}
\label{sec:prelim-sparse-routing}

Recent sparse expert models route each sample or token through only a subset of
available experts, activating only a fraction of parameters per input
\cite{shazeer2017outrageouslylargeneuralnetworks,fedus2022switchtransformersscalingtrillion,deepseekai2024deepseekv2strongeconomicalefficient}. Let \(\mathbf{h}^{(b)}\) denote a pooled representation for sample \(b\).
A router network \(G_{\phi}(\cdot)\) produces gate logits
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{r}^{(b)} = G_{\phi}(\mathbf{h}^{(b)}),
\qquad
\tilde{\mathbf{r}}^{(b)} = \text{AdjustedScores}\!\left(\mathbf{r}^{(b)}\right),
\qquad
\mathbf{p}^{(b)} = \mathrm{softmax}\!\left(\tilde{\mathbf{r}}^{(b)}\right),
\end{equation}
where \(p_i^{(b)}\) is the initial routing weight for expert \(i\). The
adjusted scores \(\tilde{\mathbf{r}}^{(b)}\) may include expert bias, training
noise or temperature scaling before the routing softmax.
\textbf{Top-\(k\) routing} retains only the highest-scoring experts and zeros
out all others, while \textit{capacity constraints} prevent any single expert
from receiving too many assignments.
The selected expert set is
\[
\mathcal{S}_b=\operatorname{TopK}(\mathbf{p}^{(b)},k).
\]
The output is a weighted sum over selected experts:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{y}^{(b)}
=
\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}_b}
\alpha_i^{(b)}E_i(\mathbf{h}^{(b)}),
\qquad
\alpha_i^{(b)}
=
\frac{p_i^{(b)}}{\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S}_b}p_j^{(b)}}.
\end{equation}
Only the selected experts are evaluated, keeping active computation small even
when the total expert count is large. Frozen teachers fit directly into this framework: each one contributes a
weighted distillation loss for the samples routed to it.

Without regularization, sparse routing collapses onto a small subset of
experts. In a sparse \textbf{Mixture of Experts} (MoE) layer, overloaded experts
become the latency bottleneck while under-used ones receive few gradient
updates and fail to specialize
\cite{shazeer2017outrageouslylargeneuralnetworks,lepikhin2020gshard,zhou2022mixtureofexpertswithexpertchoice}.
Let a mini-batch contain \(T\) routed items and let \(N\) denote the number of
experts. For item \(b\), the router produces probabilities
\(\mathbf{p}^{(b)}\in\Delta^{N-1}\). A top-\(k\) router induces the binary
assignment mask
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{a}^{(b)}_i
=
\mathbf{1}\!\left\{
i\in \operatorname{TopK}\!\left(\mathbf{p}^{(b)},k\right)
\right\},
\qquad
n_i
=
\sum_{b=1}^{T}\mathbf{a}^{(b)}_i ,
\end{equation}
where \(n_i\) is the number of routed items assigned to expert \(i\). Balanced
routing corresponds to
\[
n_i\approx \frac{kT}{N}.
\]
When \(n_i\) is much larger than this value for a few experts, the router has
collapsed to a small ensemble of busy experts, leaving the rest idle. Auxiliary load-balancing losses track two quantities, \textit{soft} load and
\textit{hard} load:
\begin{equation}
\bar{p}_i
=
\frac{1}{T}\sum_{b=1}^{T}p_i^{(b)},
\qquad
\bar{q}_i
=
\frac{1}{kT}\sum_{b=1}^{T}\mathbf{a}^{(b)}_i .
\label{eq:prelim-soft-hard-load}
\end{equation}
\(\bar{p}_i\) is the average routing weight before hard selection, while
\(\bar{q}_i\) is the fraction of items the expert actually receives after
top-\(k\) filtering. The \textbf{Switch Transformer}
balancing loss uses their scaled dot product
\cite{fedus2022switchtransformersscalingtrillion}:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{bal}
=
\alpha_{bal}N\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bar{p}_i\bar{q}_i .
\label{eq:prelim-switch-load-balance}
\end{equation}
The factor \(N\) normalises for the number of experts. Since \(\bar{q}_i\) is
non-differentiable through the top-\(k\) decision, the loss penalizes
concentrating probability on already-loaded experts through the differentiable
\(\bar{p}_i\) term. Entropy and \(z\)-loss terms stabilize sparse selection without dominating
the task and distillation objectives.

Sparse routing is most effective when expert usefulness varies by input
\cite{shazeer2017outrageouslylargeneuralnetworks,fedus2022switchtransformersscalingtrillion}.
In a generic \textit{sample-adaptive} router, the selected expert set is a function of a
sample representation:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{S}_b
=
\operatorname{TopK}
\left(
\mathrm{softmax}(G_{\phi}(\mathbf{h}^{(b)})), k
\right).
\end{equation}
The loss is then computed only from experts in \(\mathcal{S}_b\). This
differs from \textit{fixed ensembling}, where every expert contributes to every example
with the same global weight. In multimodal QA, one sample may require OCR and document layout reasoning,
another chart interpretation and a third natural image recognition.

In distillation, frozen teachers replace MoE experts: the router selects which
ones supervise each sample and the routed distillation loss sums only over the
selected set:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{route}^{(b)}
=
\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}_b}
\omega_i^{(b)}
\mathcal{L}_{KD}^{(b,i)}.
\end{equation}
Since teachers are frozen, only the student and router parameters receive
gradient updates. Routing by relevance does not guarantee gradient alignment:
a highly-scored teacher can still push against the CE objective, so
gradient-aware reweighting after routing is needed to filter misaligned signals
\cite{zhu2021studentcustomized,yuan2020reinforcedmultiteacherselection,yu2020gradient}.

\section{Multi-Teacher Distillation}

Let \(\mathcal{T}=\{T_1,\ldots,T_N\}\) be the teacher pool and
\(\mathcal{L}_{KD}^{(b,i)}\) the distillation loss from teacher \(T_i\) on
sample \(b\). The simplest aggregation rule is uniform averaging:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{KD}^{(b)}
=
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathcal{L}_{KD}^{(b,i)}.
\end{equation}
Uniform averaging isolates teacher diversity from any learned selection, but
equal influence is the wrong prior when teacher strengths vary across document
images, charts and natural scene text.

Heterogeneous pools differ across multiple dimensions: tokenizer, language backbone, vision encoder,
pretraining data and answer calibration. One model may provide stronger supervision for OCR-heavy samples while another
is better calibrated on charts. But teacher distributions can also disagree on
the same answer position when their tokenizers split the answer string
differently. A multi-teacher method must therefore \textbf{select and weight teachers,
not just aggregate them}.

Weighted aggregation generalizes this:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{KD}^{(b)}
=
\sum_{i=1}^{N}w_i^{(b)}\mathcal{L}_{KD}^{(b,i)},
\qquad
w_i^{(b)}\ge0,
\qquad
\sum_{i=1}^{N}w_i^{(b)}=1.
\end{equation}
Fixed weights ignore input variation entirely, while sample-dependent weights
allow the best teacher for each input type to dominate.
Section~\ref{sec:related-work-gap} covers prior methods in detail.

Sparse routing implements sample-dependent weighting directly: frozen teachers
act as experts, each contributing a loss only for the samples routed to it. Routing introduces two problems that ensembling avoids: early collapse onto
one teacher leaves the remaining teachers unused and a highly-ranked
teacher can still push the gradient against the supervised CE objective.

This second issue is an \textbf{optimization conflict}, not just a selection
problem. In the standard combined loss
\[
\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{CE} + \alpha\,\mathcal{L}_{KD},
\]
both terms update the same parameters: the combined step is reinforced when
CE and KD gradients agree and partially cancelled when they diverge
\cite{zhu2021studentcustomized,yu2020gradient}. In multi-teacher distillation,
each teacher contributes a separate KD gradient, so selecting a teacher subset does not guarantee the selected gradients align
with the supervised update. GRACE uses a \textbf{two-stage design}: routing proposes
candidate teachers, then a \textbf{post-routing compatibility check} verifies that
their KD gradients align with the supervised CE objective.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related-work-gap}

Four lines of prior work bear directly on GRACE, each addressing part of the
problem without handling all three constraints together.

\textbf{Response-based and vocabulary-mismatched KD.}
Standard response-based KD supervises the student with the teacher's soft
output distributions
\cite{hinton2015distillingknowledgeneuralnetwork,xu2024surveyknowledgedistillationlarge,fang2021compressingvlm},
but KL, reverse KL and Jensen-Shannon divergences all assume a shared
vocabulary. Cross-tokenizer methods relax this by comparing probability mass
over decoded strings: \textbf{ULD}~\cite{boizard2024towardscrosstokenizerdistillation}
computes the L1 distance between rank-sorted probability vectors and
tree-Wasserstein methods define a ground geometry over tokens without
requiring index equality
\cite{takezawa2021supervisedtreewasserstein,leyamadafukumizucuturi2019treesliced},
but both assume a single fixed teacher and provide no per-sample selection
mechanism.

\textbf{Representation matching and CKA alignment.}
Matching hidden states or CKA similarity structure sidesteps the vocabulary
problem entirely and works across architectures with different hidden
dimensions \cite{kornblith2019similarityrepresentations,dasgupta2025improving}.
However, similar representational structure does not imply similar answer
distributions and these methods leave teacher selection unresolved. GRACE uses
CKA as a diagnostic rather than a training signal, since the primary
distillation objective is output-level.

\textbf{Multi-teacher KD, routing and gradient-conflict resolution.}
Reinforcement-based and meta-learning methods learn sample-dependent teacher
weights or selection policies
\cite{yuan2020reinforcedmultiteacherselection,zhang2023adaptivemultiteachermetalearning,yu2024selectdistill},
and sparse routing from mixture-of-experts models selects a teacher subset
per input
\cite{shazeer2017outrageouslylargeneuralnetworks,fedus2022switchtransformersscalingtrillion},
yet neither line addresses tokenizer mismatch. Gradient-direction filters
\cite{zhu2021studentcustomized,yu2020gradient} resolve optimization conflict
between auxiliary and primary losses, but prior multi-teacher methods do not
combine them with cross-tokenizer matching or sparse selection.

The gap is structural: vocabulary-aligned KD breaks across tokenizers,
cross-tokenizer transport assumes a single teacher, representation matching
leaves teacher selection open, routing-based methods do not verify gradient
compatibility. \textbf{GRACE closes all four gaps simultaneously.}
