\chapter{Background and Related Work}
\label{chap:preliminaries}

This chapter introduces the background concepts and related work used
throughout the thesis. It first covers the building blocks of VLMs and
knowledge distillation, then turns to the cross-tokenizer, routing and
multi-teacher challenges. The final section connects these threads into the
research gap addressed by this thesis.

\section{Vision-Language Models}

Modern vision-language models often combine a vision encoder with a language
model. On the visual side, many systems use a Vision Transformer pretrained
with an image-text objective, commonly CLIP
\cite{radford2021learningtransferablevisualmodels} or SigLIP
\cite{zhai2023sigmoidlosslanguageimage}, which converts an image into a
sequence of patch embeddings that capture both local and global visual
features. A connector module then fuses these visual tokens with text, using
cross-attention, a Q-Former or a learned projector
\cite{alayrac2022flamingovisuallanguagemodel,li2023blip2bootstrappinglanguageimagepretraining,liu2023visualinstructiontuning}. This design separates visual perception from language generation: the vision
encoder produces a compact sequence of image features, while the language model
provides the autoregressive prior used to answer questions. A useful way to
distinguish VLM families is by where and how visual-language fusion happens, as shown
in Figure~\ref{fig:prelim-vlm-families}. Dual-encoder models align image and
text embeddings contrastively, cross-attention models let language tokens
attend to visual features directly and projector-based models map visual
embeddings into the language model hidden space and then decode with a standard
causal language model. This thesis focuses on the projector-based generative
design, because distillation targets output token distributions, not retrieval
embeddings.
In this setting, projected image embeddings are inserted into the
language-model context as visual tokens, so answer generation remains ordinary
next-token prediction conditioned on both the image representation and the
textual question.

For the generative setting studied in this thesis, the student VLM produces
answer tokens autoregressively. For language modeling, the
Transformer maps each hidden state \(\mathbf{h}_j\) to next-token logits and
probabilities \cite{vaswani2017attentionneed}:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{z}_j=\mathbf{W}_{o}\mathbf{h}_j+\mathbf{b}_{o},
\qquad
p_j(v)=\mathrm{softmax}(\mathbf{z}_j)_v
=
\frac{\exp(z_{j,v})}{\sum_{u\in\mathcal{V}}\exp(z_{j,u})}.
\end{equation}
At answer position \(j\), the VLM can be abstracted as a mapping from the
multimodal input and previous answer tokens to next-token logits:
\[
\mathbf{z}_j = f_{\theta}(x,y_{<j}),
\qquad
\mathbf{z}_j \in \mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{V}|},
\]
where \(x\) is a multimodal input and \(\mathbf{z}_j\) denotes the logits used
for autoregressive answer generation at position \(j\).
For an answer sequence \(y=(y_1,\ldots,y_L)\), the model factorizes the
conditional probability as
\begin{equation}
p_{\theta}(y\mid x)
=
\prod_{j=1}^{L}p_{\theta}(y_j\mid x,y_{<j}).
\end{equation}
Token-level distillation therefore compares teacher and student answer-token
distributions at supervised positions, such as \(p_{t,j}\) and \(p_{s,j}\)
\cite{vaswani2017attentionneed,hinton2015distillingknowledgeneuralnetwork}. This is important for VQA
because short answers such as numbers, names and document fields may differ by
only one or two tokens, so supervision must preserve position-level information,
not only sequence-level accuracy after decoding the full answer.
This output-centric view motivates the next sections: standard KD compares
teacher and student answer-token distributions directly, while heterogeneous
VLM distillation needs a tokenizer-agnostic matching objective.

\section{Knowledge Distillation}

Knowledge distillation transfers information from a higher-capacity teacher
model to a student model
\cite{hinton2015distillingknowledgeneuralnetwork,xu2024surveyknowledgedistillationlarge}.
Let \(\mathcal{L}_{CE}\) denote the supervised cross-entropy loss of the student.
A generic distillation objective takes the form
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}
=
\lambda_{CE}\mathcal{L}_{CE}
+
\lambda_{KD}\mathcal{L}_{KD},
\end{equation}
where \(\mathcal{L}_{KD}\) is a teacher-guided loss. In token-level distillation,
the teacher and student are compared on their output distributions, not only on
hard labels. The student therefore learns richer distributional structure:
the relative probabilities over alternative classes, often called
\textit{dark knowledge}, which hard labels discard
\cite{hinton2015distillingknowledgeneuralnetwork}.
For a single answer position, a common same-vocabulary form is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{KD}
=
\tau^2
\operatorname{KL}\!\left(
\mathbf{p}_t^{(\tau)}
\middle\|
\mathbf{p}_s^{(\tau)}
\right),
\end{equation}
where \(\tau\) is the distillation temperature. The cross-entropy term anchors
the student to the ground-truth answer, while the KD term transfers the
teacher's relative preference over alternative tokens. In the heterogeneous
setting of this thesis, this template remains useful, but the comparison term
must be changed because teachers and students may not share the same tokenizer.

The output distributions used in KD are often softened before comparison. Let
\(\mathbf{z}_s\) and \(\mathbf{z}_t\) denote student and teacher logits. With
temperatures \(\tau_s\) and \(\tau_t\), the corresponding distributions are
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{p}_s = \mathrm{softmax}\!\left(\frac{\mathbf{z}_s}{\tau_s}\right),
\qquad
\mathbf{p}_t = \mathrm{softmax}\!\left(\frac{\mathbf{z}_t}{\tau_t}\right).
\end{equation}

Many classical KD methods compare \(\mathbf{p}_s\) and \(\mathbf{p}_t\) using
divergences such as forward KL or Jensen-Shannon divergence. However, such
approaches usually assume that teacher and student share the same vocabulary
\cite{hinton2015distillingknowledgeneuralnetwork,lin1991jensen,boizard2024towardscrosstokenizerdistillation}.
The temperature controls how much probability mass is exposed beyond the top
answer token. When \(\tau>1\), the softmax distribution becomes flatter and
low-rank alternatives receive non-negligible probability. When \(\tau<1\), the
distribution becomes sharper and approaches hard-label supervision. This matters
because the teacher may encode useful semantic alternatives in the tail of the
distribution, for example related words, number formats or subword variants.
Section~\ref{sec:trie-wasserstein-distillation} applies the same softening idea
before trie-Wasserstein tokenizer-independent distribution matching. Since
student and teacher temperatures may be chosen independently, \(\tau_t\)
controls how much dark knowledge is exposed from the teacher, whereas
\(\tau_s\) controls the smoothness of the student distribution being matched.
For this reason, the temperatures are treated as part of the distillation
interface throughout this thesis, not merely numerical constants inside the
softmax.

For multimodal question answering, distillation should focus on
\textit{answer tokens}, not \textit{prompt tokens}~\cite{liu2023visualinstructiontuning}.
The KD loss is therefore applied only on supervised answer positions after
masking ignored tokens and truncating student and teacher answer spans to a
common length. The result is a token-aligned distillation signal that remains
compatible with different teacher and student tokenizers.
Let \(\Omega_b^{CE}\) denote the non-ignored student answer positions for
sample \(b\), so the supervised loss is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{CE}^{(b)}
=
\frac{1}{|\Omega_b^{CE}|}
\sum_{j\in\Omega_b^{CE}}
-\log p_s(y_j\mid x_b,y_{<j}).
\end{equation}
The same masking principle is used for KD positions. This prevents the
distillation objective from spending capacity on prompt reconstruction, padding
tokens or formatting artifacts and concentrates the teacher signal on the
answer span that is scored by the evaluation benchmarks.
For multiple teachers, the KD position set can be teacher-specific because
teacher tokenizers and generated answer spans may differ. The training code
therefore constructs \(\Omega_{b,i}^{KD}\) separately for each teacher
\(T_i\), then averages only over valid aligned answer positions for that
teacher.

Knowledge distillation methods can also be grouped by the type of teacher
signal they transfer. Common response-level divergences and feature-level
similarity
losses used in knowledge distillation are collected in
Table~\ref{tab:prelim-kd-objectives}
\cite{hinton2015distillingknowledgeneuralnetwork,lin1991jensen}. In the
table, \(p\) and \(q\) denote teacher and student output distributions over
token events \(t\). \(m=\frac{1}{2}(p+q)\) is the JS midpoint distribution.
\(\mathbf{u}=\Phi_T(f_T(x,y))\) and
\(\mathbf{v}=\Phi_S(f_S(x,y))\) are teacher and student feature vectors. The
index \(r\) marks feature dimensions or normalized feature bins.
\textit{Response-based} distillation uses the teacher's output
distribution or generated response as supervision. In its standard form, the
loss compares next-token distributions:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{resp}
=
\frac{1}{|\Omega|}
\sum_{j\in\Omega}
\operatorname{KL}\!\left(
\mathbf{p}_{t,j}^{(\tau)}
\middle\|
\mathbf{p}_{s,j}^{(\tau)}
\right),
\end{equation}
where \(\Omega\) is the set of supervised answer positions. This objective is
closely tied to the student's final prediction layer and therefore provides a
task-facing signal. It is the classical form of KD introduced by Hinton et
al.~\cite{hinton2015distillingknowledgeneuralnetwork} and remains common in
language-model and VLM compression
\cite{xu2024surveyknowledgedistillationlarge,fang2021compressingvlm}.

\textit{Feature-based} distillation aligns intermediate representations
between student and teacher layers. Given student and teacher hidden states
\(\mathbf{H}_s^{(\ell)}\) and \(\mathbf{H}_t^{(m)}\) at selected layers, a
direct matching objective is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{feat}
=
\left\|
\phi_s\!\left(\mathbf{H}_s^{(\ell)}\right)
-
\phi_t\!\left(\mathbf{H}_t^{(m)}\right)
\right\|_2^2,
\end{equation}
where \(\phi_s\) and \(\phi_t\) are projection functions that make the hidden
states comparable. Feature-based methods are useful when output vocabularies
are incompatible, because the comparison can be defined in representation
space without vocabulary-space matching
\cite{dasgupta2025improving,kornblith2019similarityrepresentations}. However,
they are less directly connected to answer likelihood and may transfer
teacher-specific internal structure that is not useful for the downstream task.
This distinction motivates treating response-level and representation-level
signals as complementary, not interchangeable
\cite{klabunde2023similarity,dasgupta2025improving}.

\section{Tokenizer-Agnostic Distribution Matching}

Same-vocabulary KD assumes that teacher and student probabilities are indexed
by the same token set. Heterogeneous VLM distillation violates this: a teacher
may tokenize the string ``invoice'' as one token while the student splits it
into several subword pieces. Token-index KL then compares unrelated bins and is
invalid \cite{boizard2024towardscrosstokenizerdistillation}. The natural fix is to decode each token into a string and compare probability
mass over decoded strings, not vocabulary indices. Let
\(\mathcal{V}_t\) and \(\mathcal{V}_s\) be the teacher and student
vocabularies with decoding maps \(\psi_t:\mathcal{V}_t\rightarrow\mathcal{S}\)
and \(\psi_s:\mathcal{V}_s\rightarrow\mathcal{S}\) into a shared string space
\(\mathcal{S}\). Distribution matching is then defined over decoded strings,
not vocabulary indices. This motivates transport-based objectives that replace
same-vocabulary KL with a distance over probability mass on a shared lexical
support.

Optimal transport compares two probability measures by the minimum cost required
to move mass from one to the other
\cite{peyre2019computationaloptimaltransport}. For probability measures
\(\mu_s\) and \(\mu_t\), the \(p\)-Wasserstein cost is
\begin{equation}
W_p^p(\mu_s,\mu_t)
=
\min_{\gamma \in \Pi(\mu_s,\mu_t)}
\int_{\Omega_s \times \Omega_t}
\|x-y\|^p \, d\gamma(x,y),
\end{equation}
where \(\Pi(\mu_s,\mu_t)\) is the set of joint couplings with marginals
\(\mu_s\) and \(\mu_t\). For discrete weights \(\mathbf{a}\), \(\mathbf{b}\)
and cost matrix \(\mathbf{C}\), this becomes
\begin{equation}
W(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})
=
\min_{\mathbf{P}\in\mathcal{U}(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})}
\langle \mathbf{P}, \mathbf{C} \rangle,
\qquad
\mathcal{U}(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})
=
\left\{
\mathbf{P}\ge 0 \mid
\mathbf{P}\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{a},\
\mathbf{P}^{\top}\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{b}
\right\}.
\end{equation}
Unlike pointwise divergences, Wasserstein distances reflect displacement even
when supports do not overlap, as Figure~\ref{fig:wasserstein-geometry}
illustrates.

For tokenizer-agnostic matching, dense OT is still costly because it requires a
pairwise ground cost between all source and target bins. A \textit{tree metric}
provides a structured alternative: probability mass is placed on a shared
hierarchy and the distance is the sum of weighted \textit{subtree-mass imbalances}
\cite{takezawa2021supervisedtreewasserstein,peyre2019computationaloptimaltransport}.
For a rooted tree \(T=(V,E)\) with edge weights \(w(e)\), one convenient form is
\begin{equation}
W_T(\mu,\nu)
=
\sum_{e\in E}
w(e)\,
\left|
\mu\!\left(\Gamma(e)\right)-\nu\!\left(\Gamma(e)\right)
\right|,
\label{eq:prelim-tree-wasserstein}
\end{equation}
where \(\Gamma(e)\) denotes the subtree below edge \(e\). The key point is that,
on a tree metric, transport decomposes into edgewise subtree-mass imbalance and
avoids a dense source-target coupling
\cite{takezawa2021supervisedtreewasserstein,leyamadafukumizucuturi2019treesliced}.
The computation is efficient because the comparison follows the tree hierarchy
directly and avoids materializing a full pairwise transport matrix.
Figures~\ref{fig:prelim-tree-ot} and
\ref{fig:prelim-optimal-transport} show the two commonly used OT views: a
tree-metric view for fast subtree-mass comparisons and a matrix view for
discrete transport plans. Tree-sliced OT extends this idea by averaging Wasserstein distances over
tree metrics and shows that tree metrics preserve the geometric view of OT while
admitting fast closed-form computations
\cite{leyamadafukumizucuturi2019treesliced}.

A contrasting family of methods avoids output distributions altogether by
matching intermediate representations. Representation matching transfers
knowledge through hidden-state comparison, not final token distributions. Let
\(\mathbf{H}_s\in\mathbb{R}^{n_s\times d_s}\) and
\(\mathbf{H}_t\in\mathbb{R}^{n_t\times d_t}\) denote student and teacher
hidden-state matrices. Because the sequence lengths and dimensions may differ,
alignment usually requires pooling, projection, interpolation or layer
selection:
\begin{equation}
\tilde{\mathbf{H}}_s = A_s(\mathbf{H}_s),
\qquad
\tilde{\mathbf{H}}_t = A_t(\mathbf{H}_t),
\qquad
\mathcal{L}_{hid}
=
\left\|\tilde{\mathbf{H}}_s-\tilde{\mathbf{H}}_t\right\|_F^2 .
\end{equation}
Here, \(A_s\) and \(A_t\) denote alignment operations that place student and
teacher activations in a comparable space. This family of methods is naturally
appealing for heterogeneous models because it avoids direct vocabulary
comparison and can use information from image tokens, text tokens or fused
multimodal states \cite{dasgupta2025improving}.

Centered Kernel Alignment (CKA) provides a complementary way to compare
representations through their
similarity structure, not through elementwise hidden-state equality
\cite{kornblith2019similarityrepresentations}. Given two activation matrices
\(\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d_x}\) and
\(\mathbf{Y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d_y}\) for the same \(n\) examples, the
linear CKA score can be written as
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{CKA}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})
=
\frac{\|\mathbf{Y}^{\top}\mathbf{X}\|_F^2}
{\|\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X}\|_F\,
\|\mathbf{Y}^{\top}\mathbf{Y}\|_F}.
\end{equation}
Higher CKA values indicate that the two representations induce similar
geometry over the examples. Because CKA is invariant to isotropic scaling and
orthogonal transformations, it is often more stable than direct hidden-state
matching when the teacher and student have different hidden dimensions. In this
thesis, CKA is therefore relevant both as a possible representation-level
distillation signal and as an analysis tool for measuring how closely student
features follow teacher features
\cite{kornblith2019similarityrepresentations,klabunde2023similarity}.

These advantages do not make representation-only matching sufficient.
Representation matching does not require a shared tokenizer, but it also does
not directly optimize answer probabilities. A hidden-state objective can make
the student resemble the teacher internally while still leaving the final
answer distribution poorly calibrated. In a multi-teacher setting, the problem
is more difficult: two teachers may have useful but incompatible internal
representations and a representation-only objective does not specify which
teacher should dominate for a given input. It also does not detect whether a
teacher's representation loss produces a gradient that agrees with the
supervised answer loss. For these reasons, representation matching is better
viewed as a complementary signal or diagnostic baseline, not a complete
solution to heterogeneous multi-teacher VLM distillation
\cite{kornblith2019similarityrepresentations,klabunde2023similarity,dasgupta2025improving}.

\section{Sparse Routing and Expert Selection}
\label{sec:prelim-sparse-routing}

Recent sparse expert models route each sample or token through only a subset of
available experts, improving efficiency and allowing specialized computation
\cite{shazeer2017outrageouslylargeneuralnetworks,fedus2022switchtransformersscalingtrillion,deepseekai2024deepseekv2strongeconomicalefficient}. Let \(\mathbf{h}^{(b)}\) denote a pooled representation for sample \(b\).
A router network \(G_{\phi}(\cdot)\) produces gate logits
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{r}^{(b)} = G_{\phi}(\mathbf{h}^{(b)}),
\qquad
\tilde{\mathbf{r}}^{(b)} = \text{AdjustedScores}\!\left(\mathbf{r}^{(b)}\right),
\qquad
\mathbf{p}^{(b)} = \mathrm{softmax}\!\left(\tilde{\mathbf{r}}^{(b)}\right),
\end{equation}
where \(p_i^{(b)}\) is the initial routing weight for expert \(i\). The
adjusted scores \(\tilde{\mathbf{r}}^{(b)}\) may include expert bias, training
noise or temperature scaling before the routing softmax.
\textit{Top-\(k\) routing} retains only the highest-scoring experts and zeros
out all others. \textit{Capacity constraints} prevent one expert from
receiving too many assignments.
If \(\mathcal{S}_b=\operatorname{TopK}(\mathbf{p}^{(b)},k)\) is the selected
expert set, the sparse expert output can be written as
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{y}^{(b)}
=
\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}_b}
\alpha_i^{(b)}E_i(\mathbf{h}^{(b)}),
\qquad
\alpha_i^{(b)}
=
\frac{p_i^{(b)}}{\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S}_b}p_j^{(b)}}.
\end{equation}
Only the selected experts are evaluated, so the activated computation can remain
small even when the total number of expert parameters is large. The same
abstraction is useful for teacher routing: each teacher can be treated as an
expert that contributes a weighted distillation signal for a subset of samples.

Sparse routing also needs mechanisms that prevent collapse onto a small subset
of experts. In a sparse MoE layer, collapse is not only a modeling problem but
also a systems problem: the overloaded experts determine the step latency,
while under-used experts receive few gradients and remain poorly specialized
\cite{shazeer2017outrageouslylargeneuralnetworks,lepikhin2020gshard,zhou2022mixtureofexpertswithexpertchoice}.
Let a minibatch contain \(T\) routed items and let \(N\) denote the number of
experts. For item \(b\), the router produces probabilities
\(\mathbf{p}^{(b)}\in\Delta^{N-1}\). A top-\(k\) router induces the binary
assignment mask
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{a}^{(b)}_i
=
\mathbf{1}\!\left\{
i\in \operatorname{TopK}\!\left(\mathbf{p}^{(b)},k\right)
\right\},
\qquad
n_i
=
\sum_{b=1}^{T}\mathbf{a}^{(b)}_i ,
\end{equation}
where \(n_i\) is the number of routed items assigned to expert \(i\). Balanced
routing corresponds to \(n_i\approx kT/N\). When \(n_i\) is much larger than
this value for a few experts, the router has effectively reduced the model to a
small ensemble of busy experts while wasting the capacity of the remaining
ones. The routing statistics used by many auxiliary losses can be written as a
\textit{soft} load and a \textit{hard} load:
\begin{equation}
\bar{p}_i
=
\frac{1}{T}\sum_{b=1}^{T}p_i^{(b)},
\qquad
\bar{q}_i
=
\frac{1}{kT}\sum_{b=1}^{T}\mathbf{a}^{(b)}_i .
\label{eq:prelim-soft-hard-load}
\end{equation}
Here, \(\bar{p}_i\) measures how much probability mass the router gives to
expert \(i\) before hard selection, whereas \(\bar{q}_i\) measures how often the
expert is actually selected after sparse routing. The Switch Transformer
balancing loss uses their scaled dot product
\cite{fedus2022switchtransformersscalingtrillion}:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{bal}
=
\alpha_{bal}N\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bar{p}_i\bar{q}_i .
\label{eq:prelim-switch-load-balance}
\end{equation}
The factor \(N\) keeps the magnitude approximately independent of the number of
experts because the uniform solution gives
\(\sum_i (1/N)(1/N)=1/N\). The hard load \(\bar{q}_i\) is not differentiable
through the top-\(k\) decision, but \(\bar{p}_i\) is differentiable. The loss
therefore gives the router a gradient signal that discourages assigning high
average probability to already busy experts.

In this thesis, the GRACE router uses entropy and \(z\)-loss terms in place of
a hard capacity constraint, so load balancing is understood here as a
stabilizer for sparse teacher selection, not a replacement for the task or
distillation loss.

Sparse routing becomes especially useful when the highest-utility expert
depends on the input
\cite{shazeer2017outrageouslylargeneuralnetworks,fedus2022switchtransformersscalingtrillion}.
In a generic sample-adaptive router, the selected expert set is a function of a
sample representation:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{S}_b
=
\operatorname{TopK}
\left(
\mathrm{softmax}(G_{\phi}(\mathbf{h}^{(b)})), k
\right).
\end{equation}
The output or loss is then formed only from experts in \(\mathcal{S}_b\). This
differs from fixed ensembling, where every expert contributes to every example
with the same global weight. Sample-adaptive routing is especially relevant
when inputs have different structure. In multimodal QA, one sample may require
OCR and document layout reasoning, another may require chart interpretation
and another may depend mainly on natural image recognition. A router can learn
to give different experts or teachers different responsibility across these
cases.

The mathematical distinction can be stated compactly. A global weighting scheme
uses one vector \(\boldsymbol{\pi}\in\Delta^{N-1}\) for the full training set:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}^{(b)}
=
\sum_{i=1}^{N}\pi_i \mathcal{L}_i^{(b)}.
\end{equation}
A sample-adaptive scheme uses \(w_i^{(b)}\):
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}^{(b)}
=
\sum_{i=1}^{N}w_i^{(b)} \mathcal{L}_i^{(b)},
\qquad
w_i^{(b)}=0 \ \text{for} \ i\notin\mathcal{S}_b .
\end{equation}
The sparsity constraint limits the number of active experts and makes the
teacher-selection decision interpretable. It also reduces the chance that
low-utility or irrelevant teachers dominate the update for a sample.

The same routing abstraction can be used for distillation by treating frozen
teachers as experts. The router does not combine hidden activations. Instead,
it combines teacher-guided losses.
If \(T_i\) is teacher \(i\), then teacher \(T_i\) contributes a loss
\(\mathcal{L}_{KD}^{(b,i)}\) for sample \(b\). A routed distillation loss takes
the form
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{route}^{(b)}
=
\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}_b}
\omega_i^{(b)}
\mathcal{L}_{KD}^{(b,i)}.
\end{equation}
This view connects MoE selection to multi-teacher KD: teacher routing is a
conditional computation mechanism over supervision signals. Unlike standard
MoE, the experts here are frozen, so only the student and router parameters
receive gradient updates. Routing in a distillation setting has two additional complications. First,
teacher quality is not a fixed property of the teacher alone. It depends on the
input, the current student state and the task objective. Second, a high router
score does not guarantee that the selected teacher produces a gradient aligned
with supervised learning. This means routing is useful but not a complete
solution to teacher selection by itself
\cite{zhu2021studentcustomized,yuan2020reinforcedmultiteacherselection,yu2020gradient}.

\section{Multi-Teacher Distillation}

A pool of frozen teachers can be aggregated by assigning each teacher a
distillation weight. Let \(\mathcal{T}=\{T_1,\ldots,T_N\}\) be such a pool. For sample
\(b\), teacher \(T_i\) produces a per-teacher distillation loss
\(\mathcal{L}_{KD}^{(b,i)}\). In principle, one could average these losses uniformly,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{KD}^{(b)}
=
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathcal{L}_{KD}^{(b,i)},
\end{equation}
but this assumes every teacher is equally useful for every sample. In practice, different teachers may provide conflicting guidance because their
predictions, specialized strengths and KD gradients need not agree on the same
training sample. Some teachers may help the supervised objective on a given
sample, while others may push the student in a different direction
\cite{zhu2021studentcustomized}. The sparse-routing framework introduced in
Section~\ref{sec:prelim-sparse-routing} applies directly here by treating
teachers as experts whose usefulness varies across samples
\cite{yuan2020reinforcedmultiteacherselection,zhang2023adaptivemultiteachermetalearning,yu2024selectdistill}.
More generally, an adaptive multi-teacher objective can be written as
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{KD}^{(b)}
=
\sum_{i=1}^{N}w_i^{(b)}\mathcal{L}_{KD}^{(b,i)},
\qquad
w_i^{(b)}\ge0,
\qquad
\sum_{i=1}^{N}w_i^{(b)}=1.
\end{equation}
Uniform averaging is the special case \(w_i^{(b)}=1/N\). A routed method
lets the weights depend on the sample, the student state and the current
teacher-selection policy. This is useful when teachers specialize differently:
one teacher may score higher on documents, another on charts and another on
natural scene text. The main risk is that adaptive weighting can collapse to one
teacher
\cite{yuan2020reinforcedmultiteacherselection,zhang2023adaptivemultiteachermetalearning,yu2024selectdistill}.


Sparse routing does not remove the possibility of gradient conflict. Even after
it selects a teacher subset, the remaining teachers may still induce KD
gradients that disagree with the supervised objective. To see why, consider the
standard combined distillation loss
\(\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{CE} + \alpha\,\mathcal{L}_{KD}\).
This objective minimizes a supervised term and a teacher-guided term together.
Because both terms share the same student parameters, the combined update
direction depends on whether the gradients \(\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{CE}\)
and \(\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{KD}\) agree or conflict. When these two
gradient directions are aligned, the combined update reinforces both objectives.
When they oppose each other, the resulting update is a compromise that may
degrade one or both objectives. This is an instance of
\textit{multi-objective optimization}, where a single parameter update must serve
competing objectives simultaneously
\cite{zhu2021studentcustomized,yu2020gradient}. In multi-teacher distillation,
the problem is compounded because each teacher contributes its own KD gradient.
A routing mechanism can select a small teacher subset, but selection alone
cannot guarantee that the remaining KD gradients support the supervised task
update.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related-work-gap}

Previous work on heterogeneous VLM distillation falls into four groups.

\textbf{Response-based and cross-tokenizer KD.}
Response-based KD transfers teacher output distributions to the student and
has been applied to language-model and multimodal compression
\cite{hinton2015distillingknowledgeneuralnetwork,xu2024surveyknowledgedistillationlarge,
fang2021compressingvlm,hu2024visualprogramdistillation,yang2024clipcid}.
Its supervision is task-facing, but standard KL or JS losses assume a shared
vocabulary. Tokenizer-agnostic and optimal-transport methods relax this by
comparing probability mass over decoded strings
\cite{boizard2024towardscrosstokenizerdistillation,takezawa2021supervisedtreewasserstein,
leyamadafukumizucuturi2019treesliced,dasgupta2025improving},
yet are typically formulated for single-teacher transfer and do not address
multi-teacher selection or weighting.

\textbf{Representation matching and CKA alignment.}
Representation-level methods align hidden states or similarity structures,
bypassing vocabulary mismatch entirely
\cite{kornblith2019similarityrepresentations,dasgupta2025improving}.
However, hidden-state similarity is an indirect proxy for task behavior:
high CKA does not guarantee matching answer probabilities after the language
head, and these methods provide no mechanism for choosing among teachers.

\textbf{Multi-teacher KD and sparse routing.}
Multi-teacher KD exploits teacher diversity for different input types
\cite{yuan2020reinforcedmultiteacherselection,zhang2023adaptivemultiteachermetalearning,
yu2024selectdistill}, and sparse expert routing improves scalability by
activating only a subset of teachers per sample
\cite{shazeer2017outrageouslylargeneuralnetworks,fedus2022switchtransformersscalingtrillion,
zhou2022mixtureofexpertswithexpertchoice}.
The limitation is that routing estimates \textbf{relevance}, not
\textbf{objective compatibility}: a routed teacher can still produce a KD
gradient that conflicts with the supervised loss.

\textbf{Gradient-conflict resolution.}
Gradient-based filtering directly measures whether auxiliary objectives agree
with the supervised update direction
\cite{zhu2021studentcustomized,yu2020gradient},
but these methods are not designed as cross-tokenizer multi-teacher objectives
and do not define a tokenizer-agnostic output loss.

Together, these groups leave a clear gap: no existing method jointly handles
tokenizer mismatch, sample-adaptive teacher selection and objective-aware
post-routing weighting. Addressing all three requires combining
\textbf{tokenizer-agnostic output matching}, \textbf{sparse sample-dependent
routing} and \textbf{gradient-agreement refinement} in a single framework.
