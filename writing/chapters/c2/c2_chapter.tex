\chapter{Background and Related Work}
\label{chap:preliminaries}

Three bodies of prior work bear directly on GRACE: knowledge distillation,
tokenizer-agnostic distribution matching and sparse expert routing. The final
section shows where these converge into the research gap the thesis addresses.

\section{Vision-Language Models}

Modern vision-language models often combine a vision encoder with a language
model. On the visual side, many systems use a Vision Transformer pretrained
with an image-text objective, commonly CLIP
\cite{radford2021learningtransferablevisualmodels} or SigLIP
\cite{zhai2023sigmoidlosslanguageimage}, which converts an image into a
sequence of patch embeddings that capture both local and global visual
features. A connector module then fuses these visual tokens with text, using
cross-attention, a Q-Former or a learned projector
\cite{alayrac2022flamingovisuallanguagemodel,li2023blip2bootstrappinglanguageimagepretraining,liu2023visualinstructiontuning}. This design separates visual perception from language generation: the vision
encoder produces a compact sequence of image features, while the language model
provides the autoregressive prior used to answer questions. VLM families differ
in where and how vision-language fusion happens, as shown
in Figure~\ref{fig:prelim-vlm-families}. Dual-encoder models align image and
text embeddings contrastively, cross-attention models let language tokens
attend to visual features directly and projector-based models map visual
embeddings into the language model hidden space and then decode with a standard
causal language model. This thesis focuses on projector-based generative VLMs
because their autoregressive output distributions provide the supervision
target for distillation.
In this setting, projected image embeddings are inserted into the
language-model context as visual tokens, so answer generation remains ordinary
next-token prediction conditioned on both the image representation and the
textual question.

The student VLM produces answer tokens autoregressively. For language modeling, the
Transformer maps each hidden state \(\mathbf{h}_j\) to next-token logits and
probabilities \cite{vaswani2017attentionneed}:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{z}_j=\mathbf{W}_{o}\mathbf{h}_j+\mathbf{b}_{o},
\qquad
p_j(v)=\mathrm{softmax}(\mathbf{z}_j)_v
=
\frac{\exp(z_{j,v})}{\sum_{u\in\mathcal{V}}\exp(z_{j,u})}.
\end{equation}
At answer position \(j\), the VLM can be abstracted as a mapping from the
multimodal input and previous answer tokens to next-token logits:
\[
\mathbf{z}_j = f_{\theta}(x,y_{<j}),
\qquad
\mathbf{z}_j \in \mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{V}|},
\]
where \(x\) is a multimodal input and \(\mathbf{z}_j\) denotes the logits used
for autoregressive answer generation at position \(j\).
For an answer sequence \(y=(y_1,\ldots,y_L)\), the model factorizes the
conditional probability as
\begin{equation}
p_{\theta}(y\mid x)
=
\prod_{j=1}^{L}p_{\theta}(y_j\mid x,y_{<j}).
\end{equation}
Token-level distillation therefore compares teacher and student answer-token
distributions, \(p_{t,j}\) and \(p_{s,j}\), at each supervised position
\cite{vaswani2017attentionneed,hinton2015distillingknowledgeneuralnetwork}. In VQA, short answers such as numbers, names and document fields can differ by
a single token, so per-position supervision accuracy matters.
Standard KD compares these distributions directly; heterogeneous VLM distillation instead requires a vocabulary-independent matching objective.

\section{Knowledge Distillation}

Knowledge distillation trains a compact student to match the outputs of a
larger teacher
\cite{hinton2015distillingknowledgeneuralnetwork,xu2024surveyknowledgedistillationlarge}.
Let \(\mathcal{L}_{CE}\) denote the supervised cross-entropy loss of the student.
A generic distillation objective takes the form
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}
=
\lambda_{CE}\mathcal{L}_{CE}
+
\lambda_{KD}\mathcal{L}_{KD},
\end{equation}
where \(\mathcal{L}_{KD}\) is a teacher-guided loss. Token-level distillation compares teacher and student output distributions
alongside hard labels. The student therefore learns the teacher's relative probabilities over alternative
answers, often called \textit{dark knowledge}, which hard labels discard
\cite{hinton2015distillingknowledgeneuralnetwork}.
For a single answer position, a common same-vocabulary form is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{KD}
=
\tau^2
\operatorname{KL}\!\left(
\mathbf{p}_t^{(\tau)}
\middle\|
\mathbf{p}_s^{(\tau)}
\right),
\end{equation}
where \(\tau\) is the distillation temperature. The cross-entropy term anchors
the student to the ground-truth answer, while the KD term transfers the
teacher's relative preference over alternative tokens. The same structure applies in the heterogeneous setting, but the comparison
term must handle different teacher and student tokenizers.

The output distributions used in KD are often softened before comparison. Let
\(\mathbf{z}_s\) and \(\mathbf{z}_t\) denote student and teacher logits. With
temperatures \(\tau_s\) and \(\tau_t\), the corresponding distributions are
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{p}_s = \mathrm{softmax}\!\left(\frac{\mathbf{z}_s}{\tau_s}\right),
\qquad
\mathbf{p}_t = \mathrm{softmax}\!\left(\frac{\mathbf{z}_t}{\tau_t}\right).
\end{equation}

Many classical KD methods compare \(\mathbf{p}_s\) and \(\mathbf{p}_t\) using
divergences such as forward KL or Jensen-Shannon divergence. However, these
approaches usually assume that teacher and student share the same vocabulary
\cite{hinton2015distillingknowledgeneuralnetwork,lin1991jensen,boizard2024towardscrosstokenizerdistillation}.
The temperature controls how much probability mass is exposed beyond the top
answer token. When \(\tau>1\), the softmax distribution becomes flatter and
low-rank alternatives receive non-negligible probability. When \(\tau<1\), the
distribution becomes sharper and approaches hard-label supervision. The teacher's tail distribution often encodes semantic alternatives
such as related words, number formats or subword variants.
The same softening applies before Byte-Trie Wasserstein matching. Since
student and teacher temperatures may be chosen independently, \(\tau_t\)
controls how much dark knowledge the teacher exposes, whereas
\(\tau_s\) controls the smoothness of the student distribution under matching.

GRACE restricts distillation to the supervised answer positions, matching the
span evaluated at test time~\cite{liu2023visualinstructiontuning}.
The KD loss therefore masks ignored tokens and truncates student and teacher
answer spans to a common length, producing a signal that works across different teacher and student tokenizers.
Let \(\Omega_b^{CE}\) denote the non-ignored student answer positions for
sample \(b\), so the supervised loss is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{CE}^{(b)}
=
\frac{1}{|\Omega_b^{CE}|}
\sum_{j\in\Omega_b^{CE}}
-\log p_s(y_j\mid x_b,y_{<j}).
\end{equation}
The same masking applies to KD positions, keeping the teacher signal on the
evaluated answer span rather than on prompt reconstruction, padding or formatting.
For multiple teachers, the KD position set can be teacher-specific because
teacher tokenizers and generated answer spans may differ. Per-teacher KD positions \(\Omega_{b,i}^{KD}\) are constructed separately for
each teacher, restricted to valid aligned answer positions.

KD methods differ in the type of teacher signal they use. Common response-level divergences and feature-level
similarity losses are collected in
Table~\ref{tab:prelim-kd-objectives}
\cite{hinton2015distillingknowledgeneuralnetwork,lin1991jensen}. In the
table, \(p\) and \(q\) denote teacher and student output distributions over
token events \(t\), and \(m=\frac{1}{2}(p+q)\) is the JS midpoint distribution.
The teacher and student feature vectors are
\[
\mathbf{u}=\Phi_T(f_T(x,y)),
\qquad
\mathbf{v}=\Phi_S(f_S(x,y)).
\]
The index \(r\) marks feature dimensions or normalized feature bins.
\textit{Response-based} distillation uses the teacher's output
distribution as supervision. In its standard form, the loss compares
next-token distributions:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{resp}
=
\frac{1}{|\Omega|}
\sum_{j\in\Omega}
\operatorname{KL}\!\left(
\mathbf{p}_{t,j}^{(\tau)}
\middle\|
\mathbf{p}_{s,j}^{(\tau)}
\right),
\end{equation}
where \(\Omega\) is the set of supervised answer positions. This is the classical KD form, introduced by Hinton et
al.~\cite{hinton2015distillingknowledgeneuralnetwork} and still common in
language-model and VLM compression
\cite{xu2024surveyknowledgedistillationlarge,fang2021compressingvlm}.

\textit{Feature-based} distillation aligns intermediate representations
between student and teacher layers. Given student and teacher hidden states
\(\mathbf{H}_s^{(\ell)}\) and \(\mathbf{H}_t^{(m)}\) at selected layers, a
direct matching objective is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{feat}
=
\left\|
\phi_s\!\left(\mathbf{H}_s^{(\ell)}\right)
-
\phi_t\!\left(\mathbf{H}_t^{(m)}\right)
\right\|_2^2,
\end{equation}
where \(\phi_s\) and \(\phi_t\) are projection functions that make the hidden
states comparable. Feature-based methods apply when output vocabularies
are incompatible, since the comparison is defined in representation
space without direct vocabulary comparison
\cite{dasgupta2025improving,kornblith2019similarityrepresentations}. However,
they predict answer likelihood less directly and can transfer internal structure
specific to the teacher architecture, so this thesis treats response-level and
representation-level signals as complementary
\cite{klabunde2023similarity,dasgupta2025improving}.

\section{Tokenizer-Agnostic Distribution Matching}

Same-vocabulary KD assumes that teacher and student probabilities are indexed
by the same token set. Heterogeneous VLM distillation breaks this assumption: a teacher
may tokenize the string ``invoice'' as one token while the student splits it
into several subword pieces; directly applying KL over their respective
vocabularies then compares unrelated indices and is invalid \cite{boizard2024towardscrosstokenizerdistillation}. The natural fix is to decode each token into a string and compare probability
mass over decoded strings instead of vocabulary indices. Let
\(\mathcal{V}_t\) and \(\mathcal{V}_s\) be the teacher and student
vocabularies with decoding maps into a shared string space \(\mathcal{S}\):
\[
\psi_t:\mathcal{V}_t\rightarrow\mathcal{S},
\qquad
\psi_s:\mathcal{V}_s\rightarrow\mathcal{S}.
\]
Transport-based objectives replace same-vocabulary KL with a distance over
probability mass on a shared string support.

Optimal transport compares two probability measures by the minimum cost required
to move mass from one to the other
\cite{peyre2019computationaloptimaltransport}. For probability measures
\(\mu_s\) and \(\mu_t\), the \(p\)-Wasserstein cost is
\begin{equation}
W_p^p(\mu_s,\mu_t)
=
\min_{\gamma \in \Pi(\mu_s,\mu_t)}
\int_{\Omega_s \times \Omega_t}
\|x-y\|^p \, d\gamma(x,y),
\end{equation}
where \(\Pi(\mu_s,\mu_t)\) is the set of joint couplings with marginals
\(\mu_s\) and \(\mu_t\). For discrete weights \(\mathbf{a}\), \(\mathbf{b}\)
and cost matrix \(\mathbf{C}\), this becomes
\begin{equation}
W(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})
=
\min_{\mathbf{P}\in\mathcal{U}(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})}
\langle \mathbf{P}, \mathbf{C} \rangle,
\qquad
\mathcal{U}(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})
=
\left\{
\mathbf{P}\ge 0 \mid
\mathbf{P}\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{a},\
\mathbf{P}^{\top}\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{b}
\right\}.
\end{equation}
Unlike pointwise divergences, Wasserstein distances reflect displacement even
when supports are disjoint, as Figure~\ref{fig:wasserstein-geometry}
illustrates.

For tokenizer-agnostic matching, dense OT is still costly because it requires a
pairwise ground cost between all source and target bins. A \textit{tree metric} replaces the pairwise cost matrix: probability mass is
placed on a shared hierarchy and the distance reduces to the sum of weighted
\textit{subtree-mass imbalances}
\cite{takezawa2021supervisedtreewasserstein,peyre2019computationaloptimaltransport}.
For a rooted tree \(T=(V,E)\) with edge weights \(w(e)\), one convenient form is
\begin{equation}
W_T(\mu,\nu)
=
\sum_{e\in E}
w(e)\,
\left|
\mu\!\left(\Gamma(e)\right)-\nu\!\left(\Gamma(e)\right)
\right|,
\label{eq:prelim-tree-wasserstein}
\end{equation}
where \(\Gamma(e)\) denotes the subtree below edge \(e\). On a tree metric, transport reduces to per-edge subtree-mass imbalances,
avoiding the dense source-target coupling of standard OT
\cite{takezawa2021supervisedtreewasserstein,leyamadafukumizucuturi2019treesliced}.
Figures~\ref{fig:prelim-tree-ot} and
\ref{fig:prelim-optimal-transport} show the two commonly used OT views: a
tree-metric view for fast subtree-mass comparisons and a matrix view for
discrete transport plans. Tree-sliced OT shows that averaging over random tree metrics
preserves OT geometry while admitting fast closed-form computation
\cite{leyamadafukumizucuturi2019treesliced}.

A different approach avoids output distributions by matching intermediate
representations directly. Let the student and teacher
hidden-state matrices be
\[
\mathbf{H}_s\in\mathbb{R}^{n_s\times d_s},
\qquad
\mathbf{H}_t\in\mathbb{R}^{n_t\times d_t}
\]
Because the sequence lengths and dimensions may differ,
alignment usually requires pooling, projection, interpolation or layer
selection:
\begin{equation}
\tilde{\mathbf{H}}_s = A_s(\mathbf{H}_s),
\qquad
\tilde{\mathbf{H}}_t = A_t(\mathbf{H}_t),
\qquad
\mathcal{L}_{hid}
=
\left\|\tilde{\mathbf{H}}_s-\tilde{\mathbf{H}}_t\right\|_F^2 .
\end{equation}
Here, \(A_s\) and \(A_t\) denote alignment operations that place student and
teacher activations in a comparable space. This family applies to heterogeneous models because it avoids direct vocabulary
comparison and can draw on visual, textual or fused multimodal representations \cite{dasgupta2025improving}.

Centered Kernel Alignment (CKA) compares representations through their
similarity structure and avoids direct elementwise hidden-state matching
\cite{kornblith2019similarityrepresentations}. Given activation matrices
\(\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d_x}\) and
\(\mathbf{Y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d_y}\) over the same \(n\) examples, the
linear CKA score is
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{CKA}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})
=
\frac{\|\mathbf{Y}^{\top}\mathbf{X}\|_F^2}
{\|\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X}\|_F\,
\|\mathbf{Y}^{\top}\mathbf{Y}\|_F}.
\end{equation}
Higher CKA values mean the two representations share the same pairwise
similarity structure across the evaluated samples. Because CKA is invariant to
isotropic scaling and orthogonal transformations, it remains valid when teacher
and student have different hidden dimensions, unlike direct hidden-state matching. This thesis uses CKA both as a distillation signal and as a diagnostic for teacher-student feature alignment
\cite{kornblith2019similarityrepresentations,klabunde2023similarity}.


\section{Sparse Routing and Expert Selection}
\label{sec:prelim-sparse-routing}

Recent sparse expert models route each sample or token through only a subset of
available experts, reducing computation to a fraction of parameters per input
\cite{shazeer2017outrageouslylargeneuralnetworks,fedus2022switchtransformersscalingtrillion,deepseekai2024deepseekv2strongeconomicalefficient}. Let \(\mathbf{h}^{(b)}\) denote a pooled representation for sample \(b\).
A router network \(G_{\phi}(\cdot)\) produces gate logits
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{r}^{(b)} = G_{\phi}(\mathbf{h}^{(b)}),
\qquad
\tilde{\mathbf{r}}^{(b)} = \text{AdjustedScores}\!\left(\mathbf{r}^{(b)}\right),
\qquad
\mathbf{p}^{(b)} = \mathrm{softmax}\!\left(\tilde{\mathbf{r}}^{(b)}\right),
\end{equation}
where \(p_i^{(b)}\) is the initial routing weight for expert \(i\). The
adjusted scores \(\tilde{\mathbf{r}}^{(b)}\) may include expert bias, training
noise or temperature scaling before the routing softmax.
\textit{Top-\(k\) routing} retains only the highest-scoring experts and zeros
out all others, while \textit{capacity constraints} prevent any single expert
from receiving too many assignments.
The selected expert set is
\[
\mathcal{S}_b=\operatorname{TopK}(\mathbf{p}^{(b)},k).
\]
The sparse expert output can be written as
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{y}^{(b)}
=
\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}_b}
\alpha_i^{(b)}E_i(\mathbf{h}^{(b)}),
\qquad
\alpha_i^{(b)}
=
\frac{p_i^{(b)}}{\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S}_b}p_j^{(b)}}.
\end{equation}
Only the selected experts are evaluated, keeping active computation small even
when the total expert count is large. The same abstraction transfers directly to teacher routing: each teacher acts as
a frozen expert that contributes a weighted distillation loss for a subset of samples.

Sparse routing also needs mechanisms that prevent collapse onto a small subset
of experts. In a sparse MoE layer, collapse is both a modeling problem and a
systems problem: the overloaded experts determine the step latency, while
under-used experts receive few gradients and remain poorly specialized
\cite{shazeer2017outrageouslylargeneuralnetworks,lepikhin2020gshard,zhou2022mixtureofexpertswithexpertchoice}.
Let a mini-batch contain \(T\) routed items and let \(N\) denote the number of
experts. For item \(b\), the router produces probabilities
\(\mathbf{p}^{(b)}\in\Delta^{N-1}\). A top-\(k\) router induces the binary
assignment mask
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{a}^{(b)}_i
=
\mathbf{1}\!\left\{
i\in \operatorname{TopK}\!\left(\mathbf{p}^{(b)},k\right)
\right\},
\qquad
n_i
=
\sum_{b=1}^{T}\mathbf{a}^{(b)}_i ,
\end{equation}
where \(n_i\) is the number of routed items assigned to expert \(i\). Balanced
routing corresponds to
\[
n_i\approx \frac{kT}{N}.
\]
When \(n_i\) is much larger than
this value for a few experts, the router has effectively reduced the model to a
small ensemble of busy experts while wasting the capacity of the remaining
ones. The routing statistics used by many auxiliary losses can be written as a
\textit{soft} load and a \textit{hard} load:
\begin{equation}
\bar{p}_i
=
\frac{1}{T}\sum_{b=1}^{T}p_i^{(b)},
\qquad
\bar{q}_i
=
\frac{1}{kT}\sum_{b=1}^{T}\mathbf{a}^{(b)}_i .
\label{eq:prelim-soft-hard-load}
\end{equation}
Here, \(\bar{p}_i\) measures how much probability mass the router gives to
expert \(i\) before hard selection, whereas \(\bar{q}_i\) measures how often the
expert is actually selected after sparse routing. The Switch Transformer
balancing loss uses their scaled dot product
\cite{fedus2022switchtransformersscalingtrillion}:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{bal}
=
\alpha_{bal}N\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bar{p}_i\bar{q}_i .
\label{eq:prelim-switch-load-balance}
\end{equation}
The factor \(N\) keeps the magnitude approximately independent of the number of
experts because the uniform solution gives
\[
\sum_i (1/N)(1/N)=\frac{1}{N}.
\]
The hard load \(\bar{q}_i\) is non-differentiable
through the top-\(k\) decision, but \(\bar{p}_i\) is differentiable. The loss
therefore gives the router a gradient signal that discourages assigning high
average probability to already busy experts.

In GRACE, entropy and \(z\)-loss terms stabilize sparse teacher selection while
the task and distillation losses remain the main training objectives.

Sparse routing is most effective when the best expert for a given input
depends on that input
\cite{shazeer2017outrageouslylargeneuralnetworks,fedus2022switchtransformersscalingtrillion}.
In a generic sample-adaptive router, the selected expert set is a function of a
sample representation:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{S}_b
=
\operatorname{TopK}
\left(
\mathrm{softmax}(G_{\phi}(\mathbf{h}^{(b)})), k
\right).
\end{equation}
The loss is then computed only from experts in \(\mathcal{S}_b\). This
differs from fixed ensembling, where every expert contributes to every example
with the same global weight. In multimodal QA, one sample may require OCR and document layout reasoning,
another chart interpretation, and a third natural image recognition.

A global weighting scheme
uses one vector \(\boldsymbol{\pi}\in\Delta^{N-1}\) for the full training set:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}^{(b)}
=
\sum_{i=1}^{N}\pi_i \mathcal{L}_i^{(b)}.
\end{equation}
A sample-adaptive scheme uses \(w_i^{(b)}\):
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}^{(b)}
=
\sum_{i=1}^{N}w_i^{(b)} \mathcal{L}_i^{(b)},
\qquad
w_i^{(b)}=0 \ \text{for} \ i\notin\mathcal{S}_b .
\end{equation}
The sparsity constraint caps the number of active experts and limits the
influence of low-utility teachers on any given update.

Distillation can reuse this routing framework by treating frozen teachers as
experts, with the router selecting which teachers supervise each sample.
Teacher \(T_i\) contributes loss \(\mathcal{L}_{KD}^{(b,i)}\) for sample
\(b\). A routed distillation loss takes the form
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{route}^{(b)}
=
\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}_b}
\omega_i^{(b)}
\mathcal{L}_{KD}^{(b,i)}.
\end{equation}
Teacher routing is then MoE selection applied to distillation: it determines which teacher losses update the student for each sample. Unlike standard
MoE, the experts here are frozen, so only the student and router parameters
receive gradient updates. Routing in a distillation setting faces an additional
complication: teacher quality varies with the input, the current student state
and the task objective. A high router score can still produce a KD gradient that
conflicts with supervised learning, so GRACE adds gradient agreement after routing
\cite{zhu2021studentcustomized,yuan2020reinforcedmultiteacherselection,yu2020gradient}.

\section{Multi-Teacher Distillation}

Multi-teacher distillation uses several teachers rather than one. Let \(\mathcal{T}=\{T_1,\ldots,T_N\}\) be the teacher pool, and let
\(\mathcal{L}_{KD}^{(b,i)}\) denote the distillation loss produced by teacher
\(T_i\) for sample \(b\). The simplest aggregation rule is uniform averaging:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{KD}^{(b)}
=
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathcal{L}_{KD}^{(b,i)}.
\end{equation}
Uniform averaging is useful as a reference because it isolates teacher
diversity from any learned selection. Its limitation is that it assigns equal
influence to all teachers, even when their strengths vary across document
images, charts and natural scene text.

A heterogeneous teacher pool can differ along multiple axes: tokenizer, language backbone, vision encoder,
pretraining data and answer calibration. One model may provide stronger supervision for OCR-heavy samples while another
is better calibrated on charts. But teacher distributions can also disagree on
the same answer position when their tokenizers split the answer string
differently. A multi-teacher method must therefore select and weight teachers,
not just aggregate them.

A more general multi-teacher objective assigns weights to teachers:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{KD}^{(b)}
=
\sum_{i=1}^{N}w_i^{(b)}\mathcal{L}_{KD}^{(b,i)},
\qquad
w_i^{(b)}\ge0,
\qquad
\sum_{i=1}^{N}w_i^{(b)}=1.
\end{equation}
The weights can be fixed globally, learned globally or conditioned on the
sample. Fixed weights ignore input variation entirely. Globally learned weights
can identify a single dominant teacher but give every sample the same mixture.
VLM tasks require sample-dependent weights: the best teacher for scene-text
OCR may differ from the best for chart reading or document layout. Existing adaptive
multi-teacher methods that address this setting are surveyed in
Section~\ref{sec:related-work-gap}.

Sparse routing implements sample-dependent weighting directly. The
routing framework in Section~\ref{sec:prelim-sparse-routing} can be applied to
teacher selection by treating frozen teachers as experts whose losses are
selected for each sample. A routed teacher set reduces the teacher losses per update and makes
teacher-sample assignments explicit. However, routing introduces two design questions that
standard ensembling does not address. First, the router must avoid collapsing
onto a single teacher too early, otherwise the teacher pool becomes an expensive
single-teacher system. Second, a teacher that appears relevant to the input may
still produce a KD direction that conflicts with the supervised CE objective.

The second issue is an optimization conflict rather than only a selection
problem. Consider the standard combined distillation loss
\[
\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{CE} + \alpha\,\mathcal{L}_{KD}.
\]
Because both terms share the same student parameters, the combined update
reinforces learning when CE and KD gradients agree and partially offsets
learning when they point in competing directions. This is the multi-objective
optimization setting studied by gradient-conflict methods
\cite{zhu2021studentcustomized,yu2020gradient}. In multi-teacher distillation,
each teacher contributes a separate KD gradient, so selecting a teacher subset
does not by itself verify that the selected gradients support the supervised
update. GRACE therefore uses routing to propose candidate teachers and
gradient-agreement refinement to check whether their KD gradients align with
the supervised CE objective.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related-work-gap}

The thesis builds on four lines of prior work: response-based KD,
cross-tokenizer distribution matching, representation matching, and adaptive
multi-teacher selection. Each addresses part of the problem, but no prior method handles all three
constraints together.

\textbf{Response-based and vocabulary-mismatched KD.}
Response-based KD transfers teacher output distributions to the student
\cite{hinton2015distillingknowledgeneuralnetwork,xu2024surveyknowledgedistillationlarge}.
In multimodal models, it has been used for compressing VLMs or transferring
vision-language behavior from larger models to smaller ones
\cite{fang2021compressingvlm,hu2024visualprogramdistillation,yang2024clipcid}.
This family supervises the output distribution used at prediction time, which
directly matches the generation objective. Its main limitation in heterogeneous
teacher-student settings is the shared-vocabulary assumption: KL, reverse KL
and Jensen-Shannon losses compare coordinates that are only meaningful when
teacher and student token indices refer to the same vocabulary items.

Cross-tokenizer KD methods relax this assumption by comparing decoded strings
or probability mass over a shared support
\cite{boizard2024towardscrosstokenizerdistillation,dasgupta2025improving}.
Optimal-transport and tree-Wasserstein methods are especially relevant because
they define a ground geometry between tokens rather than requiring exact
token-index equality
\cite{takezawa2021supervisedtreewasserstein,leyamadafukumizucuturi2019treesliced}.
These methods provide the vocabulary-independent loss that GRACE uses at the
output level. All are formulated as single-teacher objectives, however: none
decides which teacher should supervise a given sample, and none checks whether
a chosen teacher's KD gradient conflicts with the supervised CE objective.

\textbf{Representation matching and CKA alignment.}
Representation-level distillation avoids the vocabulary problem by matching
hidden states, attention maps or similarity structure rather than output-token
probabilities. CKA is a common tool for comparing representations because it
measures similarity structure while being invariant to orthogonal changes of
basis and isotropic scaling \cite{kornblith2019similarityrepresentations}.
This makes CKA applicable even when teacher and student tokenizers or hidden
dimensions differ. Recent cross-tokenizer work also uses
representation-level information as a complementary alignment signal
\cite{dasgupta2025improving}.

The limitation is that representation similarity is not the same as answer
distribution matching. Two models can have similar hidden-state geometry while
their language heads assign different probabilities to answer tokens. This gap matters directly for VQA, where a small probability shift among OCR
strings or numeric answers changes the final evaluated score. Representation
matching also leaves the teacher-selection question unresolved: it can compare
a teacher and student once a teacher has been chosen, but it does not specify
which teacher should dominate for a given sample. For these reasons, this thesis
treats representation matching as a diagnostic baseline and keeps output-level
distribution matching as the primary distillation signal.

\textbf{Multi-teacher KD and sparse routing.}
Multi-teacher KD studies how to combine several teachers rather than relying on
a single supervisor. Reinforcement-based and meta-learning approaches learn
teacher weights or selection policies that adapt over training
\cite{yuan2020reinforcedmultiteacherselection,zhang2023adaptivemultiteachermetalearning,
yu2024selectdistill}. These methods show that teacher diversity raises student accuracy when teachers
make complementary errors. They also identify a core challenge: teacher choice
must be sample-dependent,
because the best teacher for document layout may differ from the best teacher
for chart reasoning or natural scene text.

Sparse expert routing selects only a subset of experts per input
\cite{shazeer2017outrageouslylargeneuralnetworks,fedus2022switchtransformersscalingtrillion,
zhou2022mixtureofexpertswithexpertchoice}. In a distillation setting, the
experts are frozen teachers and the selected objects are teacher losses rather
than forward-pass expert activations. The distinction is that routing only estimates teacher relevance. A routed teacher can still produce a KD
gradient that conflicts with the supervised CE objective, so sparse routing
must be paired with an objective-compatibility check.

\textbf{Gradient-conflict resolution.}
Gradient-conflict methods measure whether multiple objectives induce compatible
updates on shared parameters. Student-customized distillation and gradient
surgery methods show that conflicting gradients can harm learning and that
gradient direction filters or reweights auxiliary objectives
\cite{zhu2021studentcustomized,yu2020gradient}. These ideas apply directly to multi-teacher KD, where each teacher contributes
a separate KD gradient.
However, prior gradient-conflict methods do not address cross-tokenizer output
matching, and they do not provide a sparse teacher-selection mechanism for a
heterogeneous VLM teacher pool.

No existing method closes all four gaps simultaneously. Vocabulary-aligned KD
breaks across tokenizers. Cross-tokenizer transport fixes that but assumes a
single teacher. Representation matching avoids the vocabulary problem but
produces no answer-calibrated signal and leaves teacher selection unresolved.
Routing-based methods select teachers but none verify gradient compatibility.
GRACE addresses all four within one framework by combining a Byte-Trie
Wasserstein output loss, sparse teacher routing and gradient-agreement
refinement.
