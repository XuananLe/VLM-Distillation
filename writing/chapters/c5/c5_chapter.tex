To provide qualitative evidence of attention improvement, we use DEX-AR
attribution maps \cite{bousselham2026dexar}. For token \(t\), layer \(l\) and
head \(h\), DEX-AR forms a gradient-based attribution map
\[
\mathbf{G}_{t}^{(l,h)}
=
\nabla_{\mathbf{A}^{(l,h)}} y_t \odot \mathbf{A}^{(l,h)},
\]
DEX-AR then keeps visually grounded heads with weights defined as
\[
w^{(l,h)}=\max\!\big(S_{\mathrm{img}}^{(l,h)}-S_{\mathrm{text}}^{(l,h)},0\big).
\]
The token-level and sentence-level attribution maps are defined as
\[
\mathbf{H}_t=\sum_{l,h} w^{(l,h)} \mathbf{G}_{t}^{(l,h)},
\qquad
\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{sent}}=\sum_t \delta_t \mathbf{H}_t .
\]
Here \(\delta_t=1/|\Omega_{\mathrm{ans}}|\) for answer tokens
\(t\in\Omega_{\mathrm{ans}}\), where \(\Omega_{\mathrm{ans}}\) is the set of
generated answer token positions and \(\delta_t=0\) otherwise. The
sentence-level map is therefore the uniform average of token-level maps over
the generated answer span. Raw attention maps and generic saliency methods are not well suited here because the model is autoregressive and the quantity of interest is which
image regions drove the answer, not which regions are visually salient in
general. DEX-AR is designed for this target: it attributes each answer token
back to the input image and averages those attributions across the answer
span. Figure~\ref{fig:dexar-architecture} summarizes the pipeline before the
qualitative TextVQA example, making the later heatmaps easier to interpret as
answer-grounding evidence, not generic saliency maps.

\textbf{The gain is not merely an artifact of comparison with lower-scoring baselines.}
On two benchmarks, \methodname{} removes about half of the original
teacher-student gap and even on DocVQA, where the teacher ceiling is highest,
it still closes almost 40\% of the gap.

\textbf{The distilled student can outperform individual teachers.}
On TextVQA, \methodname{} reaches 69.38 and exceeds Gemma-3-4B-it at 57.80 by
+11.58 points (+20.03\%). On ChartQA, it exceeds InternVL3-1B at 75.30 by
+4.90 points (+6.51\%) and Gemma-3-4B-it at 63.60 by +16.60 points (+26.10\%).
This shows that routed multi-teacher supervision can combine useful signals
that are not available from any single teacher alone.

\textbf{Reverse KL consistently underperforms forward KL.}
This pattern can be explained by the different student-logit gradients.
Let \(p_s=\mathrm{softmax}(z_s)\) be the student distribution and let \(p_t\)
be the fixed teacher distribution. For forward KL,
\[
\mathcal{L}_{FKL}
=
\mathrm{KL}(p_t\|p_s)
=
\sum_v p_t(v)\log\frac{p_t(v)}{p_s(v)},
\qquad
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_{FKL}}{\partial z_s(v)}
=
p_s(v)-p_t(v).
\]
Thus, every teacher-supported token directly pulls the student distribution
toward the teacher's soft target
\cite{hinton2015distillingknowledgeneuralnetwork}. Reverse KL gives
\[
\mathcal{L}_{RKL}
=
\mathrm{KL}(p_s\|p_t)
=
\sum_v p_s(v)\log\frac{p_s(v)}{p_t(v)}.
\]
The student-logit gradient is
\[
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_{RKL}}{\partial z_s(v)}
=
p_s(v)
\left(
\log\frac{p_s(v)}{p_t(v)}
-
\mathcal{L}_{RKL}
\right).
\]
The main difference is the factor \(p_s(v)\). If \(p_t(v)>0\) but
\(p_s(v)\rightarrow 0\), then
\[
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_{FKL}}{\partial z_s(v)}
\rightarrow
-p_t(v),
\qquad
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_{RKL}}{\partial z_s(v)}
\rightarrow
0.
\]
Teacher-supported tokens that the student currently assigns low probability
therefore receive lower-magnitude correction under reverse KL. In finite training, this
student-weighted gradient can make reverse KL less useful for answer-token
distillation, especially when teacher distributions contain useful secondary
mass over OCR variants, numeric formats or plausible answer alternatives. We
interpret this as an optimization-bias explanation, not as a universal rule,
because recent LLM distillation work shows that the mode-seeking
interpretation of reverse KL alone does not fully describe KD behavior
\cite{wu2025rethinking}.

\textbf{SHM-CKA alone is not sufficient.}
Representation similarity is not the same as task-useful output supervision.
CKA measures hidden-state geometry \cite{kornblith2019similarityrepresentations}, but
hidden-state similarity does not imply output equivalence:
\[
\mathrm{CKA}(H_s,H_t)\approx 1
\quad\not\Rightarrow\quad
p_s(\cdot\mid x)\approx p_t(\cdot\mid x).
\]
A linear-head example makes this precise. Suppose the teacher representation is
an orthogonal rotation of the student representation,
\(H_t=H_sR\) with \(R^\top R=I\). Linear CKA gives
\[
\mathrm{CKA}(H_s,H_t)
=
\frac{\|H_t^\top H_s\|_F^2}
{\|H_t^\top H_t\|_F\|H_s^\top H_s\|_F}
=
1.
\]
However, the answer logits can still differ:
\[
z_s = H_sW_s,
\qquad
z_t = H_tW_t = H_sRW_t.
\]
Unless \(W_s=RW_t\), the two models can have identical CKA but different
logits and different answer-token distributions.
This distinction matches the difference between representational similarity
and functional similarity discussed in prior work
\cite{klabunde2023similarity}. In heterogeneous VLM distillation, the final
answer distribution also depends on the language head, tokenizer, output
vocabulary and task-specific teacher behavior. Although SHM-CKA is competitive
on ChartQA, it scores below \methodname{} on TextVQA and DocVQA, where
output-level tokenizer-agnostic matching and sample-dependent teacher weighting
are more important.

The SHM-CKA results motivate a closer look at representation similarity. We use
Centered Kernel Alignment (CKA) as an analysis tool to examine whether teacher and student layers
exhibit clear correspondence. For centered hidden-state matrices
\(\tilde{\mathbf{H}}_s\) and \(\tilde{\mathbf{H}}_t\), linear CKA is
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{CKA}(\tilde{\mathbf{H}}_s,\tilde{\mathbf{H}}_t)=
\frac{\left\|\tilde{\mathbf{H}}_t^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{H}}_s\right\|_F^2}
{\left\|\tilde{\mathbf{H}}_t^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{H}}_t\right\|_F
\left\|\tilde{\mathbf{H}}_s^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{H}}_s\right\|_F}.
\end{equation}
Values closer to \(1\) indicate higher representation similarity
\cite{kornblith2019similarityrepresentations}.
CKA is therefore used here as a representation-analysis tool, not as evidence
that two models implement the same answer function. A high CKA score indicates
similar hidden geometry on the evaluated samples, but it does not guarantee
similar answer-token probabilities after the language head.

High internal similarity is not sufficient to justify layer-wise
distillation, as the figures and tables below confirm.

vision stack. Both datasets contain broad high-similarity regions and the
DocVQA vision map has higher CKA values across the middle layers. Teacher and
student can therefore remain relatively close in both language and vision
representations. However, the main benchmark results in
Table~\ref{tab:main-comparison} still contain a clear gap between the student
models and higher-scoring teachers. The layer-wise baseline also fails to
consistently outperform output-level distillation. This pattern supports the
design choice in GRACE: output-level tokenizer-agnostic KD and sample-dependent
teacher weighting produce higher benchmark scores than representation matching
alone, because high hidden-state similarity does not guarantee that direct
layer matching will produce better task transfer.

\label{sec:ablation}

main components of \methodname{} on DocVQA. Section~\ref{sec:ablation-settings}

The variants form a cumulative sequence of increasingly specific controls.
The single-teacher ULD row tests whether cross-family token
distribution transfer is useful before any teacher mixture is introduced.
Uniform averaging then tests the value of teacher diversity without any
adaptive weighting. The routing-only variant adds sample-conditioned teacher
selection, while the full method adds the post-routing gradient check. The two
final rows isolate the refinement module itself by removing temporal smoothing
or by fixing the blend to rely only on routed weights over the active set. This
ordering separates the benefit of using more teachers from the benefit of
deciding which teacher signals should actually shape the update.

\textbf{Teacher diversity is necessary but not sufficient because uniform
multi-teacher averaging adds useful supervision while still failing to resolve
tokenizer mismatch, teacher specialization and conflicting KD directions.}
Across the ablation results in Table~\ref{tab:docvqa-planned-ablation}, the
full method is the highest-scoring setting for both student sizes. Moving from a
single cross-family teacher (ULD) to uniform four-teacher
averaging adds +3.46 on SmolVLM-256M and +4.31 on SmolVLM-500M. The gain
shows that multiple heterogeneous teachers provide higher-scoring supervision than
any single teacher, but uniform weighting leaves a further gap of +4.78/+3.52
to the full method.

\paragraph{Routing without gradient refinement.}
On SmolVLM-500M, routing-only scoring (63.23) is \textit{worse} than uniform
averaging (69.59) by $-6.36$ points. The router successfully identifies
candidate teachers, but without gradient refinement it assigns incorrect
relative weights and amplifies the contribution of conflicting teachers.
Without GRACE, these wrong weights degrade the result below a naive equal
average. On SmolVLM-256M the pattern reverses:
routing-only (57.45) beats uniform (55.70) by +1.75. SmolVLM-500M, the larger
student, produces a sharper routing distribution that amplifies weighting
errors when uncorrected. SmolVLM-256M produces a flatter routing signal closer
to uniform, so routing-only is less damaging and even slightly beneficial.
This capacity-dependent behavior shows that a larger student representation
does not automatically produce safer teacher weighting. A larger
student may give the router a more separable representation of the sample, but
the resulting gate can still be confidently wrong when teacher relevance and
objective compatibility diverge. The smaller student appears to produce a less
peaked routing signal, which behaves closer to a softened uniform mixture. This
supports the design choice of treating routing as a proposal step and
gradient-agreement refinement as a validation step.

\vspace{0.5em}

\textbf{Gradient-agreement refinement acts as the critical corrective layer
because it adjusts routed teacher weights according to whether each teacher's
KD direction agrees with the supervised task objective.}
Full GRACE recovers +9.88 points over routing-only on SmolVLM-500M and +3.03
on SmolVLM-256M. Decomposing the 500M gain, the active-set router-weight
variant (\(\lambda_{\mathrm{blend}}\!=\!1.0\)) already recovers +7.22 points
(63.23~\(\to\)~70.45) by replacing uniform post-routing weights with learned
router weights while retaining the GRACE active-set filter. Full score-softmax
blending then adds another +2.66 points (70.45~\(\to\)~73.11). On
SmolVLM-256M, the active-set router-weight variant adds only +0.52 points
(57.45~\(\to\)~57.97), while full score-based blending adds +2.51 points
(57.97~\(\to\)~60.48). These results show that active-set filtering and
router weighting help, but agreement-score weighting is still needed for the
highest final score.

Removing EMA smoothing ($\beta_{\mathrm{EMA}}\!=\!0$) degrades both students
(256M: 60.48~$\to$~57.45, 500M: 73.11~$\to$~71.25), confirming that temporal
smoothing stabilizes score estimates but is secondary to the refinement step
itself.
The fixed-blend and no-EMA variants separate two roles of the refinement
module. The fixed-blend variant keeps the active-set restriction but removes
the score-softmax influence, so it tests whether filtering alone is enough. Its
lower score shows that selecting an active teacher set is useful but
incomplete. The no-EMA variant keeps score weighting but removes temporal
smoothing, showing that batch-level gradient agreement is noisy and benefits
from accumulated teacher-level history. Together, these two controls indicate
that GRACE needs both a teacher-compatibility signal and a stable way to use
that signal across training updates.

\Needspace{13\baselineskip}
\begin{rqanswerbox}{Answer to RQ2}
The ablation isolates three cumulative contributions: (1)~heterogeneous
teacher diversity (+3 to +4 pts over single-teacher ULD), (2)~sparse routing
structure (beneficial only when paired with refinement) and
(3)~gradient-agreement refinement, which adds +3 to +10~pts over
routing-only and is the single largest contributor. EMA smoothing provides
additional stabilization by reducing the effect of noisy batch-level
gradient-agreement scores before they are converted into final KD weights.
Without gradient refinement, routing can degrade performance below uniform
averaging.
\end{rqanswerbox}

\section{Efficiency Analysis}
\label{sec:efficiency-analysis}
This section addresses RQ3 from Chapter~\ref{chap:introduction}. Runtime,
training cost and storage overhead are analyzed using the H200 rental rate and
the observed Cloudflare R2 storage footprint.

\textbf{Component-level overhead.}
\methodname{} adds online overhead from three sources: sparse trie-Wasserstein
support construction, teacher routing and gradient-agreement scoring. The
trie-Wasserstein term is the dominant online algorithmic addition because it
must compare sparse student and teacher output supports at supervised answer
positions. Its cost depends on the retained top-\(K_{\mathrm{TW}}\) support,
the number of active trie edges and the number of routed teachers. The teacher
router is comparatively small because it is an MLP over pooled student hidden
states, so its cost is minor relative to the student forward and backward pass.
Gradient-agreement refinement is more expensive than ordinary fixed weighting
because it scores teacher-specific KD directions. This cost scales with the
number of teacher-specific KD losses being scored, while top-\(k\) routing keeps
the final KD aggregation sparse.


\textbf{Offline versus online cost.}
The implementation separates teacher inference from student training. Teacher
logits are generated offline and stored before distillation, so the online
training loop does not need to run the frozen teachers during every student
update. This reduces online GPU memory pressure and makes the reported
experiments feasible on a single high-memory GPU. The tradeoff is that cost
moves to the offline cache-generation stage and to storage and I/O. This design
is attractive when the same cached teacher logits are reused across multiple
ablations, since the expensive teacher forward passes are paid once. It becomes
less attractive when datasets, prompts or teacher pools change frequently,
because the corresponding cached logits must be regenerated.


\textbf{Scalability.}
The main scaling pressure comes from the product of dataset size, number of
teachers and cached answer-token logits. Adding teachers increases offline
generation time, remote storage and the number of candidate KD losses that may
need to be scored. Increasing \(K_{\mathrm{TW}}\) raises the online cost of
sparse trie construction, while increasing \(k_{\mathrm{route}}\) raises the
number of teacher losses used per sample. The current four-teacher setting is a
workable operating point in this tradeoff: it is large enough to expose teacher
specialization, but still small enough for sparse routing, gradient refinement
and remote-cache streaming to remain manageable.


\begin{rqanswerbox}{Answer to RQ3}
\methodname{} offloads teacher inference to a one-time offline stage,
making online GPU cost comparable to standard finetuning.
Per answer position, trie-Wasserstein matching costs
$O(K_{\mathrm{TW}}\bar{L})$ over retained token paths, routing adds
$O(d_h N)$ for the MLP gate over $N$ teachers, and gradient-agreement
scoring adds $O(N|\Theta|)$ for per-teacher cosine comparisons.
Top-$k$ routing bounds the active teacher count per sample at
$k_{\mathrm{route}} \ll N$, keeping aggregation sparse.
Offline logit generation is paid once and amortized across all
subsequent runs, offering a favorable cost-performance tradeoff
for iterative experimentation.
\end{rqanswerbox}

\section{Discussion}

Taken together, the efficiency results and benchmark tables show that across
all three benchmarks and both student sizes, the four method families
produce a consistent performance ordering. Same-family KL baselines obtain the
lowest scores, single cross-family distillation scores higher, uniform
multi-teacher weighting improves further and \methodname{} obtains the highest
scores overall
\cite{hinton2015distillingknowledgeneuralnetwork,boizard2024towardscrosstokenizerdistillation}.
No single component fully addresses the heterogeneous distillation problem:
heterogeneous teachers improve scores, but the student still needs a
tokenizer-agnostic loss, sample-dependent routing and a stable
post-routing weighting scheme in the evaluated setting. The training recipe
explains this pattern because \methodname{} combines a
shared-trie Wasserstein loss for mismatched tokenizers with routed
multi-teacher selection, top-\(k\) masking, routed-weight renormalization and
EMA-smoothed gradient-agreement refinement
\cite{boizard2024towardscrosstokenizerdistillation,peyre2019computationaloptimaltransport,fedus2022switchtransformersscalingtrillion}.
The smoother DocVQA training curve in Figure~\ref{fig:training-curve-comparison}
is consistent with that design: the router narrows the teacher set first, then
GRACE reweights only the routed teachers, reducing the score oscillations caused by
uniformly mixing heterogeneous teacher signals.
\paragraph{First-order interpretation of gradient-agreement refinement.}
The gradient-agreement component can also be understood from a first-order
multi-objective view. Let
\(g_{CE}=\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_{CE}\) and
\(g_i=\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_{KD}^{(i)}\) be the supervised and
teacher-specific KD gradients. A combined update with teacher weights \(w_i\)
is
\[
\Delta\theta
=
-\eta
\left(
g_{CE}
+
\alpha_{KD}\sum_i w_i g_i
\right).
\]
The first-order change in the supervised loss is
\[
\Delta\mathcal{L}_{CE}
\approx
g_{CE}^{\top}\Delta\theta
=
-\eta\|g_{CE}\|_2^2
-
\eta\alpha_{KD}\sum_i w_i\langle g_{CE},g_i\rangle.
\]
If \(\langle g_{CE},g_i\rangle<0\), then teacher \(i\) contributes a term that
increases the first-order CE loss when the supervised objective should decrease. This is the
gradient-conflict case studied in multi-task optimization
\cite{yu2020gradient}. GRACE handles this conflict through teacher weighting
with cosine agreement:
\[
\cos(g_{CE},g_i)
=
\frac{\langle g_{CE},g_i\rangle}
{\|g_{CE}\|_2\|g_i\|_2}.
\]
Teachers with \(\cos(g_{CE},g_i)<0\) are suppressed before they distort
the supervised update, which explains why gradient refinement improves over
routing alone. The largest gain appears on ChartQA and the trend holds for
SmolVLM-256M as well. Sample-dependent teacher
assignment has the largest observed benefit when the task mixes visual parsing
and numerical reasoning. The method also benefits both evaluated student
models. The comparison with SHM-CKA reinforces this conclusion by showing that
heterogeneous teacher access is not enough when the method relies only on
hidden-state matching with no routed output-level KD. SHM-CKA is
competitive on ChartQA yet scores lower than \methodname{} on TextVQA and
DocVQA. Together with
the CKA heatmaps, this confirms that internal similarity alone does not
guarantee effective transfer. \textbf{Sample-dependent teacher weighting
therefore remains necessary, particularly when the teacher pool contains
models with different tokenizers, architectures and task-specific strengths}
\cite{kornblith2019similarityrepresentations,dasgupta2025improving}. The
remaining limitations and future directions are discussed in the concluding
chapter.
