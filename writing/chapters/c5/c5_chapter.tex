\methodname{} is the top method for both student sizes across all three benchmarks. Fine-tuning barely improves over the original student scores, and same-family
logit-based KD depends strongly on the divergence:
forward KL improves slightly, while reverse KL and Jensen-Shannon often reduce

Cross-family ULD outperforms same-family KD, and uniform multi-teacher

\section{Efficiency Analysis}
\label{sec:efficiency-analysis}
This section answers RQ3 by summarizing the training overhead introduced by
\methodname{}. The main online costs come from three components:
trie-Wasserstein support construction, teacher routing and gradient-agreement
scoring. Trie-Wasserstein matching builds sparse token supports and accumulates
trie-edge masses at answer positions. The router is an MLP over pooled student states; its cost scales with the
hidden size and number of teachers. Gradient-agreement
scoring introduces overhead from comparing teacher-specific KD gradients,
bounded by top-\(k\) routing.

Teacher inference is offline: cached logits remove the cost of running frozen
teachers during training, shifting that computation to a one-time caching step. The main
scaling factors are the number of teachers, the retained trie support size
\(K_{\mathrm{TW}}\) and the number of routed teachers \(k_{\mathrm{route}}\).
An average training run on the reported configurations costs approximately
\textbf{14.41 USD}, excluding one-time offline teacher logit caching and remote
storage. This figure covers the student forward and backward passes, sparse
routing computation and gradient-agreement scoring for the full training run.

\begin{rqanswerbox}{Answer to RQ3}
\methodname{} adds overhead from trie-Wasserstein matching, routing and
gradient-agreement scoring, while offline caching replaces repeated teacher forward passes with logit
loading from cache. Per answer position,
trie-Wasserstein matching scales with \(K_{\mathrm{TW}}\), routing scales with
the number of teachers \(N\), and top-\(k\) routing keeps the active teacher
count bounded by \(k_{\mathrm{route}}\). Thus, the method trades per-step
matching and scoring cost for distillation with offline pre-cached teacher logits
and sparse teacher selection at each step.
\end{rqanswerbox}

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The results show that heterogeneous distillation requires more than adding teachers
to the loss: the gains come from combining cross-tokenizer output matching,
sample-dependent routing and gradient reweighting after routing
\cite{hinton2015distillingknowledgeneuralnetwork,boizard2024towardscrosstokenizerdistillation,peyre2019computationaloptimaltransport,fedus2022switchtransformersscalingtrillion}.
Figure~\ref{fig:training-curve-comparison} shows fewer oscillations for
\methodname{} than Uniform on DocVQA, consistent with restricting reweighting to the routed teacher subset.

\textbf{Behavior of same-family logit-based KD.}
The same-family logit-based baselines show different behavior across distributional
losses for distillation on answer tokens. Forward KL gives small but consistent
gains, while Reverse KL performs substantially worse in this setting.
A likely reason is that Reverse KL is weighted by the current student
distribution, so tokens the teacher assigns high probability to but that the student currently assigns
low probability receive weaker correction. This is undesirable for OCR-heavy
and numerically sensitive VQA tasks, where teacher probability mass may appear on
low-probability answer variants. Reverse KL is, in short, the wrong objective
when the student must recover teacher probability mass on tokens it currently
neglects. This explanation is limited to the reported
KD experiments on answer tokens and should not be read as a general claim about
Reverse KL in all distillation settings
\cite{wu2025rethinking}.

\textbf{Cross-family ULD.}
ULD consistently outperforms all same-family KD variants despite using only one
teacher. The likely reason is that sorted-rank matching avoids the
shared-vocabulary assumption entirely: comparing sorted probability vectors
penalizes distributional shape differences without requiring that corresponding
indices refer to the same token. ULD therefore sets the cross-family baseline that Uniform and \methodname{} must exceed.
However, rank-based comparison discards the semantic relationship between tokens:
two tokens with adjacent ranks but unrelated decoded strings are treated as
equally close. Byte-Trie Wasserstein replaces rank proximity with byte-path
proximity, giving lower transport cost to tokens with similar decoded content.
This is why \methodname{} improves over ULD even with the same Qwen2-VL teacher
in the pool.

ULD measures the L1 gap between two rank-sorted probability vectors: it detects whether teacher and student distribute
probability similarly across rank positions, but rank-\(k\) in one vocabulary
need not decode to a similar string as rank-\(k\) in the other. Unlike ULD, our loss measures transport through the shared trie, so
probability mass on \texttt{<wb>invoice} in the teacher and mass on
\texttt{in} in the student are connected by a short trie path and penalized
in proportion to their byte distance, not their rank gap. For OCR and document tasks where
teacher and student split answer strings at different subword boundaries, this
distinction is direct: ULD cannot credit partial byte-level agreement, whereas
Byte-Trie Wasserstein does. Table~\ref{tab:uld-vs-tw} makes this concrete.

representation alignment and output calibration are largely orthogonal
objectives in heterogeneous VLM distillation.

\textbf{First-order interpretation of gradient-agreement refinement.}
The first-order analysis in Section~\ref{sec:gradient-agreement} shows that a
teacher whose KD gradient conflicts with the CE gradient partially
offsets the supervised update. Suppressing such teachers explains the
ablation finding that gradient-agreement refinement outperforms routing alone,
consistent with treating routing as candidate selection and gradient agreement
as corrective reweighting \cite{yu2020gradient}.

\textbf{Visual grounding.}
To inspect whether distillation changes which image regions the student attends
to, we use \textbf{DEX-AR} attribution maps \cite{bousselham2026dexar}, which aggregate
token-level visual attributions into a sequence-level heatmap for autoregressive
VLMs. Figure~\ref{fig:textvqa-sample3-qualitative} shows a representative
TextVQA example \cite{singh2019vqamodelsread} where the question asks
\emph{``who is this copyrighted by?''} and
the relevant evidence is the copyright text on the lower-left border. The
original SmolVLM-500M concentrates on the \emph{Virgin} logo; \methodname{}
redirects attention to the copyright text, correctly locating the region needed
to answer the question. The shift follows from the output-distribution supervision: pushing the student
toward teacher probability mass on the correct answer tokens causes the model
to attend to the image regions that support those tokens.
