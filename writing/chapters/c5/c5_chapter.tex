Step 0 is the hardest decision point for all four models: entropy is high
and greedy probability is at its lowest, because the model must commit to
an answer format before any context has been generated. The step-0 values
are already informative: original and ULD start at 2.0 and 1.8, fine-tuning
at 1.6 and \methodname{} at 1.35. For the first three, step 0 is the entropy
peak across the whole sequence. Fine-tuning is the exception, where the
worst uncertainty arrives much later.

Beyond step 0, the models diverge sharply. The original SmolVLM-500M
(Figure~\ref{fig:dist-original}) oscillates throughout, with entropy
between 0.6 and 1.35 and greedy probability between 0.65 and 0.88 with
no clear trend, reflecting poor calibration for DocVQA. The apparent
improvement at steps 17+ is a statistical effect: fewer samples are still
generating at those steps and the remaining ones are producing predictable
terminal sequences.

Fine-tuning (Figure~\ref{fig:dist-finetune}) is stable in the middle
(greedy 0.65 to 0.80 for steps 1 to 15) but collapses from step 16 onward,
with entropy peaking at 3.4 and greedy falling to 0.28 at step 20. Top-20
mass stays near 1.0 throughout, so the model is not completely confused:
it knows the next token is among the top candidates but cannot choose
confidently between them. One-hot supervision provides no gradient signal
about near-correct alternatives, leaving the model without a basis for
deciding at answer boundaries. \textbf{This failure mode is invisible in accuracy
numbers alone.}

ULD (Figure~\ref{fig:dist-uld}) eliminates the instability. Greedy
probability stabilises at 0.82 to 0.93 and entropy declines steadily from
1.8 to 0.2 to 0.3, the expected pattern as generated context progressively
narrows the next-token distribution. ULD also generates longer answers than
the other models (step 31 vs.\ step 22). Since entropy stays low throughout,
this reflects more complete answer generation rather than uncertainty.

\methodname{} (Figure~\ref{fig:dist-grace}) produces the sharpest
distributions of all four models. After step 1, greedy probability locks
at 0.92 to 0.97 and entropy stays at 0.1 to 0.3 for almost all steps. The
gap between greedy and top-5 mass is negligible (both near 0.95 to 0.98),
meaning \textbf{virtually all probability concentrates on a single token} at each
step. This is distinctly sharper than ULD, where the top-5 exceeds greedy
by roughly 0.08. The sharpness reflects the tokenizer-aware loss, where
byte-path proximity provides finer-grained semantic guidance than rank
matching, as well as the multi-teacher pool. The comparison between Uniform
and \methodname{} in Table~\ref{tab:main-comparison} isolates routing and
gradient-agreement filtering. The comparison with ULD isolates the loss
design.

Together, the four panels show that soft supervision qualitatively reshapes
generation confidence beyond what accuracy scores capture. Fine-tuning
restores content prediction but introduces late-sequence boundary uncertainty
that one-hot labels cannot resolve. ULD restores calibration and produces
longer, more complete answers. \methodname{} goes further, producing the
sharpest per-step distributions of all four models, consistent with the
quantitative gains in Table~\ref{tab:main-comparison}.

\section{Ablation Study}
\label{sec:ablation}

The ablation builds up the full \methodname{} pipeline one component at a
time on DocVQA, keeping all other settings fixed (variants defined in
Section~\ref{sec:ablation-settings}). The sequence is designed to separate
three distinct questions: how much does teacher diversity help over a single
teacher, what does sparse routing add on top of diversity and what does
gradient-agreement refinement add on top of routing. Two further variants
then isolate the contribution of EMA smoothing and score-based blending
within the agreement step.

\textbf{Teacher diversity.}
Moving from single-teacher ULD to Uniform averaging over the full
heterogeneous pool improves DocVQA by +3.46 points for SmolVLM-256M and
+4.31 points for SmolVLM-500M. The gain comes purely from exposing the
student to a wider range of teacher signals, with no adaptive weighting.

\textbf{Routing without gradient agreement.}
Adding sparse top-\(k\) routing on top of Uniform produces inconsistent
results. SmolVLM-256M improves by +1.75 points, but SmolVLM-500M
\emph{drops} by 6.36 points, falling below even the no-routing Uniform
baseline. This is the most informative result in the ablation: a router that
selects teachers by relevance alone, without checking whether their KD
gradients align with the supervised CE objective, \textbf{can actively hurt training}.
The asymmetry between student sizes is itself informative. A smaller student
has limited capacity to absorb conflicting gradient signals, so routing errors
produce smaller absolute deviations before the CE loss pulls the parameters
back toward the correct region. A larger student can move further in the
wrong direction before self-correcting, which means a misaligned routing
decision compounds across steps rather than being smoothed out by parameter
noise. This explains why gradient-agreement refinement is especially
critical for the 500M student.

\textbf{Gradient-agreement refinement.}
Adding gradient-agreement refinement recovers and surpasses both previous
variants. Full \methodname{} improves over Routing only by +3.03 points for
SmolVLM-256M and +9.88 points for SmolVLM-500M. The recovery for the 500M
student is particularly large, which is consistent with the earlier finding
that the 500M student was most harmed by unfiltered routing.

\textbf{EMA smoothing and score blending.}
Removing EMA smoothing (\(\beta_{\mathrm{EMA}}=0\)) lowers DocVQA from 73.11
to 71.25 for SmolVLM-500M and from 60.48 to 57.45 for SmolVLM-256M, showing
that \textbf{per-batch agreement scores are too noisy to use directly} without
temporal smoothing. Setting \(\lambda_{\mathrm{blend}}=1.0\), which removes
the agreement-score softmax from the high-confidence case while keeping the
threshold and fallback, also decreases both scores, confirming that
score-weighted blending contributes independently of the other mechanisms.

\Needspace{13\baselineskip}
\begin{rqanswerbox}{Answer to RQ2}
Teacher diversity alone accounts for a consistent gain of roughly 3 to 4
points over single-teacher ULD. Sparse routing without gradient-agreement
refinement is unreliable: it helps the smaller student but hurts the larger
one, demonstrating that \textit{relevance-based selection} is insufficient on its own.
Gradient-agreement refinement is the \textbf{largest single contributor}, recovering
and extending the gains lost to unfiltered routing. EMA smoothing and
score-based blending each provide additional, independently verifiable
improvements.
\end{rqanswerbox}


\section{Efficiency Analysis}
\label{sec:efficiency-analysis}
The main online costs come from trie-Wasserstein matching, teacher routing and
gradient-agreement scoring. Trie-Wasserstein matching builds sparse token
supports and accumulates edge masses at answer positions. Teacher routing cost
scales with the hidden size and number of teachers. Gradient-agreement scoring
adds per-teacher gradient comparisons limited to the top-\(k\) routed teachers.

Teacher inference is \textbf{offline}: logits are cached once before training, so each
update only loads them from disk rather than running four teacher forward
passes. This is the dominant cost saving: without caching, each training
step would require forwarding all four teachers on the same batch, which
would multiply the per-step memory and compute by the number of teachers.
With caching, that cost is paid once before training begins and amortised
across all steps. The main online scaling factors are the number of teachers,
the retained trie support size \(K_{\mathrm{TW}}\) and the number of routed
teachers \(k_{\mathrm{route}}\). Top-\(k\) routing bounds the active teacher
set to \(k_{\mathrm{route}}\) per step and prevents the online cost from
growing linearly with pool size. An average training run on the reported
configurations costs approximately \textbf{14.41 USD}, excluding one-time
offline teacher logit caching and remote storage. The 14.41 USD figure covers
only the student training itself, including all routing and
agreement-scoring overhead, which is low enough to be completed on a single
rented GPU in a few hours and places heterogeneous multi-teacher distillation
within reach of researchers without dedicated compute infrastructure.

\begin{rqanswerbox}{Answer to RQ3}
\methodname{} adds overhead from trie-Wasserstein matching, routing and
gradient-agreement scoring, while offline caching replaces repeated teacher forward passes with logit
loading from cache. Per answer position,
trie-Wasserstein matching scales with \(K_{\mathrm{TW}}\), routing scales with
the number of teachers \(N\) and top-\(k\) routing keeps the active teacher
count bounded by \(k_{\mathrm{route}}\). The dominant cost is the per-step trie matching and agreement scoring, while teacher
inference is paid once at caching.
\end{rqanswerbox}

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Heterogeneous distillation requires more than adding teachers to the loss:
the gains come from combining cross-tokenizer output matching, sample-dependent
routing and gradient reweighting after routing
\cite{hinton2015distillingknowledgeneuralnetwork,boizard2024towardscrosstokenizerdistillation,peyre2019computationaloptimaltransport,fedus2022switchtransformersscalingtrillion}.
This is already visible in the training dynamics: Figure~\ref{fig:training-curve-comparison}
shows fewer loss oscillations for \methodname{} than Uniform on DocVQA,
consistent with fewer teachers receiving weight per step and conflicting
gradients being filtered out before they affect the update.

\textbf{Behavior of same-family logit-based KD.}
Same-family logit-based KD is sensitive to the divergence choice. Forward KL
gives small but consistent gains, while Reverse KL performs substantially worse
in this setting. Reverse KL weights its gradient by the current student distribution, so
tokens the teacher scores highly but the student neglects receive weaker
correction. The effect is worst on OCR-heavy and numerically sensitive tasks, where the
correct answer token is often low-probability under the student's current
distribution. \textbf{Reverse KL is therefore poorly suited to this setting}, where the student must
recover teacher probability mass on tokens it currently neglects, though the
claim is specific to short answer tokens and Reverse KL can behave differently
in other distillation regimes \cite{wu2025rethinking}.

\textbf{Cross-family ULD.}
This method consistently outperforms all same-family KD variants despite using only one
teacher. \textbf{Sorted-rank matching} avoids the shared-vocabulary assumption entirely:
comparing sorted probability vectors penalizes distributional shape differences
without requiring that corresponding indices refer to the same token. ULD
therefore sets the cross-family baseline that Uniform and \methodname{} must exceed.
However, rank-based comparison discards the semantic relationship between
tokens: two tokens with adjacent ranks but unrelated decoded strings receive
the same low transport cost as two adjacent tokens whose strings are nearly
identical. Byte-Trie Wasserstein replaces rank proximity with byte-path
proximity, giving lower transport cost to tokens with similar decoded content.
This is why \methodname{} improves over ULD even with the same Qwen2-VL teacher
in the pool.

Table~\ref{tab:uld-vs-tw} illustrates this across four token pairs spanning
the full range from exact match to no shared prefix.

All four rows carry the same rank-space probability difference, so ULD assigns
an identical loss of 0.20 in every case and cannot distinguish any of them.
Byte-Trie Wasserstein, by contrast, reflects the degree of byte-path overlap:
an exact decoded match (\texttt{doc}/\texttt{doc}) has zero cost, a
near-morphological variant (\texttt{invoice}/\texttt{invoices}) sharing a
7-byte prefix pays only approximately 0.05, a shorter prefix overlap
(\texttt{doc}/\texttt{document}, 3 shared bytes) costs 0.60 and a completely
unrelated pair (\texttt{abc}/\texttt{def}, no shared bytes) pays the full
3.29. The progression tracks intuitive semantic distance in a way that rank
position cannot, which is why \methodname{} improves over ULD even when both
distill from the same teacher.

\textbf{Representation matching and SHM-CKA.}
SHM-CKA tests whether hidden-state alignment alone is sufficient for
cross-family distillation by training the student to match Qwen2-VL layer
representations via CKA similarity, bypassing the vocabulary problem
entirely. It is competitive on ChartQA for SmolVLM-500M, suggesting that
representational alignment provides a useful signal for visual-reasoning
tasks where the answer distributions are more structured. However, it trails
\methodname{} overall, indicating that matching internal representations does
not reliably translate into matching output distributions on answer tokens
\cite{kornblith2019similarityrepresentations,klabunde2023similarity}.

\textbf{First-order interpretation of gradient-agreement refinement.}
A teacher whose KD gradient conflicts with the CE gradient partially offsets
the supervised update (first-order analysis in
Section~\ref{sec:gradient-agreement}). Suppressing such teachers explains why
gradient-agreement refinement outperforms routing alone, where routing acts as
candidate selection and gradient agreement provides corrective reweighting
\cite{yu2020gradient}.

\textbf{Visual grounding.}
This asks whether a VLM attends to the image region that contains
the evidence for its answer, rather than relying on a nearby logo, decorative
text or irrelevant object. We use \textbf{DEX-AR} attribution maps as a
qualitative diagnostic \cite{bousselham2026dexar}. DEX-AR is designed for
autoregressive VLMs: it combines attention maps with gradient sensitivity and
weights each generated token by how visually grounded its prediction is,
suppressing function words and subword continuations that attend mainly to
linguistic context. The result is a single heatmap over image regions that
reflects where the model looked when producing the answer. In our experiments
the heatmap is used only as a diagnostic, not as a training signal.
